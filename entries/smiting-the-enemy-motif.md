## Overview

Ancient kings and gods were often represented striking, trampling, or standing over defeated foes. Egyptian art uses the image from the early dynasties onward, and related poses occur in Levantine and Assyrian material. Biblical texts use this vocabulary to describe royal victory, divine judgment, and the defeat of enemies. The motifs show how ancient writers and artists made supremacy visible. Artistic conventions also help readers distinguish an image of total domination from a record of what a ruler actually did to individual captives.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

On the [[narmer-palette|Narmer Palette]], the Egyptian king raises a mace over a defeated foe. Repetitions of the general pose over later periods made it a conventional expression of kingship. Levantine figurines and the [[baal-with-thunderbolt|stele of Baal with a thunderbolt]] show deities with an arm raised to strike. Similar poses can express divine protection, victory, or a ruler acting under a god's authority [@anep, figs. 292, 296–297] [@keel-symbolism, 290–293].

The use of an enemy below the ruler's feet is another form of the theme. Egyptian objects, including the [[tutankhamun-footstool|footstool of Tutankhamun]], place figures of enemies under the king. Assyrian royal inscriptions and reliefs likewise represent the subjection of enemies. These works repeatedly invoke a hierarchy in which the victorious ruler stands above those subjected to him [@rima3, A.0.102.14, A.0.102.88].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ps 110:1, 5–6 combines the image of a [[footstool]] with the shattering of kings and judgment of nations. Ps 18:37–42 (MT 18:38–43) describes enemies falling beneath the royal speaker's feet. 1 Kgs 5:3 (MT 5:17) uses similar language for David's victories. In Josh 10:24 commanders place their feet on captured kings' necks. The ceremony gives a concrete narrative expression to the relationship shown in the images.

Ps 2:9 depicts the nations being broken like a potter's vessel. Some scholars compare Egyptian [[execration-texts|execration rites]], in which inscribed objects representing enemies could be destroyed. The comparison helps explain the force of a broken vessel as an image of defeat. The specific ritual background of the psalm remains debated.

Several texts use the motif for God. Ps 68:21 (MT 68:22) speaks of God striking his enemies' heads, while Hab 3:13 associates a similar blow with deliverance. Isa 63:1–6 depicts the [[divine-warrior|divine warrior]] treading his foes like grapes. Mal 4:3 (MT 3:21) compares the defeated wicked to ashes beneath the feet of the righteous. In 1 Cor 15:25 the placing of enemies under Christ's feet recalls Ps 110 and serves a different account of victory.

## Interpretive Significance

The pose and the language are conventional expressions of authority. They can accompany a particular historical claim, a ceremony, or an account of divine action. The artistic and poetic evidence establishes the range of the motif without settling the details of historical practices, which require their own sources.
