## Overview

The Kurkh Monoliths are two Assyrian standing stone monuments from the ninth century BCE, found at Kurkh (modern Üçtepe) in southeastern Turkey. One records a campaign of Ashurnasirpal II; the other preserves the earliest surviving account of the battle of Qarqar in 853 BCE, fought by Shalmaneser III against an alliance of western rulers. The account names Ahab of Israel among the allies. It gives an independent point of reference for the reign of the king described in 1 Kings and records an episode that the biblical narrative does not mention.

## Description and Provenance

Both monuments are rounded limestone slabs carved with a standing king and symbols of the Assyrian gods. The slab of Ashurnasirpal is about 1.93 meters high, and that of Shalmaneser about 2.21 meters. Their inscriptions are in Akkadian, the language used for Assyrian royal inscriptions, written in cuneiform, a script composed of wedge-shaped signs. The British consul John Taylor found both stones near Diyarbakır in 1861, and both are now in the British Museum in London. Taylor's record establishes the findspot, but the ancient name of the site is debated. <!-- VERIFY: ancient identification of Kurkh; do not equate the site with a named Assyrian town without further evidence -->

## Contents

Ashurnasirpal's monument records a campaign in the upper Tigris region in 879 BCE [@rima2]. The monument of his son [[shalmaneser-iii|Shalmaneser III]], usually dated to c. 852 BCE, recounts early campaigns and ends with the [[battle-of-qarqar|battle of Qarqar]]. <!-- VERIFY: basis for dating the engraving to c. 852 BCE rather than to 853 BCE --> Adad-idri (Hadadezer) of Damascus and Irhuleni of [[hamath|Hamath]] head the list of allied forces and are usually understood as the coalition's leaders. In the second column, lines 91–92, the list credits [[ahab|Ahab]] with “2,000 chariots, 10,000 troops” (own rendering), the largest chariot contingent reported there [@rima3, A.0.102.2].

The same list assigns 1,000 camels to Gindibu the Arab in line 94, the earliest securely dated Assyrian reference to the people called [[qedarites-and-arabs|Arabs]]. The king goes on to claim a victory and the capture of enemy chariots, cavalry, and horses. The later [[black-obelisk|Black Obelisk]] records further fighting against Damascus and Hamath; the victory claim should not be taken to mean that the alliance ceased to resist Assyria after Qarqar.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Most scholars identify the Ahab named at Qarqar with the son of Omri who reigns in Samaria in 1 Kgs 16:29–33. The Assyrian account identifies him by his connection to Israel, not by naming his father or describing his religious policies. Its concern is the force he contributed, not the evaluation of his reign that shapes the account in Kings. The date of the battle places Ahab on the same timeline as Shalmaneser, without settling the dates of his accession or death.

In 1 Kgs 20:31–34, Ahab makes a covenant with [[ben-hadad|Ben-hadad]] of Damascus, and 1 Kgs 22:1 reports three years without war between Aram and Israel. These notices make cooperation between the two kingdoms intelligible, even though they do not mention Assyria or Qarqar. It is debated whether Shalmaneser's Hadadezer is the Ben-hadad of that account. <!-- VERIFY: current assessment of the proposed identification of Hadadezer with the Ben-hadad of 1 Kgs 20:31–34 --> The inscription does not establish that the battle fell within the biblical three-year interval or that the covenant was made to oppose Assyria.

## Scholarly Issues

The recorded figure of 2,000 [[chariots-and-horses|chariots]] is not a verified count. Some scholars accept it as evidence of Israel's military strength; others suspect a mistake in the writing of the number and have proposed 200 instead. <!-- VERIFY: support for accepting 2,000 as a historical total and for the proposed correction to 200 --> The engraving contains errors, including a repeated expression in the account of crossing the Euphrates (second column, line 82). That shows why the figures deserve care, but it does not establish that this particular number was written incorrectly.
