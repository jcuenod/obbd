## Overview

Egyptian harpers’ songs survive as inscriptions in tombs and as a composition in a New Kingdom papyrus. Some celebrate the enduring life of the deceased. Others, especially a song attributed to the tomb of King Intef, observe that the dead provide no report of their condition and urge enjoyment while life lasts. These songs give readers of Ecclesiastes a comparison for advice about food, fragrance, clothing, and pleasure in the face of death. Their variety shows that such advice could carry different meanings even within Egyptian funerary culture.

## Manuscripts and Date

The song attributed to King Intef survives on the recto of Papyrus Harris 500 (British Museum EA 10060), a New Kingdom manuscript. An inscription from the tomb of Paatenemheb at Saqqara, now in Leiden, preserves another version of part of the song in association with musicians. The papyrus title locates the song in the tomb of a king named Intef. Several Egyptian kings bore that name, and the title alone cannot establish the original date or place of composition.

Other harpers’ songs appear beside images of singers or musicians in tombs, addressing the deceased or a broader audience. Some reference banqueting customs and speak to the living in the presence of a memorial for the dead. The physical setting is clear in inscribed examples. Whether a particular song originated as an actual performance at a feast, as a funerary composition, or as a text adapted between those settings remains open.

## Contents

The Intef song recalls that earlier generations have died and that even their monuments disappear. It asks what can be known from the dead and turns to the opportunities of the present. Fine clothes, scented oil, celebration, and freedom from anxiety belong to the advice it gives. Another New Kingdom example, in the tomb of Neferhotep at Thebes, takes issue with songs that disparage the realm of the dead. It describes that realm as the destination of all and the haven of the just. The texts share the setting of [[care-for-the-dead|care for the dead]] while making room for different voices about what follows death.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Eccl 9:7–10 advises eating bread, drinking wine, wearing clean garments, using oil, and enjoying life with one’s spouse. It grounds the urgency in the absence of activity and knowledge in [[sheol-and-the-netherworld|Sheol]]. Eccl 2:24 connects enjoyment of food, drink, and toil with God’s provision. Eccl 3:19–22 considers the shared mortality of humans and animals and asks what can be known of what follows, before affirming the enjoyment of one’s work. The combination of sensory detail with reflection on mortality makes the Intef song especially helpful for comparison. Ecclesiastes places enjoyment within a sustained account of God, work, and the limits of human knowledge.

Job 7:9–10 uses the absence of return from Sheol to express loss. In Isa 22:13, Jerusalem’s eating and drinking in the face of death draws condemnation because the occasion called for mourning. An Old Babylonian episode of the [[gilgamesh-epic|Gilgamesh epic]] preserved on the so-called Meissner tablet offers further comparison. A tavern keeper urges Gilgamesh to enjoy ordinary life while he searches for a way past human mortality. These settings give similar observations and advice differing force.

## Scholarly Issues

The shared list of pleasures has led some readers to propose literary influence on Ecclesiastes, but there is no known line of textual transmission between the surviving Egyptian witnesses and the Hebrew book. The broader circulation of comparable advice argues for examining each composition’s rhetorical setting as well as its imagery. A tomb song can acknowledge the limits of the living person’s knowledge while continuing to serve the commemoration of the dead.
