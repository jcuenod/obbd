## Overview

Thebes was a major Egyptian center of kingship and worship in the area of modern Luxor. Its temples on the east bank of the Nile and tombs and memorial temples on the west preserve records spanning many centuries. The temple of Amun at Karnak was particularly important. The Assyrian king Ashurbanipal reports that his army plundered the city in the seventh century BCE. The prophet Nahum invokes the fate of Thebes to challenge the Assyrian capital of Nineveh, while Jeremiah and Ezekiel also name the city in oracles against Egypt.

## Location and Identification

Thebes lay in Upper Egypt. The large complexes of Karnak and Luxor occupied the east bank, while a series of temples and cemeteries stood on the west. Egyptians knew the city as Waset. The biblical name No-Amon is commonly understood in relation to the Egyptian expression *niwt Imn*, “city of Amun.” Some older translations identified the city as Alexandria, which dates to a later age and lies in a different part of Egypt.

## Archaeological History

Thebes became a center of royal and religious activity especially during the New Kingdom. Rulers enlarged the precincts of Amun at Karnak and added temples and inscriptions on both banks. On the west bank, royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings stood within a wider landscape of cemeteries and memorial temples. The [[medinet-habu-sea-peoples|reliefs at Medinet Habu]] illustrate the use of a royal memorial temple to commemorate military achievement.

Other Theban monuments preserve details with connections to biblical studies. The [[bubastite-portal|Bubastite Portal]] at Karnak bears Shoshenq I's place-name list. The [[merneptah-stele|Merneptah Stele]] was found in the king's memorial temple on the west bank. The [[tomb-of-rekhmire|tomb of Rekhmire]] depicts work and deliveries from foreign regions. Each object belongs to a different period and shows how these temple and tomb complexes accumulated records over time.

The annals of [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]] describe an expedition against Tantamani, ruler of the Kushite dynasty, and the plundering of Thebes. The event is usually dated around 664–663 BCE. The Assyrian records emphasize the removal of wealth and people, consistent with the emphases of royal victory accounts.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Nah 3:8–10 asks whether Nineveh is stronger than No-Amon, a city whose waters and allies failed to save it. The text recalls people going into exile and describes violence against its inhabitants. If it refers to the seventh-century Assyrian sack, the memory supplies a lower limit for dating the oracle in its present form. The analogy turns an Assyrian victory into a warning to Assyria itself.

Jer 46:25 names Amun of No alongside Pharaoh and the gods and kings of Egypt. Ezek 30:14–16 includes No among cities facing judgment. These references treat the city as an emblem of Egyptian political and religious power. The prominence of Amun and of the city that honored him helps explain why the city's fate had wider significance.
