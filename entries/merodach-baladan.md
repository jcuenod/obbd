## Overview

Merodach-baladan, known in Akkadian as Marduk-apla-iddina II, was a Chaldean leader who twice ruled Babylon during the late eighth century BCE. His first reign ended when Sargon II took Babylon, and Sennacherib drove him from power again after a brief return. Kings and Isaiah describe an embassy from him to Hezekiah of Judah. This visit plausibly belongs to the diplomacy of resistance to Assyria, although the sources leave its date and immediate purpose unsettled. The biblical accounts make the encounter an occasion for anticipating Judah's future relations with Babylon.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Marduk-apla-iddina came from Bīt-Yakīn, a Chaldean political group in southern [[babylonia|Babylonia]]. The Babylonian Chronicle places his accession around the time of Sargon II's rise to power. The reign is often dated 722/721–710 BCE. The chronicle and Assyrian inscriptions describe [[sargon-ii|Sargon II's]] intervention and the end of the reign. The king returned to power in 703 BCE for a brief period before [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] expelled him. His name also appears on inscribed objects associated with his rule, including records of grants [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles] [@rinap3-1].

## Biblical Portrayal

In 2 Kgs 20:12–19, a Babylonian king called Berodach-baladan sends an embassy after hearing that [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] has been ill. Isa 39:1–8 presents the same episode with the form Merodach-baladan, closer to the Akkadian name. Hezekiah shows the visitors his valuables and resources. Isaiah then announces that treasures and members of his royal family will be taken to Babylon. The prediction turns a diplomatic visit into a scene about the later fate of the Davidic house and Jerusalem.

2 Chr 32:31 says that Babylonian officials sent envoys to inquire about a sign associated with Hezekiah. Chronicles presents this as a test of the king. Its explanation gives prominence to Hezekiah's conduct rather than describing the negotiations of the two kingdoms.

## Historical Assessment

A Babylonian ruler dependent on resistance to Assyria had a reason to seek other partners. Many historians therefore understand the embassy as an attempt to build ties against Assyrian power. Suggested dates include the last years of his first reign and the period of his return or resistance shortly before the [[sennacherib-campaign-701|Assyrian campaign of 701 BCE]]. No surviving diplomatic report independently documents the visit, and the relation of the episode to the ordering of events in Kings is debated.

The references to later exile give the biblical account a significance extending beyond the eighth-century visit. Many scholars view the wording or placement of the prediction in Isaiah as shaped by knowledge of Judah's later relations with Babylon. The independent records establish the identity, reigns, and political setting of the visiting king's patron, while the narration of the visit is biblical.
