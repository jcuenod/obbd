## Overview

Manasseh, son of Hezekiah, ruled Judah from Jerusalem for much of the seventh century BCE. Kings credits him with fifty-five years, the longest reign it assigns to any king of Judah. Assyrian royal inscriptions independently name him as a subordinate ruler who owed service to the empire. The biblical accounts remember him differently: Kings makes his conduct a reason for Judah's coming disaster, while Chronicles recounts his captivity, repentance, and return. The inscriptions establish his place in Assyria's imperial system but leave the biblical claims about his religious policies and captivity unconfirmed.

## Extrabiblical Sources

A Nineveh prism of [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]], dated c. 673 BCE, lists Manasseh among twenty-two rulers of the eastern Mediterranean coastlands and Cyprus. Esarhaddon says he ordered these rulers to supply timber and stone items for his palace complex at Nineveh [@rinap4, no. 1]. The [[esarhaddon-prisms|prism account]] describes what the group supplied without identifying Manasseh's individual contribution. It nonetheless places a named king of Judah among those whose resources the Assyrian ruler could command.

Prism C of [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]], an edition of his annals distinct from the [[rassam-cylinder|Rassam Cylinder]], names Manasseh in its account of the first Egyptian campaign, dated c. 667 BCE. The account says that the kings it lists brought gifts and that their forces and vessels were mobilized for the expedition [@anet, 294–295]. It does not specify the size of any Judahite force or say that Manasseh provided ships. Together the two inscriptions attest [[tribute-and-vassalage|vassalage]], the relationship in which Judah kept its own king but owed service to [[assyria|Assyria]]; they do not establish Manasseh's personal attitude toward imperial rule.

## Biblical Portrayal

In 2 Kgs 21:1–18, Manasseh reverses Hezekiah's removal of local places of worship, builds altars to Baal and to the [[host-of-heaven|host of heaven]], and installs an Asherah image in the Jerusalem temple (2 Kgs 21:3–7). The account says he made his son pass through fire, an expression associated with [[child-sacrifice]], and accuses him of sorcery and the shedding of innocent blood. The narrator presents these actions as leading to judgment on Jerusalem and Judah. Jer 15:4 likewise attributes Judah's coming calamity to what Manasseh did in Jerusalem.

In 2 Chr 33:10–13, Assyrian officers take Manasseh to Babylon in bonds, and YHWH restores him to his kingdom after he humbles himself and prays. The following verses credit him with building an outer wall west of Gihon and removing foreign gods and altars (2 Chr 33:14–16). Captivity and restoration, neither of which appears in Kings, allow Chronicles to portray both punishment and a return to legitimate worship within the same reign. Whether a known stretch of Jerusalem's Iron Age defenses corresponds to the wall described here remains disputed.

## Historical Assessment

The reign falls within the *Pax Assyriaca*, a modern name for the relative stability of parts of the empire under Assyrian control. After Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BCE, different parts of the southern Levant developed along different lines. The sizable seventh-century olive oil installations at Philistine Ekron show regional economic activity but are not direct evidence of Judah's recovery [@neaehl, 5:1952–1958]. Settlement evidence from the Negev and Judean Desert is more directly relevant to Judah, though its dating and connection to Manasseh's policies require separate argument. <!-- VERIFY: cite regional surveys and distinguish seventh-century settlement from changes attributable to Manasseh's reign. -->

Assyrian records do not corroborate the captivity episode. Its pattern of wrongdoing, distress, and restoration gives it a clear theological role in Chronicles, though some scholars allow for a historical detention behind the account. Similarly, Assyrian political dominance makes cultural influence plausible, but the royal inscriptions do not show that Assyria required Manasseh to install its gods in Jerusalem. The worship of the heavenly host described in Kings does not, by itself, identify that worship as an imported or imposed Assyrian cult [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 337–340, 369–371].
