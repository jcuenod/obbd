## Overview

Winepresses in ancient Israel and Judah converted grapes into juice for fermentation and storage. Many were cut into bedrock, with a shallow surface for treading grapes and a lower vat to collect the juice. The installations range from simple village presses to complexes capable of handling substantial harvests. Biblical authors use them to evoke the work and celebration of the vintage, the end of prosperity when the presses fall silent, and the violence of judgment. Presses, storage cellars, and inscribed vessels illuminate different stages of wine production and distribution.

## Archaeological Evidence

A simple rock-cut press consists of a gently sloping treading floor and a channel leading to a collecting vat. Workers crushed grapes with their feet and directed the juice toward the lower basin. Larger installations sometimes had several working surfaces or an intermediate settling pit that could trap solids before juice reached the collecting chamber. Biblical Hebrew terms include *gat* and *yeqeb*. Their usage varies, and an English translation may call one feature or the entire installation a winepress [@king-stager-life; @abd].

Bedrock installations are often difficult to date unless associated floors, deposits, or pottery survive. Reuse can obscure the original date and change the form of a press. Excavated late eighth- and seventh-century BCE examples around Jerusalem include complexes with multiple treading surfaces and a central collecting chamber. Their size and uniformity have been interpreted as evidence for organized production, possibly on royal estates. Simpler installations show how the same work could be done on a smaller scale [@neaehl].

At [Gibeon](gibeon.md), the excavators interpreted numerous rock-cut chambers as cellars for storing jars. Inscribed handles bear the name of Gibeon and personal names. The cellars concern storage and complement the presses that attest crushing. The [Samaria Ostraca](samaria-ostraca.md) record deliveries of a product often rendered “old wine,” further evidence for the administrative handling of produce [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions; @neaehl].

## In the Bible

The vineyard song in Isa 5:1–7 describes clearing stones, planting select vines, building a tower, and hewing a vat. The investment in infrastructure makes the disappointing harvest intelligible. Matt 21:33 draws on the description of the prepared vineyard for a story about its tenants. In Judg 6:11 Gideon uses a winepress for beating out wheat while trying to conceal it from the Midianites. A recessed winepress offers cover. Ordinary threshing benefits from an open location and wind for winnowing.

At Shechem, people tread grapes and celebrate in a temple (Judg 9:27). The festival at Shiloh takes place near vineyards (Judg 21:19–21). The notice suggests the connection between vineyards and seasonal gatherings without identifying the feast. In Isa 16:10, the end of treading and shouting marks the loss of Moab's harvest joy. Neh 13:15 associates treading with the transport of produce, both activities Nehemiah challenges on the Sabbath.

In Isa 63:1–6, the divine warrior's garments are stained as though he had trodden grapes. Lamentations likens Judah itself to what God crushes in a press (Lam 1:15). The ripe harvest and overflowing vats of Joel 3:13 signal judgment, and Rev 14:19–20 develops the image with a winepress of divine wrath and an outflow of blood. Pressure underfoot and a visible stream from the press explain the force of the imagery.
