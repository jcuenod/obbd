## Overview

Three annual festivals in the Hebrew Bible follow the agricultural year of the southern Levant. Unleavened Bread belongs to spring, Weeks or Harvest marks the grain harvest, and Booths or Ingathering comes near the end of the growing season. The biblical calendars give the festivals different names and rules, and some connect them to Israel's remembered history. Agricultural lists and ritual texts from nearby societies provide comparisons for the organization of festival time. The biblical calendars are the main evidence for how harvest, pilgrimage, offerings, and public memory became associated with one another.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The small inscription known as the [[gezer-calendar|Gezer Calendar]] lists parts of a year through tasks such as ingathering, sowing, and harvesting. It shows how familiar rhythms could organize the year without identifying a festival for each task. At Late Bronze Age Ugarit, related ritual tablets prescribe offerings for a particular month, in rites often associated with the vintage. They show how recurring seasonal activities and dated offerings could form part of a city's religious life [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions] [@ktu3].

Ritual texts from [[emar-texts|Emar]] on the Euphrates describe a festival called *zukru*. It brought together offerings and public rites at prescribed points in the calendar, with a fuller observance at intervals. The texts show the importance of scheduling and participation for communal worship. Their associations differ from those of the Israelite festivals and do not establish a shared festival of the same name or origin.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 23:14–17 lists Unleavened Bread, Harvest, and Ingathering as three occasions to appear before YHWH. Exod 34:18–26 calls the grain festival Weeks and connects it with the first produce of the wheat harvest. Both calendar passages associate Unleavened Bread with the departure from Egypt. Their accounts join seasonal observance with a remembrance of the [[passover-origins|exodus and Passover traditions]].

Deut 16:1–17 places Passover and Unleavened Bread, Weeks, and Booths at the place YHWH chooses. The instructions for Weeks start counting from the time grain is cut, while those for Booths refer to produce from the threshing floor and winepress. The text describes celebration that includes dependents and people without family resources. Lev 23:4–44 supplies a more detailed calendar with dates and observances, and Num 28:16–29:39 specifies festival offerings.

Booths has an especially clear association with gathering produce, and Lev 23 connects living in booths with Israel's wilderness experience. Weeks is associated with counting and bringing produce in the Hebrew Bible. A link between Weeks and the giving of the law belongs to later Jewish interpretation. Other scenes, such as the vintage celebration at Shechem, show that harvest could occasion celebrations beyond the three calendars (Judg 9:27).

## Interpretive Significance

Julius Wellhausen proposed that agricultural festivals acquired historical explanations as the biblical calendars developed. Many scholars still distinguish the language and concerns of the Covenant Code, Deuteronomy, and Priestly legislation. The chronological order, local practice, and stages of development within those bodies of law remain debated. The biblical evidence shows agriculture and historical memory together in several forms, with Booths and Unleavened Bread as especially clear examples.
