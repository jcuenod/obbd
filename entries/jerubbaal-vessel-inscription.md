## Overview

Fragments of a pottery jug from Khirbet al-Raʿi, west of Lachish, preserve a short early alphabetic inscription written in ink. The excavators date the deposit to the late twelfth or early eleventh century BCE. The editors read the surviving signs as the personal name *Yrbʿl*, usually rendered Jerubbaal, although the first letter is incomplete. The same name is given to Gideon in Judges. The vessel supplies an early comparison for the name and for alphabetic writing in the region, while the identity of the person named remains unknown.

## Description and Provenance

The pieces were excavated in 2019 from a deposit at Khirbet al-Raʿi. The editors distinguish the date of the archaeological context from the interpretation of the ink traces on the jug. The signs belong to the early alphabetic tradition of the southern Levant, relatively sparsely attested in this period. The text is incomplete at both the level of individual strokes and the wider surface of the vessel [@rollston-jerubbaal, 1–15].

## Contents

The sequence includes the element *bʿl*, “lord” or Baal, which occurs in several ancient West Semitic personal names. The editors' preferred reading begins with *y*, based on the remaining traces of the damaged first sign. On that reading the sequence corresponds to *Yrbʿl*. The surviving words give no title, genealogy, or account of the vessel's purpose. A personal name on a vessel can suggest a connection to an owner or user, but that connection is not stated in the surviving inscription.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Judg 6:31–32 gives Gideon the name Jerubbaal and explains it through the challenge that [[baal|Baal]] should contend for himself. Judg 7:1 uses Jerubbaal as another name for Gideon. The vessel would attest the name independently if the reading is correct. It would leave the narrative's explanation of the name and the bearer's identity as separate questions. The related form Jerubbesheth in 2 Sam 11:21 belongs to the biblical transmission of the name. See [[writing-and-literacy|Writing and Literacy in Ancient Israel]] for the wider epigraphic setting.
