## Overview

Egyptian lists at Soleb and Amarah West in Nubia give names for regions and groups that Egypt claimed to know or rule. The earlier list belongs to Amenhotep III in the fourteenth century BCE. Both texts include a phrase conventionally read as “the land of the Shasu of Yhw.” Scholars have compared Yhw with the biblical divine name YHWH. The possible connection makes the inscriptions important for the history of Israel's God, while the Egyptian writing leaves the identity and exact location of Yhw uncertain.

## Description and Provenance

The list at Soleb is carved on a monument in Amenhotep III's temple in present-day Sudan. A related list from the time of Ramesses II appears at Amarah West. These are topographical lists, collections of names representing Egypt's geographical and political world. The overlap suggests a relationship between the lists, perhaps through copying or a shared tradition. The later list is therefore limited as a separate witness.

## Contents

The inscriptions classify some names among the lands of the [[shasu|Shasu]], an Egyptian designation for groups commonly represented as mobile pastoralists. One name is written *yhwꜣ*. The syntax sets it in a list of places or population designations. A possible connection with Seir and the southern Levant rests on the wider group of Shasu references and the comparison of the lists. The evidence does not locate Yhw on a modern map with precision.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 3:13–15 gives YHWH as the name disclosed to Moses. The consonants in the Egyptian writing make a comparison possible, although the list gives no account of a god's character or worship. Deut 33:2 associates YHWH's coming with Sinai and Seir. Judg 5:4–5 pictures YHWH marching from Seir and the region of Edom, and Hab 3:3 places the divine appearance in Teman and Paran. These poetic settings help explain why some scholars look to the south when considering the Egyptian names.

Centuries later, the [[kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions]] refer to YHWH of Teman. The expression gives independent evidence of a southern association in the Iron Age. Its relationship to the Egyptian evidence and the origins of the cult requires further argument [@ahituv-echoes].

## Scholarly Issues

A connection between Yhw and YHWH is widely considered plausible. Some interpret the name as a place associated with a deity, while others give priority to its form as the designation of a locality or group. The spelling alone cannot prove the connection. The geographical possibilities support some versions of the [[yhwh-origins|southern-origin hypothesis]], including comparisons with traditions about Midianites and Kenites. The inscriptions provide no evidence of an Egyptian cult of a god named YHWH at Soleb.
