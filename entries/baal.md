## Overview

Baal is a West Semitic title meaning “lord” or “owner.” In the texts of Late Bronze Age Ugarit on the Syrian coast, it commonly designates the storm god Hadad, who brings rain and defeats powers that threaten his rule. The title could also be attached to gods associated with particular places or sanctuaries. In the Hebrew Bible, Baal is especially prominent in accounts of the kings of Israel and their disputes with the prophets of YHWH, the God of Israel. The wider evidence explains why storms, agricultural harvests, and questions of royal allegiance all matter in these accounts.

## Sources and Iconography

The [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]] from Ugarit presents Baal as a warrior and ruler with a palace on Mount Zaphon. His clouds, thunder, and rains connect his might with the conditions that make farming possible. The name [[hadad-rimmon|Hadad]] identifies a storm deity also worshiped in Syria and neighboring regions. Ugaritic texts apply both the personal name and the title Baal to the god [@parker-unp, 81–86, 134–137] [@ddd, 132–139].

The [[baal-with-thunderbolt|Baal with Thunderbolt]] relief from Ugarit shows the god with a raised club and a weapon whose shaft branches like a plant. The figure's attributes help explain why divine power could be described in terms of both weapons and rain. The evidence for the title in the wider Levant encompasses local forms and epithets. Biblical examples include [[baal-peor|Baal of Peor]], [[baal-berith|Baal-berith]] at Shechem, and [[baal-zebub|Baal-zebub]] at Ekron. Each name must be interpreted in its own setting.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Judges summarizes Israel's conduct by saying that the people served the Baals and the Ashtoreths, a name for goddesses associated with Astarte (Judg 2:11–13). In the story of Gideon, his family has an altar to Baal alongside an [[asherah|asherah]], a ritual object whose exact form is unclear. Gideon dismantles the altar and receives the name Jerubbaal, which the story interprets as a challenge for Baal to contend against him (Judg 6:25–32).

Kings describes Ahab building a temple and altar to Baal in Samaria after marrying [[jezebel|Jezebel]], the daughter of a Sidonian king (1 Kgs 16:31–33). During a drought, the test on Mount Carmel asks which god will answer by fire. Baal gives no answer in the narrative, and rain eventually returns after YHWH answers Elijah (1 Kgs 17:1; 18:17–46). The story asserts YHWH's power in a domain commonly associated with Baal. Kings later describes the destruction of Baal's temple in Samaria under Jehu and of a temple of Baal in Jerusalem (2 Kgs 10:18–28; 11:18).

Hosea applies the word *baʿal* (“lord” or “husband”) to a different kind of dispute. The prophet says that the people did not recognize YHWH as the source of grain, wine, and oil (Hos 2:8; MT 2:10). The book then pictures Israel addressing YHWH as husband while relinquishing the term *baʿal* and the names of the Baals (Hos 2:16–17; MT 2:18–19). The effect depends on the word's ordinary sense as well as its religious associations.

## Scholarly Issues

Which form of Baal Ahab and Jezebel sponsored remains disputed. Candidates include Baal-shamem, a title meaning “Lord of Heaven,” [[melqart|Melqart]] of Tyre, and a more local form of the storm god. Kings gives the title Baal without a distinguishing name. The text therefore cannot determine which title or deity the royal cult used for itself.

Israelite personal names such as Jerubbaal, Eshbaal, and Meribbaal, and names with Baal in the inscribed pottery fragments from Samaria, show that the element was tolerated in some Israelite settings [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, Samaria 12, 27–28]. Some scholars infer that *baʿal* could once have been a title for YHWH, while the personal names alone leave the intended deity uncertain. Comparison of Ish-bosheth and Eshbaal in 2 Sam 2:8 and 1 Chr 8:33–34 illustrates a scribal substitution of *bōšet* (“shame”) for Baal. Rain and harvest explain the god's importance without establishing older reconstructions of his worship as organized around sacred prostitution [@ddd, 132–139] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 204–207].

The editors of the [[jerubbaal-vessel-inscription|vessel inscription from Khirbet al-Raʿi]] read the surviving letters as *Yrbʿl*, or Jerubbaal, although the first letter is incomplete. The reading would give an early comparison for the name in Judg 6:31–32 and its variant Jerubbesheth in 2 Sam 11:21. The inscription gives no further details about the person named [@rollston-jerubbaal].
