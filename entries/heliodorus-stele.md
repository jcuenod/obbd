## Overview

The Heliodorus Stele preserves a dossier of Greek royal correspondence issued under Seleucus IV in 178 BCE. It records the appointment of Olympiodoros to supervise sanctuaries in Coele-Syria and Phoenicia and shows how the decision passed through the king’s officials. The main surviving copy combines fragments that appeared on the antiquities market with pieces recovered in excavations at Maresha. The inscription independently attests Heliodorus and gives documentary context for the royal intervention in the Jerusalem temple treasury described in 2 Maccabees.

## Description and Provenance

The inscription is cut on a limestone stele and is now displayed in the Israel Museum. The upper portion was initially known through the antiquities market, which supplied no controlled record of its findspot. Excavations in Subterranean Complex 57 at Maresha recovered inscribed fragments belonging to the same monument. Physical joins and continuities in the text established the connection. The complex had been disturbed by looters, so the discovery helps establish local provenance without recovering the stone’s original place of display. Additional fragments identified as copies show that the correspondence circulated in more than one inscribed version.

## Contents

The dossier comprises royal instructions and letters directing their transmission. Seleucus IV writes to Heliodorus about Olympiodoros, who is to exercise oversight of sanctuaries in the region known to the administration as Coele-Syria and Phoenicia. The royal letter represents care for sanctuaries as an obligation of sound government. Heliodorus writes to Dorymenes, and Dorymenes transmits the decision to Diophanes. The sequence gives a concrete example of communication from the court to regional and local officials. Instructions for public display explain how an administrative directive could become a monumental text.

The wording is important because it establishes an administrative appointment with regional scope. It leaves the appointee’s routine powers and the treatment of particular revenues less clear. The preserved copies do not mention an inspection of the Jerusalem treasury or specify a sum that the king sought to collect there.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Second Maccabees 3:1–40 places Heliodorus at the center of a royal attempt to take money from the [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] temple. The account opens by saying that Seleucus funded sacrifices (3:3). It presents a dispute over funds deposited in the sanctuary and narrates divine intervention as the reason Heliodorus leaves without taking them. The stele confirms that an official of that name transmitted a royal decision on sanctuary oversight. It makes intervention by the administration historically intelligible while leaving the reported Jerusalem incident independently unconfirmed.

Dan 11:20 describes a ruler who sends an exactor, commonly understood as Seleucus IV in the sequence of Seleucid kings being described. Identifying the official as Heliodorus is a further inference. Second Maccabees 4:7–10 depicts Jason’s acquisition of the high priesthood by promises of revenue to [[antiochus-iv|Antiochus IV]]. That episode illustrates how religious office and royal finance could intersect. It concerns a later reign and a different transaction from Olympiodoros’s appointment.

## Scholarly Issues

Interpreters differ over how closely the inscription and 2 Maccabees 3 belong together. The overlap is real at the level of reign, personnel, and sanctuary administration. A specific link between the appointment and a raid on the treasury would require evidence that the surviving letters do not supply. The two documents also give different kinds of access to the period. The stele records how officials announced a decision. Second Maccabees tells how the sanctuary was defended and what that protection meant for the community.
