## Overview

The sarcophagus of Eshmunazar II is an Egyptian-style stone coffin discovered at Sidon in 1855. Its Phoenician inscription names the Sidonian king, curses anyone who disturbs his burial, credits him and his mother with building temples and records a royal grant of territory. The inscription belongs to the period of Persian rule, although the exact date of the king's reign is debated. Its reference to resting among the Rephaim provides an especially clear Phoenician comparison for biblical passages about the shades of the dead.

## Description and Provenance

The coffin was found in the necropolis, or burial ground, of [[sidon|Sidon]] and is now in the Louvre. Its lid is worked in the shape of a human figure, a type called an anthropoid sarcophagus. The style and dark stone reflect Egyptian manufacture or artistic practice, while the inscription is Phoenician. The combination illustrates connections between Egypt and Phoenicia in the material used for elite burial. [@anep] [@kai]

## Contents

The inscription identifies Eshmunazar as king of the Sidonians, son of Tabnit. It reports that he and his mother built sanctuaries for several gods, including [[astarte|Astarte]] and Eshmun. It also says that the “lord of kings” granted Dor and Joppa to the Sidonians. The title is generally understood to refer to a Persian king, but the inscription does not give his personal name. [@kai] [@cos2]

The king warns against opening or removing the coffin. Among the penalties he invokes are loss of an established resting place among the [[rephaim|Rephaim]] and loss of descendants. The curse links burial, ancestry and continuity within the community. In this setting the Rephaim are figures associated with the dead, comparable to ancestral figures in some Ugaritic texts. [@kai] [@ddd]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 14:9–11 depicts dead rulers rising to greet an arriving king in the realm of the dead. Isa 26:14 and Ps 88:10 (MT 88:11) also use language associated with departed shades. The Sidonian inscription shows that related language belonged to a Phoenician conception of burial and the dead. The political claims can be compared separately with notices such as Ezra 3:7, where Sidonians and Tyrians participate in trade serving Jerusalem under Persian authority. [@ddd] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

The inscription does not date the reign by reference to a named Persian king. Estimates depend on the history of Sidon's royal house, the script and the chronology of Egyptian-style coffins. Older reconstructions frequently put Eshmunazar in the early fifth century BCE, and the Louvre gives an early fifth-century date while also recording a proposal around 525 BCE. Josette Elayi has argued for an earlier reign in the late sixth century. The description here follows the broader Persian-period dating, since the arguments for a narrower date remain disputed. [@kai] [@abd]
