## Overview

The Kadesh battle reliefs and inscriptions celebrate Ramesses II’s campaign against the Hittites in his fifth regnal year, around 1274 BCE. Egyptian temples display the king in battle and describe his rescue by the god Amun after he is cut off from his forces. The scenes also preserve details of a royal military camp. They provide a comparison for biblical accounts of divine rescue and for proposed Egyptian parallels to the camp and portable sanctuary of Exodus and Numbers.

## Description and Provenance

Versions of the Kadesh scenes appear at several Egyptian temples, including Abu Simbel, Luxor, Karnak, the Ramesseum and Abydos. Their arrangement and preservation differ. The monuments integrate pictures, captions and more extended narratives, conventionally called the Poem and the Bulletin. The scene is Kadesh on the Orontes River in Syria, a point of competition between the Egyptian and Hittite empires. [[kadesh-barnea|Kadesh-barnea]] in the biblical wilderness itinerary is a different place. [@cos2, 2.5] [@ael, 2:57–72] [@anep, figs. 336–337]

## Contents

The Bulletin gives a relatively terse account of the approach to Kadesh and the Hittite attack, which follows misleading information and confusion over the enemy’s location. The Poem dwells on [[ramesses-ii|Ramesses II]] isolated in battle and calling on Amun, who comes to his aid. The reliefs represent troops, chariots and the Egyptian camp, with a royal tent inside its enclosure. Repeating the account on temple walls made a military episode into a lasting claim about the king’s prowess and divine support. [@cos2, 2.5] [@ael, 2:57–72] [@anep, figs. 336–337]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 14:13–14 portrays the Lord fighting for Israel when the people cannot save themselves. Ps 18:6–19 (MT 18:7–20) gives the related motif of a distressed figure calling on God and being delivered. The Egyptian account similarly presents divine intervention in an urgent crisis, while emphasizing the pharaoh’s acts and his special relationship with Amun. The comparisons help identify an ancient form of royal and divine-warrior language without establishing direct contact between the compositions. [@cos2, 2.5] [@abd]

Some scholars compare the tabernacle and ordered camp of Num 2:1–34 with the Egyptian camp and its centrally placed royal tent. Exod 26:15–33 has also been compared with the Egyptian pavilion’s proportions and interior division. These are debated comparisons of layouts and functions, including the placement of a seat of authority amid a camp. The biblical description is ordinarily associated with Priestly texts whose composition is generally dated much later than the reliefs. [@homan-tents, 111–116] [@abd, 6:294–299]

## Scholarly Issues

The Egyptian monuments present a triumph, but Ramesses did not secure lasting control of Kadesh. Historians commonly regard the campaign as indecisive or as falling short of the Egyptian king’s aims. Ramesses and the Hittite king Hattusili III later concluded the [[egyptian-hittite-peace-treaty|Egyptian-Hittite treaty]] in Ramesses’s twenty-first year. The treaty belongs to a subsequent phase of relations and is a separate source from the temple narratives about the battle. [@cos2, 2.5] [@beckman-hdt, 90–94]
