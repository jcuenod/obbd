## Overview

The Sefire treaties survive on three fragmentary basalt stelae from near Aleppo in Syria. They were inscribed in Old Aramaic in the mid-eighth century BCE and record obligations binding Matiʿel of Arpad to Bar-Gaʾyah of KTK. Divine witnesses, loyalty provisions and threats against treaty breakers illuminate how political agreements acquired religious force. Several curses refer to objects used in symbolic actions, providing an especially close comparison with biblical depictions of animals cut in covenant-making and warnings of the consequences of disloyalty.

## Description and Provenance

The fragments were recovered at or near modern al-Safira, southeast of Aleppo. The first two monuments entered the museum at Damascus, and fragments of the third entered the Beirut museum. Their lettering and political setting point to the middle of the eighth century BCE. Tiglath-pileser III's capture of Arpad in 740 BCE gives a likely upper limit for agreements presenting Matiʿel as its ruling king. The three objects are numbered Sefire I–III. [@kai, nos. 222–224] [@cos2, 2.82] [@rinap1, no. 35 ii]

## Contents

The inscriptions call the obligations *ʿdy*, a term for treaty or treaty oaths. The texts name parties and their descendants, invoke divine witnesses and describe duties of loyalty and assistance. The curse provisions include agricultural failure and devastation. Other curses draw on objects used in ritual. Burning wax models the threatened destruction of Arpad, and cutting a calf illustrates the fate invoked upon Matiʿel and his nobles. These actions make the threatened results of treachery visible. [@cos2, 2.82] [@kai, nos. 222–224]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Jer 34:18–20 recalls a covenant made by passing between the pieces of a calf and threatens punishment for its violators. Gen 15:9–17 also describes a promise accompanied by divided animals. Sefire gives a close comparison for the [[covenant-cutting-ceremony|enacted imagery]], while the individual texts assign different functions to the rite. Deut 28:38–42 depicts failed crops and locust damage. Hos 2:3 (MT 2:5) uses public stripping and desolation as threats, Mic 1:4 compares judgment to melting wax, and Isa 34:11–15 gives ruined places over to wild creatures. Similar images in Sefire show how familiar physical effects could express political and divine judgment. [@cos2, 2.82] [@abd, 1:1179–1184, 2:169–171]

Deut 13:6–11 (MT 13:7–12) makes loyalty more binding than a close personal relationship, a feature also found in Assyrian loyalty oaths. Ezek 17:13–19 treats breach of an oath to an overlord as an offense answerable to God. Isa 10:9 and 2 Kgs 18:34 both name Arpad among cities defeated by Assyria. [@saa2, 33–34] [@glassner-chronicles, no. 9, entries for 743–740 BCE] [@abd, 1:1179–1184, 2:169–171]

## Scholarly Issues

It remains unclear whether the stelae contain copies or overlapping parts of one agreement or versions of several agreements. Bar-Gaʾyah has been identified with an Assyrian king, the official Shamshi-ilu or a local ruler. The location and political status of KTK remain disputed. Similarities with [[hittite-vassal-treaties|Hittite treaties]], Assyrian oaths and biblical [[covenant-and-treaty-form|covenant]] texts reveal a repertoire of regional practices. Claims connecting particular texts depend on more than the presence of that shared repertoire. [@kai, nos. 222–224] [@saa2, 8–14, 28–58] [@beckman-hdt, 1–10]
