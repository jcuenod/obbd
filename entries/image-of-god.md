## Overview

Genesis says that human beings were created in God's image and likeness. In ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, image language could describe kings as representatives of gods, and words for an image could also denote a statue. A ninth-century BCE inscription from Syria uses equivalents of both Hebrew terms for a single object. These comparisons clarify the force of Genesis when it applies the language to humankind and gives humans rule over other creatures. The expression also became a basis for biblical claims about human life and conduct.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

An Egyptian king could be described as the living image of a god, giving visible effect to divine rule. Neo-Assyrian scholarly letters similarly apply the word *ṣalmu* (“image”) to the king in relation to a god. This language belongs to a wider [[royal-ideology|royal ideology]] in which a ruler acts on behalf of divine authority. Egyptian sources also offer a broader parallel. The *Instruction for Merikare* describes people as the creator's images in a passage about the care he provides for them [@ael].

At [[tell-fekheriye-statue|Tell Fekheriye]] a ruler's statue bears a bilingual inscription in Akkadian and Aramaic. The Aramaic text uses both *ṣlm* and *dmwt* for the statue, words corresponding to the Hebrew terms for image and likeness. The two words can thus refer to the same representation, even if they carry different associations in a given line. This evidence weighs against giving each word an entirely separate subject in Genesis [@kai] [@cos2].

Ancient [[cult-images|cult statues]] provide another comparison. Rituals for preparing an image could establish its role as a means of divine presence in a temple. The Tell Fekheriye monument is a ruler's statue dedicated to a deity, a different kind of image. Both settings clarify how an image could do more than display a person's physical appearance [@walker-dick-induction] [@mettinger-no-graven-image].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 1:26–28 applies *ṣelem* (“image”) and *dĕmût* (“likeness”) to humanity, male and female, and immediately describes their dominion over other creatures. Ps 8:5–8 (MT 8:6–9) also uses royal imagery of crowning and rule for humankind. In Gen 5:1–3 the terms enter a family setting, as Seth is born in Adam's likeness and image. Gen 9:6 makes humanity's creation in God's image the basis of its prohibition of bloodshed.

The noun *ṣelem* can describe tangible images. In 2 Kgs 11:18 people destroy images from the temple of Baal. Num 33:52 commands the destruction of cast figures. Genesis can therefore be compared with restrictions on divine images. Some readers see the human image as an implicit objection to making material representations of God. The creation passage itself does not explain the image language as a polemic.

## Interpretive Significance

Many historical interpreters take the language as primarily royal and functional. Humanity shares the representative authority elsewhere proclaimed of a king. Other readings emphasize an enduring relationship between God and humanity or features of human constitution, such as capacities commonly associated with likeness. These readings need not define image and likeness as separate properties. The royal comparison has particular force because of the commission that immediately follows in Genesis.

Later texts use the language for other purposes. Jas 3:9 treats the divine likeness as a reason to refrain from cursing people. Col 1:15 describes Christ as the image of the invisible God. The references to human value and to Christ's relation to God extend the vocabulary in different directions.
