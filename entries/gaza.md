## Overview

Gaza was the southernmost of the five cities conventionally associated with the [[philistines|Philistines]]. Situated on the coastal plain near the approach to Egypt, it served as an Egyptian administrative center in the Late Bronze Age and became an important city on routes linking Egypt, the Levant, and Arabia. Gaza appears in the Hebrew Bible as a border city, as the setting of Samson's final encounters with the Philistines, and as the subject of prophetic judgments. Most of the ancient city lies beneath the modern one, so written sources carry particular weight in reconstructing its history.

## Location and Identification

Gaza stood inland from the Mediterranean shore, where traffic heading along the coast could meet routes from the south and east. The main ancient settlement is generally placed beneath the historic center of Gaza City. Nearby archaeological sites, including Tell es-Sakan, Tell el-ʿAjjul, and the proposed site of ancient Anthedon, supply evidence for the wider region. They represent distinct settlements or sites with their own histories. UNESCO has documented damage at Anthedon Harbour and other heritage places in the Gaza Strip since October 2023.

## Archaeological History

Late Bronze Age letters attest Gaza's place in the Egyptian administration of Canaan. Abdi-Heba's letter from Jerusalem refers to Egyptian provision for Gaza (EA 289). Egyptian sources describe a route through northern Sinai known as the Ways of Horus. Scholars often identify Gaza, on the Levantine end of this corridor, with the place called “Canaan” in some Egyptian texts.

In 734 BCE, Hanunu of Gaza fled into Egypt before the advance of [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]]. The Assyrian king reports occupying Gaza and imposing tribute when Hanunu submitted. The episode illustrates why an empire interested in the border with Egypt would pay attention to the city. Herodotus places Cadytis, usually identified here with Gaza, at the beginning of a stretch of coastal trading centers under Arabian control (Histories 3.5). Pliny connects Gaza more explicitly with the [[incense-trade|Arabian incense trade]] (Nat. 12.63–65). Arrian describes Alexander's capture of the city after a siege in 332 BCE, between the fall of Tyre and his entry into Egypt (Anab. 2.25–27).

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 10:19 names Gaza in its description of Canaanite territory. It is one of five Philistine cities represented in the offerings sent back with the ark in 1 Sam 6:17. Judg 16:1–3 pictures the city gate as a place where the residents could watch for Samson. In Judg 16:21–30 the captured Samson is brought back to Gaza, where the Philistine rulers gather for a celebration and sacrifice to [[dagon|Dagon]]. These narratives portray Gaza as a fortified center of Philistine power and cult.

The notice about Hezekiah's campaign uses Gaza to mark the extent of his operations against the Philistines (2 Kgs 18:8). Amos condemns Gaza for deporting a whole community and delivering the captives to Edom (Amos 1:6–7). Jer 47:1 connects a prophetic utterance against the Philistines with an unnamed pharaoh's attack on Gaza. Zeph 2:4 turns the name *ʿazzâ* into a prophecy of abandonment through the similar sound of *ʿăzûbâ*, “forsaken.”

Acts 8:26 directs Philip to the [[roads-and-highways|road]] from Jerusalem to Gaza, where he will encounter an Ethiopian official. The road places the encounter within a network through which travelers moved between Jerusalem and points farther south.
