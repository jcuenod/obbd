## Overview

Anointing is the application of oil to a person or object. In the Hebrew Bible it can mark a king, consecrate a priest or sanctuary, or identify a person charged with a divine commission. Oil also had ordinary uses in grooming and care. The act takes its meaning from the occasion, the person who applies it, and the status or duty that follows. Comparisons from ancient Syria help illuminate some ritual uses of oil without assigning them all one origin.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Ritual texts from Emar in Syria describe the installation of a priestess and include the application of oil. This evidence places anointing within a sequence that changes a person's cultic status. It provides a comparison for Israelite priestly consecration. The differences in office, ritual details, and setting limit any conclusions about historical transmission [@fleming-priestly-anointing].

Oil was also a common product with a variety of domestic and public uses. The [[olive-oil-production|production of olive oil]] made it available for food, lighting, and personal care as well as ritual. The presence of oil by itself does not identify a particular rite.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 28:18–22 Jacob sets up a stone pillar at Bethel and pours oil on it. The act marks a place and object associated with his encounter with God. Exod 29:7 directs that oil be poured on Aaron's head. Lev 8:10–12 describes Moses applying oil to the sanctuary, its furnishings, and Aaron, distinguishing their installation and consecration within a larger ceremony. Ps 133:2 uses the motion of oil descending from Aaron's head to picture the goodness of kinship in unity.

In 1 Sam 10:1 Samuel pours oil on Saul and identifies him as one appointed to rule. In Isa 61:1 anointing describes a commission to bring good news. The language of being anointed can indicate an appointed role even when the text does not describe the physical act. See [[royal-ideology]], [[priesthood]], and [[messianic-expectations]].

## Interpretive Significance

Anointing can mark a transition to a status or task, but the surrounding words and actions tell readers what kind of transition is meant. The use of oil on a stone, the consecration of a sanctuary, and the appointment of a ruler have different objects and consequences. That distinction helps explain the range of later uses of anointing as an image for divine appointment.
