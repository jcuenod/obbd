## Overview

Beersheba was an Iron Age town in the northern Negev, at the southern edge of the kingdom of Judah. Its remains at Tel Beersheba include a planned settlement, defenses, storehouses, and a system for collecting and storing water. Stones from a dismantled horned altar attest worship at the site. Biblical traditions remember Beersheba as a place of wells, oaths, and sacrifice in the stories of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Later texts portray it as both a southern landmark and a destination for worship. The archaeology gives those traditions a regional setting and shows how a town functioned in dry country.

## Location and Identification

Tel Beersheba is an archaeological mound east of the modern city of Beersheba. It stands at the junction of seasonal stream valleys near routes through the northern Negev. The modern city preserves the ancient name, and there is also Iron Age evidence beneath parts of the modern city. The Iron Age remains discussed here are those of the excavated mound [@neaehl].

## Archaeological History

Excavations directed by Yohanan Aharoni revealed an early Iron Age settlement followed by successive fortified towns. The town archaeologists call Stratum II, meaning the settlement in the second numbered layer of the excavation, includes a gate, a perimeter street, domestic buildings, and public storehouses. Its regular plan suggests coordinated construction and administration. The water supply included a well outside the gate and a system that led water into an underground reservoir [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology].

A fire destroyed much of the final Iron Age town. The destruction is commonly connected with Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BCE. Scholars have debated the date of the town and its destruction on the basis of pottery and comparison with other sites, and the layer itself does not identify the attacking force. The town's layout and stores show the importance of the site within Judah independently of that attribution.

The excavators reconstructed a large [[horned-altars|horned altar]] from dressed stones that had been taken apart and reused, including blocks in a storehouse wall. Their reuse shows that the altar had already been dismantled by the time of that construction. A temple has been proposed as its original home, but no temple associated with it has been securely identified. The reason for the dismantling is unknown.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Genesis associates the name Beersheba with Abraham's oath and dispute over a well (Gen 21:22–33). A related story tells of Isaac building an altar, making an agreement, and naming a well (Gen 26:23–33). Jacob offers sacrifice there on his way to Egypt (Gen 46:1–5). These stories recall the local importance of water and worship. The excavated Iron Age settlement at the mound is later than the period usually proposed for the ancestors in Genesis and cannot date their stories.

Amos warns against seeking Beersheba and refers to an oath by the way of Beersheba (Amos 5:5; 8:14). These references suggest a place visited for religious purposes. Kings names Beersheba as the southern end of a district whose shrines Josiah defiled (2 Kgs 23:8). Aharoni proposed that the dismantled altar reflected an earlier removal of local shrines under [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]], as described in 2 Kgs 18:4. This proposed link belongs to the wider debate over [[cult-reforms-archaeology|the archaeology of cult reforms]].
