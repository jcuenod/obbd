## Overview

Geshur was a small kingdom associated in the Bible with Aram and usually placed east and northeast of the Sea of Galilee. It appears alongside neighboring Maacah in descriptions of boundaries and in accounts of the early Israelite monarchy. King Talmai of Geshur was the maternal grandfather of Absalom, who took refuge at his court. Excavations have revealed substantial Iron Age towns in the region, including a fortified center at et-Tell. Their material remains help reconstruct the region, while their exact political affiliations remain open to debate.

## Sources

Biblical narratives, boundary lists, and genealogical notices preserve the name of Geshur and describe some of its relations with Israel and Aram. They were written for different purposes and give only broad descriptions of its extent. Archaeological evidence comes from et-Tell and other settlements near the lake, including Tel Hadar and ʿEn Gev. The southern Geshurites mentioned near Philistia and the routes toward Egypt belong to a separate geographic context (Josh 13:2 and 1 Sam 27:8).

## History

At et-Tell, excavators found an Iron Age city with a monumental gate and installations connected with worship. A stone from the gate area depicts a horned or bull-headed figure. One interpretation connects it with a moon god, but the iconography can be read in more than one way. Et-Tell's size and defenses have led excavators to propose it as the capital of Geshur. Its association with the later town of Bethsaida is also debated and depends on evidence from a different period.

The other settlements around the northeastern shore take on different appearances across the Iron Age. Their architecture and pottery combine local developments with features shared with neighboring areas. Some scholars connect later changes in the region with the expansion of [[damascus|Aram-Damascus]] in the ninth century BCE. The exact date and circumstances in which Geshur lost any independent status are uncertain. In the eighth century the campaigns of Tiglath-pileser III brought Assyrian power into the region. Excavators have connected destruction at et-Tell with these campaigns.

## In the Bible

Deut 3:14 and Josh 12:5 use the boundaries of the Geshurites and Maacathites to mark the extent of neighboring territories. Josh 13:11–13 adds that these groups remained in the land alongside Israel. In 1 Chr 2:23, Geshur and Aram take the settlements of Jair, showing a more contested picture of territory associated with Gilead. The king of Maacah supplies troops to the Ammonites alongside their other Aramean allies (2 Sam 10:6–8).

Maacah, the daughter of Talmai of Geshur, marries [[david|David]] and is the mother of Absalom (2 Sam 3:3). Her name is shared with the neighboring kingdom. The match can be understood in the context of [[diplomatic-marriage|diplomatic marriage]] between royal houses, although Samuel gives no account of its terms. After killing Amnon, Absalom flees to Talmai and remains in Geshur for three years (2 Sam 13:37–38). He later refers to a vow he made there and describes Geshur as being in Aram (2 Sam 15:8). His refuge with his grandfather illustrates how kinship could secure protection across political boundaries.
