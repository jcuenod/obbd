## Overview

The Dead Sea Scrolls include hundreds of Jewish manuscripts, most surviving in fragments, found in caves near Qumran on the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea. They were copied mainly from the third century BCE to the first century CE. They include some of the earliest surviving manuscripts of biblical books, other works circulating in Second Temple Judaism, and rules and interpretations associated with a particular community. The scrolls show how biblical texts varied before the medieval manuscript tradition and how Jewish readers applied scripture in the centuries around the beginnings of Christianity.

## Manuscripts and Date

The first scrolls were found in the late 1940s, and eleven caves near [[qumran|Qumran]] yielded manuscripts during the main period of discovery. Most are written on leather, with some on papyrus and one on copper. Hebrew is the main language, alongside Aramaic and some Greek. The count varies according to how fragments are assigned to manuscripts, but roughly nine hundred or more are conventionally counted from these caves. Documents recovered elsewhere in the Judean Desert, including the Bar Kokhba letters and the manuscripts from Masada, have different findspots and histories. [@abd] [@neaehl]

Scholars compare handwriting styles in order to estimate the dates of manuscripts, a method called paleography. Radiocarbon dating measures the decay of carbon in the organic writing material and gives a probable date range after calibration. It dates the material, and the writing may be later. More recent measurements and analyses have refined or challenged proposed dates for particular scrolls. The date when a copy was written may also be much later than the date a work was composed.

## Contents

Fragments of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther are represented when books ordinarily counted together, such as Ezra and Nehemiah, are treated as units. The [[great-isaiah-scroll|Great Isaiah Scroll]] offers an unusual example of a substantially complete book. Other manuscripts copy works later known in translation, including Jubilees and parts of 1 Enoch. Still others preserve prayers, calendars, legal discussions, or works such as the [[community-rule|Community Rule]] and the [[pesher-habakkuk|Habakkuk commentary]]. Hebrew copies of biblical books and other works show that several literary traditions circulated at the same time. [@abd]

Emanuel Tov has described several patterns among the biblical manuscripts. Some are close to the later Masoretic Text, the inherited Hebrew text of the Jewish Bible. Others resemble readings known from the Samaritan Pentateuch or point to Hebrew forms behind the [[septuagint|Septuagint]], the ancient Greek translation. Some resist assignment to a known group. Eugene Ulrich emphasized that some differences reflect distinct literary editions of a book, beyond the smaller differences introduced through copying. These approaches both account for variation without requiring every manuscript to fit a few fixed text families.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

At Deut 32:8, the manuscript known as [[4qdeut-j|4QDeuteronomyʲ]] preserves a reading about divine beings where the Masoretic Text refers to Israel's descendants. One Samuel also circulated in several forms. A manuscript from Qumran describes Nahash's oppression at the point where the traditional Hebrew continues from 1 Sam 10:27 directly to the next scene. At 1 Sam 17:4, it agrees with some ancient witnesses in making Goliath two cubits shorter than in the traditional Hebrew text (four cubits rather than six, plus a span in both readings). The scrolls give early evidence for readings that can otherwise be known only from later manuscripts or ancient translations.

Isa 40:3 is interpreted in the Community Rule as calling the group to prepare a way in the wilderness through study and obedience. Mark 1:2–4 uses the verse to introduce John the Baptist. Both uses illustrate the continuing interpretation of Isaiah in the period, without establishing a direct relationship between John and the groups represented in the scrolls. Likewise the language of light, darkness, and Beliar in 2 Cor 6:14–7:1 invites comparison with sectarian writings, while a direct literary connection remains debated.

## Scholarly Issues

Many scholars associate the Qumran settlement and some of the scrolls with the Essenes, a Jewish movement described by ancient writers. Others question the link between the ruins and caves or propose that libraries were brought from elsewhere, notably Jerusalem. The collection includes manuscripts of different origins. Even on the Essene interpretation, the contents of the caves cannot all be attributed to one local group of authors. See [[jewish-sects|Jewish groups of the period]] for the wider context. [@abd] [@neaehl]

No securely identified New Testament text has been found at Qumran. A proposal identifying the tiny Greek fragment 7Q5 as part of Mark is generally rejected. Fragments that appeared on the antiquities market after 2002 require separate evaluation. A scientific investigation found that all sixteen pieces in one museum's collection had been altered in modern times to resemble scroll fragments. That discovery illustrates the value of a documented find context. See [[forgeries-in-biblical-archaeology|forgeries in biblical archaeology]].
