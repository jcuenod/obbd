## Overview

Avaris was a port and royal capital in Egypt's eastern Nile Delta, at modern Tell el-Dabʿa. During the second millennium BCE it was home to people with ties to the Levant, the lands along the eastern Mediterranean coast. It later became the capital of Egypt's Fifteenth Dynasty, whose rulers are known as the Hyksos. The city lay within the southern part of the area that later became Pi-Ramesses. Its location and long history of settlement by people from the Levant provide background for the names and movements of people in the biblical accounts of Joseph and the exodus.

## Location and Identification

Tell el-Dabʿa lies near a former eastern branch of the Nile. This position connected the settlement to inland Egypt and to maritime routes into the Mediterranean. Excavations and Egyptian inscriptions establish the identification with ancient Avaris. The nearby site of Qantir preserves the center of the later royal city of [[pi-ramesses|Pi-Ramesses]], which extended south into the area of Avaris.

## Archaeological History

The Austrian Archaeological Institute began its excavations under Manfred Bietak in 1966. The excavators documented buildings, pottery, and burial customs with counterparts in the Levant, including burials in which donkeys were placed near human graves. The settlement had houses of Levantine design, and one sacred district contained temples of both Egyptian and Levantine types. In combination, the finds indicate an established population with Levantine ties before and during [[hyksos|Hyksos]] rule. They also show that people at Avaris used Egyptian and Levantine forms side by side.

After the defeat of the Hyksos in the mid-sixteenth century BCE, the site continued to have strategic value. In the early New Kingdom, kings of the Eighteenth Dynasty built a palace complex there. Fragments of painted plaster from the complex preserve designs comparable to those of Minoan Crete, including scenes of people leaping over bulls. The paintings document contact with the Aegean world, though the route by which the techniques and designs reached Egypt remains a question.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The city called Raamses in Exod 1:11 is generally identified with Pi-Ramesses. The biblical verse presents it as one of Pharaoh's storage cities, built by Israelites under forced labor. Gen 47:11 calls the region where Joseph settled his family the land of Rameses, while Gen 45:10 calls their intended home Goshen. An eastern Delta setting fits these references, and scholars have proposed the Wadi Tumilat, a valley across the eastern Delta, as a possible location for Goshen. Its boundaries and its relation to Avaris remain uncertain.

The archaeological record shows that families with Levantine connections could settle and prosper in the eastern Delta. Fragments of a statue found in a tomb chapel at Avaris depict a high-status man with features conventionally used in Egyptian art to portray people from western Asia. David Rohl popularized the proposal that the tomb belonged to Joseph. The occupant's identity is unknown, and the proposal lacks support in the excavated evidence. The site provides regional background for the narratives, whose broader historical questions are discussed under [[exodus-historical-questions|the exodus]].
