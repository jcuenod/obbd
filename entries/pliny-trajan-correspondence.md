## Overview

Two letters in the correspondence of Pliny the Younger and the emperor Trajan discuss proceedings against people accused of being Christians in Bithynia-Pontus around 112 CE. Pliny describes how he questioned the accused and reports what some said about their gatherings. Trajan responds with guidance on handling accusations and recantation. The exchange provides evidence for Christian assemblies and for the choices of a provincial governor. Its date and administrative setting differ from those of the New Testament accounts it helps illuminate.

## Manuscripts and Date

The letters are preserved as 10.96 and 10.97 in the Latin collection of Pliny's correspondence. Book 10 collects exchanges with Trajan, many from Pliny's provincial appointment. These letters belong to the early second century CE and present the matter from the perspective of officials responsible for resolving cases [@pliny-letters, 10.96–97].

## Contents

Pliny asks whether age, repentance, or the name “Christian” itself should affect prosecution. He describes questioning the accused and marking Roman citizens for transfer to Rome. Those who denied being Christians could demonstrate this by invoking the gods, making offerings before the emperor's image, and cursing Christ. Pliny reports that some said they had met before dawn to sing to Christ and later gathered to eat. His account reflects statements made during his inquiry.

Trajan directs Pliny to deal with cases brought before him, to pardon those who deny the charge and demonstrate it by worshiping the gods, and to reject anonymous accusations. He also tells him that Christians are not to be sought out. The reply concerns the cases and procedure raised in Pliny's letter.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

First Peter 4:12–16 envisages suffering under the name Christian. Pliny's question about the name gives a later example of that name becoming a matter for legal inquiry. The use of the emperor's image can be compared with Rev 13:14–17, where worship and coercion appear within a vision. The details and purpose of the two sources are different.

Pliny's handling of citizens gives comparative evidence for the importance of status in proceedings such as those narrated in Acts 25:10–12. His report on shared food offers later evidence for Christian gathering, alongside the earlier and differently situated assembly in 1 Cor 11:17–34.
