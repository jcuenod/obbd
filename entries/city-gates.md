## Overview

City gates controlled entry to fortified towns and formed part of walls, towers and defensive earthworks. In the Bronze and Iron Age southern Levant, some gates had chambers on either side of a passage, while others used a series of piers or arches. Gates also provided places to gather, wait for news and sometimes perform religious rites. These uses help explain their prominence in biblical descriptions. Archaeologists debate whether several similar gates at well-known sites should be attributed to Solomon, whose building program is described in Kings.

## Archaeological Evidence

At Bronze Age [[dan|Dan]], a preserved mudbrick gate had an arched passage and a series of piers. Iron Age stone gates are often classified by the number of rooms or chambers lining the passage. Six-chambered gates have three chambers on each side, while four- and two-chambered plans have fewer. A gate might also be protected by towers or by a sloping bank of earth and stone, known as a glacis. The design made the entrance part of a defensive system. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

Yigael Yadin associated six-chambered gates at [[hazor|Hazor]], [[megiddo|Megiddo]] and [[gezer|Gezer]] with Solomon's building program, drawing on the list in 1 Kgs 9:15. Similar plans occur at other sites, including [[lachish|Lachish]] and Ashdod. The excavated layers and dates of the three gates remain subjects of discussion. Their shared plan alone cannot identify a builder, which is one reason the [[low-chronology-debate|chronology debate]] concerns more than the gate designs. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

Some gates also contained installations interpreted as places of worship. Standing stones and a raised platform near the gate at Dan have been discussed in that connection. At eighth-century BCE Lachish, the excavators identified a shrine and proposed that a perforated stone served as a symbolic toilet to profane the room. Others have questioned the identification of the room and the stone. Excavated gate structures thus illuminate the range of activities at an entrance while leaving particular functions open to debate. [@neaehl]

## In the Bible

In 2 Sam 18:24–33, David waits between gates while a watchman looks out for messengers. Neh 3:1–32 organizes work on Jerusalem's walls by stretches and named gates, which help locate particular groups and work assignments. Gates could also provide a place for [[courts-and-legal-procedure|legal and public proceedings]]. The scenes depend on the gate's role as an interface between the town and its surroundings.

Second Kings 23:8 names places of worship at gates in the account of Josiah's reforms, while 2 Kgs 10:27 reports that a temple of Baal was made into a latrine. These passages provide comparanda for gate rites and acts of desecration at sites such as Lachish. They cannot identify the particular rite or ruler behind the disputed installation. Another debated proposal identifies Khirbet Qeiyafa, which has two gates, with biblical Shaaraim, a place named in 1 Sam 17:52. The name and the gates provide grounds for comparison, while the identification remains unsettled.
