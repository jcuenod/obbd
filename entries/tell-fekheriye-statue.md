## Overview

A life-size basalt statue found at Tell Fekheriye in northeastern Syria bears Akkadian and Aramaic inscriptions dedicated to the storm god Hadad. It represents Hadad-yithʿi, a ruler of Guzan, and is conventionally dated to the ninth century BCE. The texts seek divine favor and threaten anyone who removes the ruler's name. The Aramaic uses terms cognate with the Hebrew words “image” and “likeness” in Genesis. The statue also preserves formulas of failed fertility and frustrated consumption that resemble biblical curses. Its two languages offer a view of how a local ruler addressed different political and cultural settings.

## Description and Provenance

The statue was found by chance in 1979 at Tell Fekheriye near the sources of the Khabur, commonly identified with ancient Sikan. Guzan lay nearby at Tell Halaf. The National Museum of Damascus catalogues the monument as inventory 7439. It stands approximately 1.65 meters high and portrays a standing man in a long garment. Cuneiform and alphabetic text are distributed across its surfaces. Since the discovery did not occur in a controlled excavation, its original installation is inferred from the dedication and regional topography [@kai; @rima2].

## Contents

The Akkadian text has thirty-eight lines, and the Aramaic has twenty-three. Both name the ruler, address Hadad, and ask that the dedication endure. The Aramaic text uses *dmwt* at the opening for the ruler's likeness and *ṣlm* for the statue in its later section (KAI 309, lines 1 and 12). Blessing includes the wellbeing of the ruler and his household. The closing curses imagine crop failure, inadequate nourishment, and other losses if anyone removes the dedicatory name. Differences in titles are noteworthy. Hadad-yithʿi is described in Akkadian in terms of governorship and in Aramaic as king of Guzan [@kai; @rima2].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 1:26–27 describes humankind as made in God's image and likeness, and Gen 5:1–3 uses the terms first for God and humanity, then for Adam and Seth. The Aramaic nouns on the monument show that cognate terms could both refer to a representation of the same person. This weakens arguments that the nouns must name two sharply differentiated aspects in Genesis. The original settings differ. The inscription concerns a ruler's material representation in a sanctuary, while Genesis speaks about human beings and relationships between them.

Curses in the statue inscription describe abundance in the usual means of production followed by scant return. Lev 26:26 and Hos 4:10 describe eating without satisfaction. Mic 6:14–15 and Hag 1:6 likewise turn expected results of work, sowing, or consumption into frustration. The comparisons place these formulations in a wider repertoire of sanctions and oracles. Similar effects can express different explanations of why a harvest or meal fails.

## Scholarly Issues

Dating relies in part on script, style, and historical context. The two texts broadly agree, but different titles and turns of phrase complicate the assumption that one is a uniformly literal translation of the other. Scholars also distinguish possible stages in composition, dedication, or reworking. The monument demonstrates the public use of both languages more securely than it reveals an exact sequence of translation.
