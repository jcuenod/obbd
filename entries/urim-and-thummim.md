## Overview

The Urim and Thummim were associated with priestly inquiries into God's will. Exodus assigns them a place in the high priest's breastpiece, and Numbers names the Urim in a procedure for guiding Joshua. Some narratives and versions of Samuel suggest that the consultation could distinguish between alternatives or yield no response. The objects and their use remain uncertain. An Assyrian ritual involving stones shows one way ancient specialists posed questions through material objects and supplies a comparison for the limited biblical evidence.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

An Assyrian text from Assur describes a form of divination that uses stones to obtain answers. Wayne Horowitz and Victor Hurowitz compared it with the Urim and Thummim because the questions and stones appear to have allowed a choice between opposed answers. This kind of practice is sometimes called *psephomancy*, meaning divination by means of stones. It offers a documented comparison, while the biblical passages do not identify the Urim and Thummim as the same objects or give a comparable set of instructions.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 28:30 places the Urim and Thummim in the breastpiece of judgment, part of the priestly clothing associated with the [[ephod|ephod]]. Lev 8:8 describes Moses putting them there as he dresses Aaron. Num 27:21 gives Eleazar responsibility for inquiring by the Urim on Joshua's behalf. In Deut 33:8 the terms occur in reverse order in the blessing of Levi. These texts establish their association with priestly service and with seeking guidance for the community.

The longer Greek form of 1 Sam 14:41 refers to the Urim and Thummim on opposite sides of an inquiry. The shorter Masoretic form does not explicitly name both objects. 1 Sam 14:37 reports an unanswered inquiry, and 1 Sam 28:6 lists dreams, the Urim, and prophets as ways through which Saul received no answer. In 1 Sam 23:9–12 David asks questions with the ephod present and receives direct responses. These scenes support a comparison with [[lots-and-cleromancy|decision by lot]], although their procedures may vary.

## Interpretive Significance

The names have been interpreted as “lights and perfections.” Other proposals relate them to opposed outcomes such as curse and innocence. Both the etymologies and their implications for the device are debated. The longer reading of 1 Sam 14:41 is important because it suggests a choice between two sides while giving little detail about the physical action. The possibility of receiving no answer further limits reconstructions.

After the early monarchy, narratives rarely describe use of the device. Ezra 2:63 and Neh 7:65 defer a decision about priestly entitlement until a priest can consult the Urim and Thummim. These verses remember it as a means of resolving religious questions and leave the particular dispute suspended.
