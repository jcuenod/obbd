## Overview

The image of a god riding on clouds appears in Late Bronze Age poems from Ugarit and in the Hebrew Bible. At Ugarit the storm god Baal bears a title often translated “Rider on the Clouds.” Biblical poets picture Yahweh moving with clouds, winds or winged beings, while Daniel describes a humanlike figure coming with the clouds of heaven. These passages use familiar storm imagery to express authority and motion. Whether particular biblical texts adapt an epithet of Baal or draw on more widely shared imagery remains debated.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]], [[baal|Baal]] is called *rkb ʿrpt* (“Rider on the Clouds”). The title suits a god whose activities are regularly associated with weather. It evokes motion and authority in a way that fits traditions of the [[divine-warrior|divine warrior]]. Near Eastern art also places storm gods on supports such as animals or vehicles, giving a visual setting for gods portrayed in motion. [@ktu3] [@parker-unp] [@keel-symbolism]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 19:1 pictures Yahweh riding on a swift cloud as he comes to Egypt, and Ps 104:3 depicts clouds as his chariot and wind as his means of travel. Deut 33:26 speaks of God riding through the heavens to help his people. Ps 18:10–11 (MT 18:11–12) brings together a cherub, wind and clouds. The related picture of Yahweh seated among winged beings is treated under [[cherub-throne|the cherub throne]]. [@keel-symbolism]

Ps 68:4 (MT 68:5) poses a linguistic problem. The Hebrew *ʿărābôt* commonly denotes dry plains or steppes, and some translations accordingly picture God riding through them. Others connect the word with the Ugaritic cloud epithet and translate the verse in terms of clouds, either by reading the Hebrew as it stands or by proposing a different form. Later in the same psalm, Ps 68:33 (MT 68:34) clearly places the rider in the heavens, which may inform the earlier verse without deciding it. [@day-dragon]

## Interpretive Significance

The combination of riding and clouds provides a close parallel between Ugaritic and biblical imagery. Many readers see biblical poets applying familiar West Semitic storm language to Israel's God. In some settings the claim may implicitly contest another deity's sovereignty. The degree of polemic and the process by which the imagery entered individual passages are harder to establish. [@parker-unp] [@keel-symbolism]

In Dan 7:9–14, a figure like a son of man comes with the clouds and receives dominion before the Ancient of Days. John Emerton and others have compared the pairing with the senior god El and the cloud-riding Baal. Others give greater weight to Daniel's own imagery and the role of the holy ones in the vision. The specific derivation from Ugaritic tradition remains debated. Mark 14:62 applies Daniel's coming-with-clouds language to Jesus. [@abd]
