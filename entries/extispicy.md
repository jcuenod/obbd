## Overview

Extispicy is divination by examining the internal organs of a sacrificed animal, typically a sheep. Inspection of the liver, called hepatoscopy, was especially important. Mesopotamian diviners used extispicy from at least the early second millennium BCE, and texts and clay models attest related practices in Syria, Anatolia and the Levant. Assyrian kings sought answers about threats, policies and appointments by this means. Ezekiel describes the Babylonian king inspecting a liver as he decides which route to take toward his intended targets.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Old Babylonian omen tablets and the [[mari-letters|Mari letters]] show that extispicy was part of a diviner's professional repertoire in the first half of the second millennium BCE. The first-millennium Mesopotamian series called *Bārûtu* assembles omens organized according to the parts examined. A diviner, or *bārû*, observed such features as a projection, depression, marking or abnormal shape. Lists state a possible configuration and then give the consequence associated with it. Clay [[liver-models|liver models]] from places such as Mari, Babylonia, Hattusa and Ugarit show how features could be named, described or taught. Finds at Hazor and Megiddo extend the material evidence to the southern Levant. [@cos1] [@anep] [@abd]

Neo-Assyrian queries to Shamash, the god of justice and divination, often give the names of interested parties, set a time limit and ask a question about a particular action or threat. Shamash and Adad were associated with the divinatory art, and practitioners could describe the answer as written on the organs. The procedure resembles a formal appeal to a judge. Reports preserve the observed features in detail and may give a favorable or unfavorable answer. A report could record observations of lungs and other organs after the liver. [@cos1] [@abd]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ezek 21:21 gives the only explicit description of the practice in the Hebrew Bible. At a fork in the road the king of Babylon shakes arrows, consults household images known as teraphim and examines the liver. The scene gives the king multiple means of seeking a decision. The oracle presents that decision within Ezekiel's account of impending judgment on Jerusalem. [@abd]

Other proposals remain disputed. In 2 Kgs 16:15 Ahaz reserves the bronze altar for an activity that may be understood as inquiry. The verse specifies no procedure. Lev 3:4 calls for the removal of a part attached to the liver as part of an offering. The provision describes the treatment of an offering and gives no omen. The Hebrew verb often translated “inquire” in Ps 27:4 has also been discussed in this connection. The psalm gives no animal or organ and reads naturally as a desire to seek God in the sanctuary. [@abd]

## Interpretive Significance

The Assyrian evidence gives a context for the seriousness with which a ruler could consult the gods before a campaign. The recording of precise questions and detailed observations made extispicy a specialized court practice with responsibilities shared by scribes and ritual experts. Ezekiel depicts the outcome as intelligible in terms of the Lord's purposes for Jerusalem, even while the foreign king interprets his decision through his own customary procedures. [@abd] [@cos1]
