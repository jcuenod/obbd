## Overview

Across much of the ancient Near East, people reported messages understood to come directly from a deity. Letters from Mari, collections from the Assyrian court, and scattered inscriptions preserve such messages alongside other forms of divination. They warn rulers, promise protection, make demands, and intervene in political disputes. They also reveal a range of speakers and ways of checking a claim. Biblical prophecy shares many of these features. Its surviving books preserve a distinctive history of collecting, editing, and transmitting prophetic words for new communities and circumstances.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Prophecy in this context refers to a message presented as divine communication through a person. It belongs to the larger field of [[divination|divination]], the attempt to discern divine knowledge or intentions. A message could come through a dream, ecstatic experience, or an utterance without any described procedure. In practice these categories can overlap. A court receiving such a report still had to decide what authority to give it.

The eighteenth-century BCE [[mari-letters|Mari letters]] preserve several dozen reports concerning prophetic activity. Some speakers are called *āpilum*, an “answerer,” or *muḫḫûm*, a term often rendered “ecstatic.” The titles do not reduce the range of speakers to two uniform offices. Women and men appear, and people could transmit a message through an official rather than approach the king in person. Reports include orders to fulfill cultic obligations, warnings about military commitments, and assurances of divine support. Some send a speaker’s hair or garment for checking the message by other means. Reports associated with Eshnunna add a smaller body of evidence from the Old Babylonian period.

Seventh-century BCE [[neo-assyrian-prophecies|Assyrian oracles]] collected under Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal include promises of support in struggles over kingship and war. Several speakers are associated with the goddess Ishtar of Arbela, and women are prominent among them. The assurances frequently tell the king to have no fear. The arrangement of several utterances on tablets gives evidence that Assyrian scribes preserved and organized oracles. Their survival reflects the concerns of the royal archive, so it cannot fully represent prophetic activity among people outside the court.

West Semitic evidence comes from different kinds of documents. On the [[zakkur-stele|Zakkur Stele]], the king credits Baalshamayn with a promise of deliverance conveyed through seers. The [[deir-alla-inscription|Deir ʿAlla inscription]] depicts Balaam son of Beor receiving a troubling vision. Its literary form differs from an archival report. The Egyptian [[report-of-wenamun|Report of Wenamun]] depicts an ecstatic at Byblos, and Lachish Letter 3 mentions someone called the prophet without preserving his words clearly in context. Such scattered references establish a wider distribution of claims to divine messages. They are too varied to reconstruct one Levantine institution.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 18:15–22 considers how a legitimate prophetic word may be recognized. Deut 13:1–5 makes loyalty to Israel’s God a separate criterion, even when a sign occurs. These passages show that an utterance did not escape testing simply because its speaker claimed divine authority. The stories of Micaiah in 1 Kgs 22:1–28 and of Jeremiah and Hananiah in Jer 28:1–17 dramatize conflicts in which different speakers claim to know the divine will. Court settings elsewhere make such contests intelligible without determining which historical events the biblical narratives preserve.

First Kings 18:26–29 portrays Baal’s prophets performing public rites and awaiting an answer. In 1 Sam 10:5–13 Saul joins a group of prophets in an exceptional experience, and Jer 29:26 associates disorderly behavior and prophesying in an adversarial letter. These passages should be read in light of their different narrators and speakers. Amos 7:10–17 presents a dispute about who has the right to speak in a royal sanctuary. Num 22–24 depicts a foreign specialist whose words ultimately resist the political purpose for which he was summoned.

Isa 41:10, 13–14 addresses an audience with the assurance that it need not fear. The form resembles Assyrian assurances to rulers, while its addressee and theological setting differ. Familiar language allowed divine promises to address new circumstances. Formal similarity alone is insufficient to establish that the prophet read an Assyrian collection.

## Interpretive Significance

The surviving records suggest many continuities across regions in the experience, reporting, and political use of prophecy. Their differences are especially visible in what happened to the words afterward. Mari sent reports to an administration, and Assyria kept collections in a royal archive. The biblical tradition transmitted extensive prophetic books in which oracles, narratives, and later interpretation became authoritative scripture. Differences in preservation affect that comparison. The available sources do document a distinctive literary and canonical history for biblical prophecy.
