## Overview

Gudea ruled the southern Mesopotamian state of Lagash near the end of the third millennium BCE. He used the Sumerian title *ensi*, a local ruler or governor. Statues, clay cylinders, and shorter inscriptions preserve his account of rebuilding temples, especially that of Ningirsu at Girsu. They portray the ruler as a shepherd chosen by the city's god and responsible for carrying out his instructions. This portrayal provides a comparison for biblical accounts that connect leadership with divine favor and make construction of a sanctuary a central achievement of the ruler.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Short building inscriptions survive on cones and bricks, while longer texts appear on stone statues and clay cylinders. Several refer to work at the Eninnu, the house of Ningirsu at Girsu. A well-known [[statue-of-gudea|statue of Gudea]] presents him seated with a drawing of a building plan on his lap. The [[gudea-cylinders|Gudea Cylinders]] narrate the temple project from divine instructions through dedication. These sources reveal how the ruler's achievements were presented for gods, successors, and people connected with the sanctuary. [@cos2, 2.155] [@anep, fig. 434]

Gudea's inscriptions identify him as *ensi* of Lagash, whereas Ningirsu is described as lord or king. This vocabulary gives the god priority in the ruler's account of authority. It does not establish that Gudea exercised only limited political power. The records describe substantial projects drawing on labor and resources beyond the immediate temple precinct. [@cos2, 2.155]

## Biblical Portrayal

Gudea is not named in the Bible. The closest comparisons concern the portrayal of rulers as builders and recipients of divine instructions. In 2 Sam 7:12–13, David's descendant is to build a house for YHWH. In 1 Kgs 5:1–5, Solomon explains that he can pursue the building project under conditions different from those of David's reign. Gudea's account similarly places the sanctuary within the relationship between a ruler and his god, using dreams to authorize the work. See [[temple-building-accounts|temple-building accounts]]. [@hurowitz-exalted-house, 33–57]

The language of the ruler as shepherd also appears in both traditions. Ezek 34:23–24 anticipates a Davidic shepherd who will tend the people. Gudea's descriptions associate successful rule with care for the land and obedience to Ningirsu. The common image gives a point of comparison across different periods and political settings. See [[shepherd-kingship|shepherd kingship]]. [@cos2, 2.155]

## Historical Assessment

Chronologies place Gudea around the late twenty-second or early twenty-first century BCE. The exact years and his relationship to the rise of the Third Dynasty of Ur remain debated. His dated place in the region's history depends on reconstructing a sequence from rulers' inscriptions and other texts. Exact endpoints are estimates within particular chronological schemes.

Gudea's building activities are unusually well documented. The picture of a humble shepherd who followed divine commands comes mainly from the monuments commissioned in his name. The inscriptions establish what he chose to commemorate, while the extent to which projects affected different parts of the population is harder to recover. The comparison with biblical accounts concerns this language of rule and temple building across societies.
