## Overview

The Sumerian poem conventionally called *A Man and His God* describes a sufferer who loses health and social standing, appeals to his personal god, and is restored. In his lament he describes rejection by those around him and sets his affliction beside the general human condition of guilt. The poem is an early literary treatment of suffering, personal prayer, and divine reconciliation. It is often read alongside Job and the individual laments of the Psalms. Its account of human fault and its direct appeal to a personal god give it a distinct approach.

## Manuscripts and Date

The poem has been reconstructed from Old Babylonian tablets, chiefly from Nippur. Its surviving form reflects the Sumerian literary tradition maintained by scribes who copied and studied older compositions. The name *A Man and His God* is a modern descriptive title. An Akkadian work often called *A Dialogue between a Man and His God* is a separate composition, and the later Akkadian pious-sufferer poem *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* belongs to a further stage of literature on the topic [@cos1, 1.179, 1.151, 1.153; @anet, 589–591; @lambert-bwl, 10–16, 21–27].

## Contents

The story gives little personal history for its sufferer. The man becomes ill, finds people around him hostile, and feels deprived of a place in the social world. His speech addresses his god directly and places grief in the household through the involvement of his mother. He treats the human species as liable to wrongdoing from birth without identifying a single offence that explains everything that has happened to him. The narrator connects the resolution to the god's favorable reception of his lament. The god turns his condition around and removes the affliction, and the man's restored speech celebrates the deity.

A personal god in Mesopotamian sources could be a source of protection and an advocate in the divine assembly. Here the personal relationship frames both the sufferer's complaint and his recovery. The poem's mixture of protest, self-examination, and praise makes lament a means of presenting a damaged relationship to the deity.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Job likewise describes being abandoned by relatives, acquaintances, and dependants (Job 19:13–19). His question about whether the clean can come from the unclean (Job 14:4) gives one point of comparison with the Sumerian lines on guilt. Job, however, continues to dispute the friends' claim that his sufferings can be inferred from wrongdoing. His eventual restoration in Job 42:10–12 follows an extended dialogue, a divine speech, and his intercession for his friends.

The Psalms provide closer comparisons for individual movements in the poem. Psalm 6 places anguish and social hostility in a direct plea for mercy. Psalm 32:3–5 brings bodily distress and the confession of sin together, and Ps 51:5 reaches back to its speaker's conception to express the depth of guilt. The texts show several approaches to the relationship between suffering and fault. Comparison illuminates their assumptions about prayer even where the explanations of suffering remain different.

## Scholarly Issues

Fragmentary lines and the sparse characterization limit how closely the poem can be read as a case history. Modern descriptions of a righteous sufferer can obscure its account of general human guilt. The manuscripts also set a firmer terminus for transmission than for original composition.
