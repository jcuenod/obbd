## Overview

Excavations at Tel Moẓa, about seven kilometers northwest of ancient Jerusalem, uncovered an Iron Age shrine and a larger temple built above it. The monumental building had a forecourt and cult objects, and stood near substantial grain storage facilities. The excavators place its construction in the late tenth or early ninth century BCE. The complex offers evidence that formal worship and economic activity were organized at a site near Jerusalem during the monarchy. Its relationship to Jerusalem and the identity of its worshippers and deity remain subjects of investigation.

## Location and Identification

Tel Moẓa lies by the Soreq valley on an approach to Jerusalem. The ancient settlement is usually identified with Mozah, named in the list of Benjaminite towns in Josh 18:26. The surviving name and location support the identification. The biblical notice places Mozah in a regional list without describing cult there.

## Archaeological History

Earlier excavations found settlements of several periods and an Iron Age storage complex. Salvage excavations in 2012–2013 and later research exposed a large temple with an eastward approach, a courtyard, and a long main building. The courtyard yielded an altar and a deposit of objects including fragments of human and horse figurines. A smaller earlier cult building has been identified beneath the monumental complex. Successive building has complicated the relation of the deposits and walls. The temple's plan has close regional parallels with northern Levantine buildings, including [Tell Tayinat](tell-tayinat.md), and points of comparison with the biblical description of the Jerusalem temple [@mazar-archaeology; @hurowitz-exalted-house].

Grain silos and other facilities place the site within the economic organization of the surrounding countryside. Estimates of the political authority behind their construction and the degree of its control over worship remain inferential. The evidence establishes a close spatial relationship between storage and cult. The dates and uses of successive phases require separate treatment, especially where later public architecture reuses parts of the earlier complex.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

First Kings describes royal sacrifice at the high place of Gibeon before the Jerusalem temple was built (1 Kgs 3:2–4). Deut 12:2–14 calls for exclusive sacrifice at the place God chooses. The account of Hezekiah and the Assyrian envoy refers to removal of local shrines and concentration of worship in Jerusalem (2 Kgs 18:4, 22). The account of Josiah again describes local places of worship being defiled (2 Kgs 23:8). These texts have different narrative settings and purposes, and none identifies the temple at Moẓa.

The site is physical evidence of a formally built cult place near Jerusalem. Its scale invites questions about authority, patronage, and the practical relationships between the Temple in Jerusalem and worship elsewhere in Judah. Identifying its god or tracing changes at Moẓa to the reforms of a specific king requires evidence beyond a broad fit in chronology. The assemblage also shows why an excavated temple and the biblical category of a high place should be compared case by case.
