## Overview

Athletic contests in the Greek and Roman world required training, observance of rules, and competition for prizes. Major festivals gave prominence to the victor's crown, a wreath publicly awarded for success. Athletic language also circulated in moral teaching, where the discipline and endurance of contestants supplied models for conduct. These practices help explain the different ways the New Testament uses racing, boxing, training, and crowns to speak about effort and reward.

## Greco-Roman Evidence

The Isthmian sanctuary near Corinth hosted a festival with athletic contests. Archaeological investigation of the sanctuary and its stadiums helps locate those contests within a wider religious and civic setting. Public honors belonged to the achievement of victory, and the material prize at major Greek festivals could be a wreath [@broneer-isthmia2].

Epictetus uses the example of someone who wishes to compete at Olympia. The prospective contestant must consider prescribed training, restricted food, and the possibility of losing after substantial effort. The example shows how readily athletic preparation could illustrate moral deliberation and self-discipline [@epictetus-handbook, 29].

## In the Bible

In 1 Cor 9:24–27 Paul sets running and boxing beside the self-control of contestants. A perishable wreath makes the reward of disciplined life tangible and gives force to his contrast with an imperishable one. The Isthmian games are a relevant local comparison for [[corinth]], although the passage does not name that festival. Phil 3:12–14 uses the movement toward a goal and prize to describe strenuous pursuit.

The comparison changes with its setting. In 2 Tim 2:5 the contestant must compete according to the rules to receive a crown. In 2 Tim 4:7–8 an impending crown follows the completion of a course and is awarded by the Lord as judge. Heb 12:1–2 emphasizes endurance and keeping one's attention on Jesus. First Timothy 4:7–8 assigns bodily training a limited value in the course of making a comparison with training in godliness.

## Interpretive Significance

The contest can illuminate preparation, endurance, judgment, or public recognition. Each image needs to be read with the point the writer makes about it. A passage about running need not describe one specific race, and the reward evoked by a crown need not have the same force in every passage. The [[stoics-epicureans-and-moral-philosophy|entry on popular moral philosophy]] gives a wider setting for the use of athletic examples in moral instruction.
