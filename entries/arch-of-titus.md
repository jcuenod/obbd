## Overview

The Arch of Titus stands on the Via Sacra, a major street adjoining the Roman Forum. It was dedicated after Titus died in 81 CE and honors him as a deified emperor. The stone panels inside its passage depict the triumph, or official victory procession, celebrated after Roman forces captured Jerusalem in 70 CE. On one panel bearers carry spoils from the Jerusalem temple, including a seven-branched lampstand. It is a principal visual source for the Roman commemoration of the war and for the fate of some of the temple vessels.

## Description and Provenance

The marble arch has a single passage. Its surviving Latin dedication names the Senate and people of Rome as dedicators and calls Titus divine, placing the monument after his death. The monument is generally dated to the reign of his brother and successor Domitian. A separate arch at the Circus Maximus stood in Titus's lifetime. Its inscription, preserved through a medieval copy and fragments, credits Titus with subduing the Jews and destroying Jerusalem.

## Contents

One large relief shows Titus riding in a four-horse chariot. A figure of Victory crowns him. The relief opposite shows men carrying the [[menorah|temple lampstand]] and other spoils through an arch. The other objects are generally identified as the table for the bread set before God and the trumpets. A smaller relief on the vault depicts Titus borne aloft on an eagle, an image of his elevation to divine status.

[[josephus|Josephus]] describes the victory procession that Titus and his father Vespasian celebrated in 71 CE. He names a gold table and a gold lampstand among the spoils and also mentions a copy of the Jewish law. He says that the gold vessels were later placed in the Temple of Peace in Rome, while the law and purple hangings were kept in the palace. The arch and his account both select prominent objects for emphasis. The sculptors also used personifications and other visual conventions that present the events from Rome's point of view.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 25:23–40 describes the table and lampstand for the wilderness sanctuary, and Num 10:1–10 prescribes silver trumpets. The Roman relief gives a much later visual point of comparison associated with [[herods-temple|Herod's temple]]. It relates to the aftermath of the destruction foretold in Mark 13:1–2 and to the siege and captivity envisaged in Luke 21:20–24. The imagery of a triumph in 2 Cor 2:14 uses a familiar Roman practice as a metaphor and predates the triumph commemorated by this monument.

## Scholarly Issues

The sculpted lampstand has a large, two-tier decorated base. Scholars disagree about whether the base reproduces the temple object, represents a mounting or support for display, or reflects a choice by the Roman sculptor. The branches are recognizable, but the treatment of the base cautions against treating every detail as an exact record. In 2012, researchers detected traces of yellow ochre on the lampstand relief. These traces document some of the monument's original color, while the complete painted appearance remains a matter of reconstruction.
