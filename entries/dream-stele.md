## Overview

The Dream Stele is an Egyptian royal inscription set up in the first regnal year of Thutmose IV, around the beginning of the fourteenth century BCE. It still stands between the paws of the Great Sphinx at Giza. The inscription tells how Thutmose, while still a prince, fell asleep in the Sphinx's shade and received a promise of kingship from the god represented by the statue. The monument gives a comparison for biblical stories in which a dream at a sacred place grants authority, a promise, or guidance.

## Description and Provenance

The upright granite monument was placed at the center of the space between the Sphinx's forelegs. Its upper register shows Thutmose in scenes of worship before the Sphinx. Below is a hieroglyphic inscription headed by a date in the king's first regnal year. The lower part of the text is damaged, so the surviving wording chiefly establishes the circumstances and contents of the dream.

## Contents

The prince comes to the vicinity of the Sphinx and rests in its shadow at midday. As he sleeps, a deity identifies himself as Horemakhet-Khepri-Re-Atum, combining the local name of the Sphinx god, “Horus in the Horizon,” with three forms of the sun god. The deity speaks as a father to a son and promises that Thutmose will rule Egypt. The god calls attention to the sand that has encroached on him and asks the prince to act. The dream thus associates the care of the sacred monument with the future king's obligations.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 1 Kgs 3:5–15, God appears in a dream to [[solomon|Solomon]] at Gibeon. Solomon is already king and asks for the discernment to govern. God grants wisdom and adds riches and honor. Both accounts use [[dreams-and-dream-interpretation|dream revelation]] to establish the divine favor behind a ruler, and the gifts in each answer a different problem. The sacrifice preceding Solomon's dream also allows comparison with [[dream-incubation|dream incubation]], although the story does not state that he meant to obtain a dream.

Other biblical scenes use individual parts of this pattern. In Gen 28:10–22 Jacob discovers the sacred character of a place through an unsought dream, receives a promise, and makes a vow. In Gen 41:1–7 Pharaoh receives symbolic dreams whose meaning has yet to be explained. The Sphinx god speaks directly to the prince. The dreams of Gudea in Sumer give an additional example of divine speech connecting a ruler with responsibility for a sanctuary [@cos2].

## Scholarly Issues

The monument publicly asserts divine election and is an example of [[egyptian-kingship|Egyptian royal legitimation]]. Some scholars have inferred from it that Thutmose may have had a disputed claim or may have succeeded ahead of other possible heirs. The surviving inscription gives no direct account of a succession dispute. Divine election could also be celebrated as part of the ordinary presentation of kingship.

Siegfried Herrmann compared Solomon's dream and the dynastic promise in 2 Sam 7:1–17 with the Egyptian *Königsnovelle*, or royal narrative, a form used to proclaim important deeds and divine sanction. The comparison draws attention to the literary presentation of royal authority and building. It remains debated whether these biblical narratives show a specifically Egyptian literary form. In 2 Samuel the message reaches David through Nathan, a distinction that matters when comparing it with the speech of the deity to Thutmose.
