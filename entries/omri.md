## Overview

Omri founded a royal dynasty in the northern kingdom of Israel in the first half of the ninth century BCE and established its capital at Samaria. Kings gives him a short and unfavorable notice. The Moabite king Mesha remembers him as the ruler who subjected Moab, and Assyrian scribes continued to use his name for Israel after his family lost power. Those independent sources help explain the regional importance of a reign that the biblical narrative treats briefly. The date and attribution of some buildings associated with the dynasty remain debated.

## Extrabiblical Sources

On the [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]], the king of Moab says that Omri, king of Israel, oppressed Moab and took possession of territory around Medeba. Mesha presents his own recovery of territory as a reversal brought about by Chemosh. The inscription establishes that Omri’s name was remembered in connection with Israelite rule over part of Moab. Its description of the duration of Israel’s control belongs to Mesha’s account of his victories, and the exact sequence is difficult to align with Kings.

The [[black-obelisk|Black Obelisk]] of Shalmaneser III labels the Israelite tribute as that of Jehu, son of Omri. Kings depicts Jehu as the overthrower of Omri’s dynasty, and the Assyrian phrase is usually understood as a political designation. Assyrian scribes later called Israel *Bīt-Ḫumri*, the House of Omri. The name functioned as a label for a territory and its rulers, beyond the life of the king who established the dynasty.

## Biblical Portrayal

First Kings 16:15–28 traces Omri’s rise from military command to kingship. After Zimri’s brief reign, part of Israel supports Omri and another part supports Tibni. The text credits Omri with purchasing the hill of [[samaria|Samaria]] and building the city there (16:24). The account closes with a negative judgment about his worship. Its brevity reflects the book’s interest in evaluating kings as well as reporting their conduct.

Second Kings 3:4–5 introduces Mesha as an extensive supplier of tribute and reports that Moab rebelled after Ahab’s death. The passage shares with Mesha’s inscription the memory of Israelite dominance and a Moabite reversal. It organizes that history around successive Israelite reigns, while Mesha celebrates the reversal as his own religious and political achievement. Second Kings 8:26 associates the Judahite royal household with Omri through Athaliah, illustrating the dynasty’s reach. Mic 6:16 invokes Omri’s statutes and Ahab’s practices as negative examples, preserving another aspect of the dynasty’s reputation.

## Historical Assessment

Omri’s reign is often placed around 882–871 BCE, with another common reconstruction placing it in 885–874 BCE. The rivalry with Tibni complicates the reckoning of Omri’s twelve years, since the text can be read as counting from a claim to rule before sole control. The extrabiblical sources attest the dynasty’s standing without dating Omri’s accession directly.

Monumental construction at Samaria and [[jezreel|Jezreel]] is often associated with Omride rulers. In the [[low-chronology-debate|debate over Iron Age chronology]], some scholars also assign building phases at Megiddo and Hazor formerly credited to Solomon to the ninth century. Aligning a stratum with a generation of rulers can make a historical attribution possible. It does not by itself name the patron or separate Omri’s work from that of his successors.
