## Overview

Mesopotamian lament prayers ask a deity to relieve distress, forgive wrongdoing, or restore a relationship that seems to have broken down. The speakers may describe pain, fear that a god has become angry, or plead for help without knowing what caused their trouble. Several types of prayer survive, each associated with particular ritual specialists and occasions. The Akkadian prayer of raised hands and the bilingual lament to soothe the heart are especially valuable for comparison with individual laments and prayers for forgiveness in the book of Psalms.

## Manuscripts and Date

Prayers survive on cuneiform tablets, including first-millennium copies from Assyrian and Babylonian libraries. Some traditions are attested much earlier. A tablet's date gives the age of that copy, and a hymn or prayer can have circulated long before it was copied. Scribes sometimes transmitted prayers in Sumerian with Akkadian translations. Some manuscripts also preserve directions about the occasion, offerings, or gestures that accompanied the words.

## Contents

A *šuilla*, from a Sumerian expression for raising the hand, was a prayer addressed to a god or goddess. Many Akkadian examples were recited in rituals performed by an incantation specialist, sometimes on behalf of another person. A prayer might praise the deity, describe the supplicant's distress, and ask for relief. The Prayer of Lamentation to Ishtar (ANET, pp. 383–385) approaches [[ishtar|Ishtar]] as a powerful goddess who can respond to distress and relieve suffering.

The *eršaḫunga*, a lament to soothe the heart, includes prayers that seek to calm an angry deity. Some texts are preserved in Sumerian with an Akkadian translation. One prayer can be addressed to any god and acknowledges uncertainty about the fault that gave offense. The *dingiršadabba* prayers include appeals to personal gods to release a sufferer from divine anger. In a different setting, temple lamentation specialists performed *balaĝ* and *eršema* prayers, often in the Sumerian register called Emesal. Their concerns could embrace the welfare of a temple or city. See [[city-laments|City Laments]] for the distinct literary works that mourn a city's destruction.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ps 6 asks for healing and an end to divine anger, while Ps 38 describes physical pain and acknowledges sin. The repeated question of how long distress will last in Ps 13:1–2 (MT 13:2–3) has analogues in Akkadian lament. Ps 88 pictures the speaker near death and still calling out at the close. Mesopotamian laments likewise give sufferers language for divine silence and for the gap between distress and relief.

Ps 19:12 (MT 19:13) asks to be cleared of hidden faults, a concern shared by prayers to a god whose grievance may be unknown. Ps 51 seeks cleansing and a renewed heart, Ps 130 asks for forgiveness from the depths, and Ps 143:2 appeals to God in recognition of human wrongdoing. Ps 141:2 associates the lifting of hands with the evening offering, giving a biblical instance of the [[prayer-postures|prayer gesture]] recalled in the Mesopotamian term *šuilla*.

## Scholarly Issues

The similarities help readers see how ancient prayers made a complaint, confessed fault, and asked for relief. Specific prayers also differ in how they imagine access to a deity. Mesopotamian rituals could ask a personal god or goddess to intercede or address multiple deities. The psalms examined here address the God of Israel, sometimes drawing on imagery from ritual worship. Shared expressions such as asking how long distress will last give evidence for broadly comparable practices of prayer.
