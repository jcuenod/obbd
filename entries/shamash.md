## Overview

Shamash, called Utu in Sumerian, was the Mesopotamian sun god associated with justice, judgment, and divination. The daily movement of the sun made unseen matters a fitting concern for a deity who crosses the world. Ugaritic texts portray a female solar deity, Shapash, with a distinct role among the gods. Biblical texts use the sun to describe divine provision and justice while sometimes condemning its worship. The comparison clarifies how solar imagery could function without making every reference evidence of a solar cult.

## Sources and Iconography

Major Mesopotamian centers of Shamash’s worship included Sippar and Larsa. Hymns and prayers appeal to his ability to see wrongdoing and judge fairly. Ritual specialists also invoke him, sometimes alongside Adad, when seeking answers through divination. On the [[code-of-hammurabi|Hammurabi stele]], the king appears before a seated god identified as Shamash. The scene associates royal justice with divine authorization. The [[tablet-of-shamash|Shamash Tablet]] gives another visual and inscriptional witness to his cult at Sippar.

Ugaritic myth calls Shapash a lamp or torch of the gods. She is female and moves across boundaries that include the realm of the dead. Her literary roles overlap with some ideas that accompany the sun’s daily course in Mesopotamia, while the identity and actions of the two deities are distinct. Similar divine associations could develop differently across the region.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Second Kings 23:5, 11 includes the sun among the objects or recipients of rites curtailed by Josiah. Ezek 8:16 depicts people turned toward the rising sun within a vision of temple offenses. Deut 4:19 and 17:3 prohibit worship of celestial bodies, and Job 31:26–28 presents a denial of secret homage to the sun or moon. Josh 15:10 names Beth-shemesh, a place name that preserves an association with the sun. It does not identify the cult practices of each period at the site.

Ps 19:4–6 (MT 19:5–7) describes the sun crossing the sky and making its heat felt everywhere. The psalm’s language of visibility and order is intelligible in a world where the sun could symbolize public justice. Ps 84:11 (MT 84:12) uses the sun as an image for God, and Mal 4:2 (MT 3:20) speaks of a sun of righteousness with healing in its wings. The Mesopotamian association of sunlight with justice illuminates the imagery. The biblical passages do not invoke Shamash by name.

## Scholarly Issues

Some scholars have proposed a form of solar worship identified with YHWH in Israel or Judah. Biblical solar language and objects such as the [[winged-sun-disk|winged disk]] are relevant, but each can also carry meanings independent of an exclusive solar cult. The existence of acts directed toward the sun and the use of solar images for YHWH do not establish that the two were identical. Some have also read Josh 10:12–13 in light of omen traditions about the sun and moon. The comparison is a proposal about the passage’s background, not an attested explanation of the episode.
