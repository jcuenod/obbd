## Overview

The biblical expression “host of heaven” can refer to the stars or to the company of heavenly beings that attends Israel's God. The two meanings belong to an ancient world in which the heavenly bodies could be understood as living or divine powers. Iron Age art from the Levant shows that symbols of the sun, moon, and stars were prominent in the period when Judah dealt with Assyria. The expression helps readers understand both biblical portrayals of YHWH commanding a heavenly host and laws and prophetic texts condemning worship directed to that host.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian traditions associate several gods with the heavenly bodies. The [[sin-moon-god|moon god Sîn]] had major sanctuaries, including one at Harran, and [[shamash|Shamash]] was associated with the sun and with justice. Images of crescents, stars, and other signs occur on Iron Age Levantine seals and related objects. Some lunar images resemble forms associated with Harran. These objects establish the circulation of astral imagery. The identity of the deity and use of the image can vary with context [@ddd] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig].

Mesopotamian scholars also read movements or appearances in the sky as signs for the king and land. This practice belongs to [[astrology]] and provides a context for the association between astral beings and divine communication. The biblical phrase “host of heaven” encompasses more than any one cult or divinatory practice.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 1 Kgs 22:19 the host stands around YHWH's throne, part of a vision of the [[divine-council|divine council]]. In Job 38:7 morning stars and sons of God celebrate creation together. Isa 40:26 depicts YHWH as calling out and counting the heavenly host, using language that can evoke a commander assembling troops. The title YHWH of hosts, used in Isa 6:3, can likewise evoke divine command. Its precise reference may vary from passage to passage.

Deut 4:19 connects sun, moon, and stars with what God has allotted to the other peoples and forbids Israel to bow to them. Deut 17:2–5 treats such worship as a grave covenant offense. 2 Kgs 17:16 gives it as one reason for Israel's fall. 2 Kgs 21:3, 5 attributes altars for the host to [[manasseh|Manasseh]], and 2 Kgs 23:4–5, 11–12 reports Josiah's removal of cult equipment, personnel, and installations. The judgments depend on the distinction between the heavenly beings under YHWH's authority and the worship due to YHWH.

Jer 19:13 and Zeph 1:5 speak of offerings or prostration to the host on rooftops. Jer 8:2 uses the same setting of devotion in a threat, when bones will lie exposed before the bodies once served. The collective expression leaves open the identity of individual astral powers. Particular cults discussed elsewhere, including that of the [[queen-of-heaven|Queen of Heaven]], require their own evidence.

## Interpretive Significance

Older accounts explained Judah's astral cults as requirements imposed by Assyria. Mordechai Cogan and other scholars questioned this explanation because the sources provide little evidence of a consistent policy imposing such worship on vassals. Many interpreters now emphasize the circulation and local adoption of astral imagery and practices during Assyrian rule. Some features may also have local precedents. The seals and biblical texts document different aspects of that process and leave room for more than one channel of influence.
