## Overview

Babylon fell to the forces of Cyrus II of Persia in October 539 BCE, ending the Neo-Babylonian Empire. A Babylonian chronicle records fighting at Opis and then an entry into the city of Babylon without battle. A royal inscription presents Cyrus as chosen by Babylon's god Marduk. The Bible also gives Cyrus a divine commission, while Daniel and some prophetic texts portray Babylon's fall differently. The distinct accounts show why it is important to distinguish the Persian conquest of the empire, the occupation of the capital, and the later stories told about both.

## Sources

The [[nabonidus-chronicle|Nabonidus Chronicle]] records the chronology of the campaign in its account of the last year of Nabonidus. The [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]], a royal inscription issued after the conquest, presents the unpopular rule of Nabonidus as the reason Marduk brought Cyrus to Babylon. Both describe the entry into the city as unopposed. The inscription explicitly uses the event to legitimize Cyrus's kingship [@abc, 109–110] [@cos2, 2.124].

The Greek authors Herodotus and Xenophon tell of Persians diverting the Euphrates and entering Babylon during a festival (Herodotus, Histories 1.191; Xenophon, Cyropaedia 7.5). Their versions differ in important details and were written well after the conquest. The river-diversion story has no counterpart in the surviving Babylonian records. It presents a more dramatic explanation for the city's fall than the accounts kept in cuneiform.

## Reconstruction

The chronicle reports that Cyrus fought the Babylonian army at Opis on the Tigris. Sippar was then taken without battle and [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] fled. On the sixteenth day of Tashritu, a commander named Ugbaru entered Babylon with the forces of Cyrus. The chronicle says that the city was taken without battle and that Nabonidus was captured [@abc, 109].

[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] himself entered Babylon on the third day of the following month, Arahsamnu. The report describes order in the city and continued observance of rites. Violent fighting earlier in the campaign is compatible with an unopposed entry into the capital. The conquest transferred political control to Persia, while the city remained inhabited and continued to function as a major center [@glassner-chronicles, no. 26, 232–239].

## Biblical Accounts

Isa 45:1–3 describes Cyrus as the anointed agent of YHWH and depicts gates opening before him. Like the Cyrus Cylinder, the oracle attributes Cyrus's success to divine appointment. Each text speaks from its own religious setting and explains the victory in terms of a different deity. Ezra 1:1–4 reports Cyrus authorizing the rebuilding of the Jerusalem temple in his first year. The cylinder describes restoration measures in other regions without naming Judah.

Dan 5:30–31 (MT 5:30–6:1) connects a banquet with the death of [[belshazzar|Belshazzar]] and the transfer of the kingdom to [[darius-the-mede|Darius the Mede]]. No such ruler is known between Nabonidus and Cyrus in Babylonian or Persian records. The historical identity of the figure in Daniel remains unresolved.

Isa 13:17–19 and Jer 51:11, 28 picture the Medes as agents of judgment on Babylon. Their language of devastation differs from the Babylonian sources' description of the city's occupation in 539 BCE. Isa 21:1–10 also links Babylon's fall with Elam and Media. Scholars have connected this oracle with the Persian conquest or with an earlier Assyrian-era defeat of Babylon. The text gives too little historical detail to settle the question.
