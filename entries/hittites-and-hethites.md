## Overview

The Hittite kingdom ruled from Ḫattuša in central Anatolia during the second millennium BCE. After its collapse, several kingdoms in Anatolia and Syria continued Hittite or Luwian political and cultural traditions and are often called Neo-Hittite. The Bible uses a similar name for northern kings and for people living in Canaan, including the sons of Heth in Genesis. These names can have different historical referents. Keeping them distinct helps readers place biblical trade, diplomacy, and local traditions alongside the archaeological and written evidence.

## Sources

The tablets from Ḫattuša record the laws, treaties, and administration of the Bronze Age kingdom. Hittite was an Indo-European language, and the court also made use of several other languages. Archaeology and monumental inscriptions in Hieroglyphic Luwian are especially important for the later northern states. Neo-Assyrian inscriptions use the regional designation Ḫatti for places in Syria and farther south, and the geographical reach of the name varies by context [@beckman-hdt, 1–6] [@cos2, 2.21, 2.31] [@rima3, A.0.102.2 ii 78–102].

## History

The Hittite state emerged in central Anatolia in the seventeenth century BCE. It later became a major power across Anatolia and northern Syria. Its contacts with Egypt are documented by the [[kadesh-battle-reliefs|Battle of Kadesh]] and the subsequent [[egyptian-hittite-peace-treaty|Egyptian-Hittite treaty]]. The capital and imperial system collapsed around the beginning of the twelfth century BCE. In the Iron Age, rulers at [[carchemish|Carchemish]] preserved part of that political and cultural inheritance. Other states, including [[hamath|Hamath]], display Luwian and Aramean elements in different combinations. The growth of Assyrian power eventually brought much of the region under its rule.

[[hittite-laws|Hittite laws]] and [[hittite-vassal-treaties|vassal treaties]] illuminate institutions of the Anatolian kingdom. The treaties became important to George Mendenhall and later scholars who compared them with biblical [[covenant-and-treaty-form|covenant forms]]. Ancient Assyrian treaties provide other parallels, and scholars debate which similarities are most significant. Literary comparisons of this kind do not depend on linking the sons of Heth to a population from Anatolia.

## In the Bible

The trade in 1 Kgs 10:28–29 involves kings of the Hittites and of Aram. In 2 Kgs 7:6, Aramean soldiers fear that Israel has hired Hittite and Egyptian kings. These passages plausibly refer to northern rulers in the period of the Neo-Hittite states or use a designation familiar from that political landscape. Josh 1:4 includes the land of the Hittites in an extensive description of territory. Its wording has been compared with the broad use of Ḫatti in Assyrian sources, and the relationship remains debated.

The name functions differently in traditions about Canaan. Gen 15:20 lists Hittites among peoples of the land, Gen 23:3–20 describes Abraham negotiating with the sons of Heth, and Gen 26:34 and 27:46 associate Esau's marriages with that group. Num 13:29 places Hittites in the hill country. Solomon's Hittite wives appear among several groups in 1 Kgs 11:1, and Ezek 16:3, 45 gives Jerusalem a Hittite mother as part of its figurative accusation. The names of people in the Genesis setting fit a local Semitic setting. Whether the designation preserves the memory of smaller immigrant groups remains uncertain.

Uriah is called the Hittite in 2 Sam 11:3. His name contains a form of the name of YHWH, a fact consistent with participation in an Israelite setting. The designation and the name together leave his personal ancestry open.
