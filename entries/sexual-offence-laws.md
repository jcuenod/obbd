## Overview

Biblical laws regulate adultery, sexual assault, prohibited kinship unions, and other sexual acts. They distinguish cases by such factors as marriage or betrothal, circumstances, the rights of households, and effects on the community and its land. Mesopotamian and Hittite collections contain comparable laws whose provisions sometimes differ sharply. Comparison helps clarify what an ancient legal text treats as evidence and whom it holds responsible. Prescribed penalties reveal legal priorities, while the extent to which particular rules were enforced requires separate evidence.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Hammurabi’s Laws §§129–133 distinguish adultery, assault of a betrothed woman, and cases of suspicion. Section 129 allows a husband to spare his wife and the king to spare the man. The treatment of suspicion includes a sworn declaration or a river ordeal, depending on the circumstances. Sections 154–158 address prohibited relations within families. The [[laws-of-eshnunna|Laws of Eshnunna]] §§26–28 likewise link offenses to marriage arrangements and status. The categories concern the woman’s circumstances as well as family and household claims.

The [[middle-assyrian-laws|Middle Assyrian Laws]] A §§12–16 contain provisions about assault and adultery that assign responsibility in different ways. Sections 55–56 distinguish forcible intercourse with an unmarried woman from a case described as consensual. They address payments and marriage and can expose another member of the offender’s household to punishment. The Hittite Laws §§187–200 include prohibited unions, and §197 uses place to assign responsibility in a case involving a woman and a man. Its mountain and house distinction is comparable to the city and countryside distinction in Deuteronomy. Neither location by itself establishes consent.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 20:14 prohibits adultery. Deut 22:13–21 sets out procedures when a husband accuses a bride, and 22:22 treats a case involving a married woman. Verses 23–27 distinguish a betrothed woman encountered in a town from one assaulted in the countryside, where no one might hear a cry for help. The law uses location as part of a way to infer what happened. It does not encompass all the circumstances under which a person might be unable to call for help.

Deut 22:28–29 concerns a man who seizes an unbetrothed virgin. It directs a payment and imposes obligations on the man. Many read the term *tāpaś*, “seize,” and the circumstances as describing assault. Others read the provision alongside the case of enticement in Exod 22:16–17 and understand its scope differently. Exodus allows the father to refuse the proposed marriage. The differences in wording and remedy matter for deciding how the two cases relate. Num 5:11–31 describes a separate [[trial-by-ordeal|ordeal]] for suspicion in the absence of witnesses.

Lev 18:6–23 sets out prohibited relations, and Lev 20:10–21 overlaps with it while specifying sanctions for several cases. Lev 18:24–30 associates prohibited acts with pollution of the land. The two lists address a male audience and organize the material differently. Lev 18:22 and 20:13 prohibit an act described in terms of a man lying with a male. Interpreters debate aspects of the formula’s scope and rationale. The legal presentation should be read in its context of kinship, bodily relations, and communal holiness.

## Interpretive Significance

Gen 38:24 illustrates an accusation and a threatened penalty in a narrative with changing information. In 2 Sam 13:1–22 Tamar’s speech and the narrator’s description present Amnon’s action as coercive. The narrative shows consequences for her and her household that a case law may leave unstated. Prov 6:32–35 portrays an injured husband refusing compensation, and Matt 1:18–19 shows deliberation about ending a betrothal without public exposure. These texts give evidence for attitudes and possible responses. They cannot establish that the death penalties prescribed in some collections were regularly imposed.
