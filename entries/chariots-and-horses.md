## Overview

Horse-drawn chariots were an important part of elite warfare and display in the Late Bronze Age Near East and continued in use into the Iron Age, alongside mounted troops. Egyptian reliefs show chariot crews, and Assyrian records name Israelite units and horses. Biblical texts describe chariots in battles, in Solomon's trade and in accounts of royal worship. They also warn against relying on military horses. Taken together, the sources show why horses could symbolize both royal resources and a form of security that biblical writers questioned.

## Archaeological Evidence

A chariot was a light vehicle with spoked wheels, usually drawn by a team of horses. It required trained animals, drivers, craftsmen and continued supplies. The [[kadesh-battle-reliefs|Egyptian reliefs of the battle of Kadesh]] show chariots serving both Egyptian and Hittite armies. The Amarna letters place chariots and horses within the military resources and gift exchanges of Late Bronze Age courts. In the Iron Age, Assyrian reliefs also show mounted cavalry, whose importance grew while chariots continued to be used. [@anep, figs. 336–337] [@moran-amarna, EA 22, 35] [@king-stager-life, 239–250]

The [[kurkh-monoliths|Kurkh inscription]] of Shalmaneser III attributes 2,000 chariots to [[ahab|Ahab]] at Qarqar in 853 BCE, the largest stated chariot contingent in the coalition. The figure is a royal inscription's report and its exact reliability is debated. Sargon II records taking a Samarian chariot unit into Assyrian service after the fall of Samaria. Administrative records from Nimrud known as the Horse Lists also attest a Samarian unit. [@rima3, A.0.102.2 ii 78–102] [@king-stager-life, 239–250]

Rows of pillared buildings at [[megiddo|Megiddo]] contain installations interpreted as feeding troughs and tethering points. They have been read as stables, storehouses and other buildings. The stable interpretation has substantial support, but the case depends partly on how the individual features are understood. The surviving complexes usually discussed as stables are generally assigned to the eighth century BCE, considerably later than the reign of Solomon in the biblical account. [@mazar-archaeology, 379–388] [@neaehl, 5:1947–1949]

## In the Bible

Exod 14:6–9, 23 presents Pharaoh pursuing Israel with horses and chariots, and Exod 15:1–4 celebrates their defeat. Judg 4:3, 13 describes Sisera's iron chariots. The description probably refers to iron fittings on vehicles made largely of wood. For biblical narrators the chariot could summarize the power of an enemy army without requiring a description of its whole force. [@king-stager-life, 239–250]

First Kings associates chariots and horses with royal wealth and trade (1 Kgs 10:26–29). In the Hebrew of 1 Kgs 10:28, a name commonly rendered Egypt has sometimes been connected with an Anatolian Muṣri, and another expression is often read as the place name Kue in Cilicia. The readings affect how the trade route is reconstructed. Deut 17:16 warns a king against multiplying horses, while Isa 31:1 criticizes those who trust in Egyptian horses and chariots. Ps 20:7 (MT 20:8) likewise places confidence in Yahweh's name above confidence in military resources. [@abd, 6:106–110]
