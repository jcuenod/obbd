## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern households and temples treated doors and thresholds as places where protection mattered. In first-millennium BCE Mesopotamia, ritual specialists buried small protective figures at entrances, and Assyrian palaces displayed monumental guardians at gates. In the Hebrew Bible, the Passover story places blood on doorposts to distinguish houses that would be spared, while Deuteronomy tells Israel to write its words on doorposts and gates. These examples show how a boundary between spaces could also mark who belonged inside or what should be kept outside.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

*Apotropaic* means intended to ward off harm. In Mesopotamian rites, small figures representing protective beings could be buried below floor level at doorways or other parts of a building. These included some [[apkallu-reliefs|sages]] portrayed in fish cloaks. Ritual texts describe their preparation and placement. Their locations suggest that protection was maintained by the presence of the objects, even after people could no longer see them. [@ddd, 72–74, 236–237]

Assyrian palace entrances could also be flanked by visible protectors. These include the [[lamassu|winged, human-headed bulls or lions]] known from palaces such as those at Nimrud and Khorsabad. The colossal figures guarded places where people entered the royal complex. Their scale and visibility distinguished them from the figurines concealed in buildings. [@anep, fig. 646] [@keel-symbolism, 120–124]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Exod 12:7, 22–23, Israelites put blood on the lintel, the beam above the entrance, and on the two sides of the doorway. Yahweh sees the blood and does not allow the destroyer to enter. Some scholars have explained the story as incorporating an older protective rite performed by herding communities. That proposal concerns the [[passover-origins|origins of Passover]]. The narrative itself presents the blood as part of God's instructions for the night Israel leaves Egypt. [@abd, 6:755–761]

Other texts use entrances to mark different obligations. In Exod 21:6, the door or doorpost is associated with the rite by which a servant elects to remain in service. A sacred threshold has been proposed, but the location and possible protective function are disputed. In 1 Sam 5:4–5, the discovery of Dagon's broken image at the threshold explains why entrants avoid stepping there. Zeph 1:9 denounces people who leap over a threshold, but whether the prophet refers to the same custom is uncertain.

Deut 6:9 and 11:20 direct Israel to write the words on the doorposts of houses and on gates. The term *mezuzah* originally meant “doorpost.” In later Jewish practice it designates a written scriptural passage placed at the entrance. Texts from the caves near Qumran have been identified as early examples of the physical observance. The written words mark a household's obligation to remember and keep the teaching. [@abd, 4:338–340]

## Interpretive Significance

Gen 4:7 portrays sin as crouching at the door, ready to act against Cain. Some interpreters compare this language with the Akkadian *rābiṣu* (“one who lies in wait”), a term that can designate a threatening being. The association fits the idea of danger at an entrance, but the verse can also be read as a figurative description of sin. The Akkadian comparison remains a proposal. [@ddd, 682–683]
