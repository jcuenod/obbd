## Overview

The Ishtar Gate was the monumental northern entrance to the inner city of Babylon. The best-known phase was built under Nebuchadnezzar II in the sixth century BCE and decorated with bulls and dragons on a blue field of glazed brick. Lions lined the Processional Way approaching the gate, part of the route used in Babylon’s New Year rites. The ruins at Babylon and the reconstructed gate in Berlin give a concrete setting for biblical descriptions of Nebuchadnezzar’s capital and of the city’s religious and political power.

## Description and Provenance

Robert Koldewey’s German expedition worked at [[babylon|Babylon]] from 1899 to 1917 and uncovered foundations of the gate and fragments of its decorated brickwork. The excavated remains preserve stages in which the structure and street were raised. The gate consisted of two successive gateways through the city’s defenses. The Berlin reconstruction represents the smaller outer gate and incorporates excavated fragments and modern additions. The reconstruction opened in 1930 and is housed in the Pergamonmuseum, whose wing containing it is under renovation. [@anep] [@abd]

## Contents

An inscription of [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] credits him with rebuilding the gate and describes its animal decorations as signs of splendor. The final glazed phase is generally assigned to his later reign, in the first half of the sixth century BCE. Bulls associated with the storm god Adad alternate with *mušḫuššu*, composite dragons associated with [[marduk|Marduk]]. Lions along the Processional Way are associated with [[ishtar|Ishtar]], after whom the gate was named. The program therefore honored more than one deity and placed their symbols on a route central to Babylon’s religious life. [@anep] [@cos2] [@abd]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Dan 4:30 (MT 4:27), Nebuchadnezzar boasts that he made Babylon his royal capital. The gate illustrates the scale of the building program and the language of splendor appropriate to the boast. Isa 13:19 emphasizes Babylon's preeminence, and Jer 51:58 predicts that its fortifications will be destroyed. Neither prophecy singles out the Ishtar Gate. The gate gives useful architectural context for the Babylonian setting of Judean court stories, including Dan 1:1–4. [@cos2] [@abd]

The [[akitu-festival|New Year festival]] included the movement of divine images along the Processional Way. Marduk, also called Bel, and Nabu, called Nebo in the Bible, are pictured in Isa 46:1–2 as burdens for animals when their images go into captivity. The contrast between an honored procession and forced removal gives the prophetic image much of its force. Jer 50:2 also announces disgrace for Bel and Merodach, another form of Marduk’s name. [@cohen-cultic-calendars] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

The excavated phases supply evidence for a series of building operations, so the approximate date often given for the glazed front does not date every part of the gate. The Berlin arrangement gives a sense of its color and ornament while reducing the scale of the original double gateway. Later Jewish dragon imagery can be compared with Marduk’s emblem, although a dragon represented on a gate gives no direct historical account of the kind of worship depicted in the Greek addition to Daniel.
