## Overview

The Hebrew Bible names several stars or groups of stars, particularly in the poetry of Job and Amos. The most securely identified are the Pleiades and Orion. Other names are harder to match with the features of the sky. Mesopotamian tablets show how scribes grouped stars, observed their annual appearances, and used them in calendars and the interpretation of signs. This background helps clarify the biblical references without fixing every identification. In Job and Amos, familiar stars help portray a world whose regular patterns are beyond human control and belong to its creator.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Babylonian and Assyrian scholars recorded the appearance of stars across the year. A compendium named *MUL.APIN* (“Plough Star”), after its opening star name, survives in copies from the first millennium BCE. It groups stars, records when certain stars become visible, and lists stars through whose part of the sky the moon travels. Such observations served practical and scholarly interests, including scheduling and [[astrology|the interpretation of celestial signs]] [@horowitz-cosmic-geography, 150–192].

In the late fifth century BCE, Babylonian astronomers developed the zodiac, a system dividing the path of the sun and planets into twelve equal signs. The system adapted features of earlier star lists, whose constellations varied in size and distribution. A reference to recognizable star groups can therefore come from a period earlier than the twelve-sign system. This matters when interpreting biblical terms sometimes translated “zodiac.”

## In the Hebrew Bible

Job 9:9 names celestial groups alongside the chambers of the south. Job 38:31–33 asks whether Job can bind the Pleiades, loosen Orion, bring a group of stars out in season, or guide another with its companions. The Hebrew names *kîmâ* and *kəsîl* are conventionally identified as the Pleiades and Orion. Amos 5:8 names the same two alongside shifts between darkness and daylight. The exact origin of the name *kəsîl*, which resembles the ordinary Hebrew word for “fool,” is uncertain.

The names *ʿāš* in Job 9:9 and *ʿayiš* in Job 38:32 are commonly treated as related. The Great Bear, the Hyades or Aldebaran, and other identifications have been proposed. The companions in Job 38:32 suggest a visible group, but they do not settle the question. Isa 13:10 uses a plural form of *kəsîl* in describing stars and constellations withholding their light in an oracle against Babylon.

## Interpretive Significance

The term *mazzārôt* in Job 38:32 and *mazzālôt* in 2 Kgs 23:5 are often connected with Akkadian words meaning “station.” Proposals include groups along the moon's path, zodiacal signs, and heavenly bodies more generally. The two spellings and their contexts give limited grounds for a precise identification. In Kings the word occurs in a list of objects of worship that includes the sun, moon, and [[host-of-heaven|host of heaven]].

Some later interpreters identified Orion with [[nimrod|Nimrod]], adding a story about the figure marked out by the stars. Job and Amos supply no identity of that kind. Their references work with the regular appearance and position of stars as familiar evidence of the ordered world.
