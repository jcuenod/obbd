## Overview

Dream incubation is the deliberate practice of seeking a dream from a god, often by sleeping in a sacred place after prayer, sacrifice, or another preparation. It is documented in Bronze Age Anatolia and provides a way to read accounts of royal dreams from Mesopotamia and the Levant. Greek healing shrines preserve later evidence for the practice. Several biblical stories place dreams or visions near altars and sanctuaries, but they give different degrees of evidence that the person intended to receive a revelation.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

During a plague in the Hittite kingdom, Muršili II asked the gods to reveal the cause through a dream, an oracle, or a prophet. His [[plague-prayers-of-mursili|plague prayers]] also refer to priests instructed to sleep in a consecrated manner. This is explicit evidence that sacred sleep could be arranged in order to obtain a message. In the Sumerian [[gudea-cylinders|Gudea Cylinders]], Gudea receives a commission to rebuild the temple of Ningirsu and later seeks further clarity through ritual and a dream [@anet, 394–396] [@cos2, 2.155; @cos1, 1.60].

In the Late Bronze Age [[aqhat-epic|Aqhat epic]], the childless Danel attends to the gods over seven days before El grants a son. The preparation has been compared with incubation, although the surviving poem gives no straightforward report of a dream received by Danel. In the [[kirta-epic|Kirta epic]], El comes to the grieving king after he weeps himself to sleep. The scene gives a clear example of a god appearing in a dream, and the king's intention to seek that particular revelation is less clear [@parker-unp, 10, 13–18, 51–53].

In later Greek sanctuaries of Asclepius, people slept in a sacred precinct in the hope of a cure or divine guidance. Inscriptions from Epidaurus record cures attributed to such visits. Healing and dream practices are also documented in later Egyptian religious settings. These sources illustrate the variety of [[dreams-and-dream-interpretation|ancient dream practices]], and their dates and institutional settings must be considered before applying them to biblical Israel.

## In the Hebrew Bible

1 Kgs 3:4–15 gives the strongest biblical case for comparison. Solomon sacrifices at [[gibeon|Gibeon]], an important shrine, and YHWH speaks to him in a dream there the same night. The place and the sequence of sacrifice and dream are consistent with incubation. The narrative says Solomon went there to sacrifice and leaves his intention about the dream unstated.

The other cases are more ambiguous. Samuel lies down at the sanctuary in 1 Sam 3:1–15. The account calls his experience a vision, and he first mistakes God's call for Eli's. In Gen 28:10–22 Jacob learns through his dream that the place where he stopped for the night is sacred. He names it [[bethel|Bethel]] and makes a vow afterward. At Beersheba in Gen 46:1–4, sacrifice precedes a divine message in night visions. The text gives no further indication of a rite to solicit the visions.

## Interpretive Significance

Isa 65:4 condemns people who sit among graves and spend nights in secret places. Some interpreters connect these gatherings with a search for dreams or other communication with the dead. The text names neither a dream nor the purpose of the night stay. The Hittite evidence supplies a useful point of comparison because it states a purpose for sacred sleep. Whether a biblical episode shows the same practice depends on what the character does before the dream, as well as what the narrator says about the reason for being there.
