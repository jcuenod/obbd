## Overview

Letters in the ancient Near East often opened with an address and a greeting before turning to the main message. The forms varied with language, purpose, and the rank of the people involved. Examples survive on clay tablets from Late Bronze Age Egypt, on pottery fragments from Judah near the end of its monarchy, and on papyrus, sheets made from the stems of a plant, from Persian-period Egypt. These letters help explain both the wording of letters quoted in the Bible and the importance biblical stories give to seals, messengers, and the authority of the sender.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Hebrew letters often name the addressee and identify the sender in relation to that person. In the [[lachish-letters|Lachish letters]], a subordinate can call the recipient his lord and himself the recipient's servant, then ask that YHWH, Israel's God, grant the recipient good news. The opening can give way to the main message with *wĕʿattâ* ("and now"). The [[arad-ostraca]] are inscribed pottery fragments that include letters with practical instructions about provisions. The [[mesad-hashavyahu-ostracon|letter from Mesad Hashavyahu]], also written on pottery, uses deferential address to petition an official for a confiscated garment [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions].

In Persian-period [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]], forms written *kʿt* or *kʿnt* ("now") can similarly signal the move to business. The [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine letters]] include a petition about rebuilding a temple, while the letters of Arshama, a Persian governor of Egypt, include directions about estate affairs [@tad]. Much earlier, rulers in the [[amarna-letters|Amarna correspondence]] used Akkadian, a language of Mesopotamia, for international exchanges on clay tablets. Their greetings could express either equality between rulers or subordination to the Egyptian king, depending on who was writing [@moran-amarna].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Biblical narratives sometimes give the business of a letter without quoting a full opening. In the Hebrew account of the king of Aram's letter about Naaman, the quoted message starts with the transition *wĕʿattâ* and moves straight to the request (2 Kgs 5:5–7). Jehu likewise begins his first message to the officials at Samaria with that transition before telling them what to do upon receipt (2 Kgs 10:1–7). Jeremiah's letter is framed differently: the narrative names its recipients and carriers, and the message then speaks in the name of God to the people exiled in Babylon (Jer 29:1–23).

Other stories draw attention to the handling of the letter itself. David sends an order to Joab through Uriah, the very man whose death the letter is meant to bring about (2 Sam 11:14–15). Jezebel writes in Ahab's name and uses his seal, making the letters appear to carry the king's authority (1 Kgs 21:8–11; see [[seals-and-bullae]]). Hezekiah receives a letter from messengers, reads it, and spreads it out in the temple (2 Kgs 19:14). These scenes give the carrier, seal, or physical document a part in the story rather than treating the letter simply as spoken dialogue.

## Interpretive Significance

Ezra presents Aramaic exchanges as part of its account of Persian rule. In Ezra 4:7–22 the writers identify themselves as the king's servants, and the reply opens with a greeting of peace before it addresses their complaint. The letters from Elephantine and from Arshama show comparable ways of addressing officials and introducing requests or orders. For the role of professional writers in such settings, see [[scribes-and-scribal-education]].

Agreement in form establishes that the letters in Ezra use recognizable conventions, but it does not establish that any given letter is an unchanged archival copy. Some scholars understand the documents to preserve genuine correspondence that was edited when it entered the book. Others understand them as literary compositions written in a plausible official style. The question is best asked of each document, since a greeting or a transition could be reproduced whether or not a writer had access to the original letter.
