## Overview

The Nephilim are figures named in Genesis and Numbers. Genesis places them in an early age when the sons of God took human wives. Numbers puts them in the frightened report of the scouts sent into Canaan. Other biblical passages describe peoples of unusual stature and heroes of great strength. Jewish writers of the Second Temple period developed the Genesis episode into accounts of rebellious heavenly beings and violent giants. These works preserve several ways of interpreting the short and ambiguous statement in Genesis.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Stories of exceptional warriors or beings of mixed divine and human ancestry circulated widely. Comparisons with Greek accounts of heroes and Titans clarify possible ways of portraying such figures. Mesopotamian traditions about the [[enmeduranki-and-the-apkallu|antediluvian sages]] likewise offer a comparison for knowledge and unusual beings associated with the time before the flood. Each body of material has its own account of origins and moral responsibility.

The Aramaic Book of Giants from Qumran makes the comparison more concrete. Among its characters it names Gilgamesh and Ḥobabish, whose names evoke the [[gilgamesh-epic|Epic of Gilgamesh]] and Humbaba. This is clear evidence that Jewish authors could draw on Mesopotamian names when developing traditions about the giants. What it establishes most directly is the setting of the later retelling.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 6:4 says that the Nephilim were on the earth at the time when the [[sons-of-god|sons of God]] had children with human women. It refers to mighty people of old, whose fame is remembered, but the brevity of the verse makes the relation among the groups difficult to establish from its wording. Its addition that the Nephilim were present afterward can be read as allowing later traditions to recognize them. Connecting the name with the Hebrew root for falling is possible, though its derivation remains uncertain.

Num 13:32–33 describes the scouts claiming that they saw the Nephilim and felt as small as grasshoppers. The reference ties the Anakim to the Nephilim. The clan identification is sometimes read as an explanatory addition, and the speech depicts the scouts' fear. Deut 2:10–11, 20–21 records names including Emim and Zamzummim for peoples of exceptional size, associating them with the [[rephaim|Rephaim]]. Deut 3:11 describes Og's enormous bed. Josh 11:21–22 recounts the defeat of the Anakim and names cities where some remained.

Goliath's height in 1 Sam 17:4 varies between the traditional Hebrew form of six cubits and a span and the four cubits and a span in a Qumran manuscript and some Greek witnesses. This is a separate account of unusual stature. In Ezek 32:27, a passage about warriors descending to Sheol has wording rendered as referring to warriors of old in the Greek tradition, while the Masoretic text has a reference to the uncircumcised. The difference affects how closely the passage can be compared with Genesis.

## Interpretive Significance

Sir 16:7 recalls ancient giants whose revolt brought judgment. The Book of the Watchers in [[first-enoch|1 Enoch]] 7:1–6 makes giants the offspring of the Watchers and describes their violence. [[jubilees|Jubilees]] 5:1–11 also links the violence and destruction of the giants with events leading to the flood. The Jewish retellings make explicit some relationships left uncertain by Genesis and emphasize how extraordinary power can become destructive.
