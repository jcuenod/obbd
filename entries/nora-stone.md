## Overview

The Nora Stone is a short Phoenician inscription found in 1773 near ancient Nora in southern Sardinia. Its letter forms place it among the earliest Phoenician inscriptions known in the western Mediterranean, around the ninth or eighth century BCE. It appears to name Sardinia and has often been read as mentioning Tarshish. The monument documents early Phoenician activity in the west and supplies a possible parallel to the biblical name. Its difficult opening and uncertain original setting leave the location of biblical Tarshish unresolved.

## Description and Provenance

The stone was found in reuse near Pula, near the remains of ancient Nora. It is kept in the archaeological museum at Cagliari. The weathered slab bears eight lines written in the Phoenician alphabet. Because it came to light outside controlled excavation in its original setting, its date depends primarily on the shapes of the letters and their comparison with other inscriptions. The original place of display and its association with a particular building remain unknown.

## Contents

The text includes the consonants *šrdn*, generally understood as the name Sardinia. The opening sequence is commonly transcribed *btršš*. Some readers divide it as the preposition *b*, meaning “in” or “at,” followed by a place name Tarshish. The beginning of the text is also where some editors suspect loss of one or more lines. The interpretation of the surviving words depends on how they restore the opening, divide the consonants, and connect the clauses.

Frank Moore Cross reconstructed a report of deliverance involving Tarshish and Sardinia and connected the text with a Phoenician expedition. Other readings understand the opening in relation to a promontory or take the inscription as a building or dedicatory text. The proposed functions of the monument remain contested. The visible use of Phoenician script on the island is firmer evidence than a reconstruction of the actions it commemorates.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The Bible uses [[tarshish|Tarshish]] in several settings. Gen 10:4 includes it among the descendants of Javan, describing geography through a genealogy. Ps 72:10 imagines kings of Tarshish and the coastlands bringing tribute. Isa 23:1 associates ships of Tarshish with news of Tyre’s downfall, and Ezek 27:12 connects Tarshish with trade in metals. In Jonah 1:3 it functions as the distant maritime destination toward which Jonah tries to flee. The inscription supplies a possible extra-biblical occurrence of the name and places Phoenician writing in an island setting consistent with long-distance contact.

## Scholarly Issues

If Tarshish is present in the text, a Sardinian location is one possibility. A reading that distinguishes where the expedition had been from the place where the monument was set up can also accommodate a location elsewhere. Tartessos in southwestern Iberia is often discussed because of western trade and the similarity of names. Arguments for its identification rely on more than the Nora text. The several biblical uses may also carry different geographical implications. The inscription establishes the early western setting of Phoenician writing, while the location and identity of Tarshish depend on evidence still open to competing readings.
