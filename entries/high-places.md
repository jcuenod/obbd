## Overview

The Hebrew word *bāmâ*, traditionally translated “high place,” refers in many biblical passages to a local place of worship. High places could contain altars or buildings and were used for sacrifice, meals, and other ritual activity. An Iron Age inscription from Moab uses a related term for a sanctuary dedicated to Chemosh. In Samuel and Kings, some high places are venues for worship of YHWH, while the histories of Hezekiah and Josiah remember them as objects of reform. Their changing treatment helps explain biblical debates over where sacrifice was legitimate.

## Archaeological Evidence

On the ninth-century BCE [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]], King Mesha says that he made a *bmt* for Chemosh. This is the clearest comparison for the Hebrew worship term in a neighboring language. The wording identifies a dedication, while the stone does not specify the form of the structure or make its height the defining feature. Hebrew and related usages have led some scholars to connect the term with an elevated back or ridge, and others stress the function of the place as a sanctuary [@cos2, 2.23] [@kai, no. 181].

The excavated precinct at [[dan|Tel Dan]] includes a large stone podium, altar remains, and associated structures. The excavators identified it as a high place. That is a plausible interpretation of the complex and an example of what a substantial local sanctuary could look like. Individual features such as [[altars]] and [[massebot|standing stones]] appear in a variety of sacred contexts, and their presence alone cannot identify an excavated site with the biblical term [@neaehl, 1:328–330] [@mazar-archaeology, 492–501].

## In the Bible

In 1 Sam 9:12–25 Samuel blesses a sacrifice at a high place and gives Saul an honored place among the guests in a hall. In 1 Sam 10:5 a group of prophets descends from a high place. In 1 Kgs 3:2–4 Solomon sacrifices at [[gibeon|Gibeon]], called the great high place, in a period when the Jerusalem temple has yet to be built. The narratives depict local sanctuaries as ordinary settings for Israelite worship, and the account in Kings explains their use in relation to the absence of a central temple.

The term also describes worship associated with other gods. In 1 Kgs 11:7 Solomon builds high places for Chemosh and Molek. In Num 22:41 Bamoth-baal, a name meaning “high places of Baal,” is a location from which Balaam looks over part of Israel. Jer 7:31 locates the high places of Topheth in the Valley of Hinnom, and 2 Kgs 23:8 mentions installations associated with gates. These locations show that the expression can name a local sacred installation without specifying a hilltop. Ezek 20:29 plays on the sound of the word in a question about the place to which the people go.

Kings gives the high places a central role in its assessment of worship. In 1 Kgs 12:31–32 Jeroboam establishes shrines and appoints priests outside the Levites. Amos 7:9 announces that the high places of Isaac will be laid waste. The accounts of [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] in 2 Kgs 18:4, 22 and of [[josiah|Josiah]] in 2 Kgs 23:8–9, 13, 15 describe suppression. The judgment in Kings reflects the ideal of central worship in Jerusalem. How each royal program relates to excavated closures or alterations is a separate and debated question in the [[cult-reforms-archaeology|archaeology of cult reform]].
