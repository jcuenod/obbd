## Overview

Adad-nirari III ruled Assyria in northern Mesopotamia from 810 to 783 BCE, spanning the late ninth and early eighth centuries BCE. He was the son of Shamshi-Adad V and Sammu-ramat, a queen mother unusually prominent in contemporary inscriptions. His western campaigns compelled Damascus and other states to pay tribute, and one inscription names Joash of Samaria among the tributaries. These records provide a historical setting for Israel's recovery from Aramean domination in Kings. They do not name the unnamed deliverer in the biblical account or establish that Assyrian intervention alone caused Israel's recovery.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Royal inscriptions present Adad-nirari as a victorious ruler supported by Assyria's gods. A slab from Calah, modern Nimrud, lists Tyre, Sidon, the land of Omri, Edom, and Philistia among territories brought into submission. The land of Omri is an Assyrian designation for Israel, retained after Omri's dynasty had ended. The slab also describes the submission of Mari, king of [[damascus]], and the receipt of wealth from his palace. These statements document Assyrian claims to supremacy rather than giving a neutral account of negotiations. [@rima3; @anet]

The Saba'a stele, an inscribed upright stone monument, likewise records tribute from Mari of Damascus. The [[tell-al-rimah-stele]] names Joash of [[samaria]], generally identified with the Israelite king called Jehoash or Joash in Kings. It also records tribute from Tyre and Sidon, placing Israel within a wider pattern of western submission. [[tribute-and-vassalage]] involved payments and obligations to a superior power; receiving such a payment did not necessarily mean that Assyria had annexed the payer's kingdom. [@rima3]

Other inscriptions illuminate the royal household and its religion. The Pazarcik boundary monument names Sammu-ramat alongside her son and records their crossing of the Euphrates during a western campaign. Statues dedicated to Nabu, a god associated with writing and wisdom, by the governor Bel-tarsi-ilumma invoke the lives of both king and queen mother. Their closing injunction urges trust in Nabu rather than another god, but the dedication also situates Nabu among other divine beings. This is evidence of emphatic devotion within worship of multiple gods, not evidence that Adad-nirari instituted a monotheistic reform, meaning exclusive recognition of one god. [@rima3]

## Biblical Portrayal

The Bible does not name Adad-nirari III. In 2 Kgs 13:10–13, Jehoash son of Jehoahaz rules Israel from Samaria, while another Joash rules Judah. The distinction matters because the Assyrian inscription concerns the northern kingdom's ruler, not the Judahite king. The inscription independently supports that ruler's historical existence, but it does not recount the events of the biblical reign summary.

Second Kings places Israel's restoration within the succession from [[hazael]] to his son [[ben-hadad]]. According to 2 Kgs 13:22–25, Hazael oppressed Israel under Jehoahaz, and Jehoash later recovered cities in three victories over Ben-hadad. In 2 Kgs 14:25–28, Jeroboam II restores Israel's border from the entrance of Hamath to the sea of the Arabah and is credited with further northern successes. The narrator attributes deliverance to YHWH, the God of Israel, and divine compassion, rather than describing Assyrian military assistance.

## Historical Assessment

Assyrian pressure on Damascus plausibly helped create opportunities for Israelite recovery. Adad-nirari's western campaigns belong especially to the early part of his reign, with further campaigning in 796 BCE. Joash's tribute is often placed around that later campaign, but connecting individual inscriptional reports with particular campaign years remains debated. The sources summarize achievements rather than supplying a continuous dated narrative, so the date of a monument need not equal the date of every event it records. Damascus's submission should not be confused with its permanent elimination as a kingdom. [@rima3]

The unnamed savior of 2 Kgs 13:5 has often been identified with Adad-nirari, whose attacks could have relieved Aramean pressure. This remains a proposed identification, not an explicit biblical or Assyrian statement. Other interpretations look to Jeroboam II, whom 2 Kgs 14:27 explicitly describes as God's instrument of deliverance, or to Elisha's role in the victory traditions of 2 Kgs 13:14–19. The secure connection is between overlapping rulers and regional circumstances; identifying one inscriptional campaign with the biblical act of salvation goes beyond the direct evidence.

Sammu-ramat's prominence also requires restraint. Her inscriptions show unusual public authority, but they do not establish a fully defined regency or the king's exact age at accession. Later Greek stories about Semiramis probably developed partly from her historical reputation, yet they cannot be read as contemporary records of her actions. Adad-nirari's reign therefore illustrates both the reach of [[assyria]] and the limits of reconstructing events from royal monuments. [@rima3]
