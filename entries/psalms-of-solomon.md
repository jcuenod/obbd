## Overview

The Psalms of Solomon are eighteen Jewish poems preserved in Greek and Syriac. The collection probably draws on Hebrew compositions from the late Second Temple period. Several psalms interpret a foreign conquest of Jerusalem as divine judgment, and many scholars connect their language with Pompey’s capture of the city in 63 BCE. Psalms 17 and 18 look toward a righteous ruler from David’s line. The poems show how one Jewish community could interpret imperial defeat and renew royal expectations before the emergence of the New Testament writings.

## Manuscripts and Date

Greek and Syriac manuscripts preserve the collection, although no Hebrew witness survives. Features of the Greek have led many scholars to propose a Hebrew original. The table of contents of Codex Alexandrinus includes the Psalms of Solomon, but the relevant leaves do not survive. The title associates the collection with the biblical king known for wisdom and songs. It provides no evidence that Solomon composed it.

Several poems are commonly read against the Roman intervention in Judea. A foreign ruler enters Jerusalem and its sanctuary, and the description of a violent end in Psalm 2 is often connected with Pompey’s death in Egypt in 48 BCE. The poems give no explicit personal names. Their dates and the interval between composition and collection therefore remain matters of historical reconstruction. References below use Rahlfs’s verse numbering where a verse is specified.

## Contents

Psalms 2 and 8 represent foreign intervention and humiliation as judgment for wrongdoing. Psalm 17 condemns people who seized a throne associated with David. This is often understood as a judgment on the [[hasmoneans|Hasmonean dynasty]]. It looks forward to a Davidic king who will cleanse Jerusalem, gather a holy people, and rule with wisdom and justice. The poem’s vision includes harsh judgment on opponents, alongside an insistence that the ruler’s strength comes from God. Psalm 18 also looks toward a ruler appointed by God.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The promise of a continuing dynasty in 2 Sam 7:12–16 gives a context for the hope for a son of David. Ps 2:8–9 pictures a royal figure exercising authority over nations, and Isa 11:2–4 connects ideal rule with divine endowment and justice. Isa 66:18–20 offers another setting for a renewed Jerusalem and the gathering of peoples. Psalm 17 brings related motifs into a particular response to the loss of political control. Other poems in the collection also contrast the future of the righteous and the wicked. Dan 12:2 provides a biblical comparison for hope beyond death without requiring identical views of resurrection in both collections.

Mark 10:47–48 applies the title Son of David to Jesus. Luke 2:11 combines Davidic geography with the proclamation of a savior and Messiah. The poems show that [[messianic-expectations|Davidic hopes]] and related titles were available in Jewish discourse before these passages were written. They do not by themselves establish a line of influence between the collection and a Gospel.

## Scholarly Issues

Earlier scholarship often attributed the poems to Pharisees. Some have suggested other groups. The poems show opposition to certain leaders and concern for piety and Jerusalem, but they do not securely identify a sect. The foreign ruler of Psalm 17 is often associated with Pompey, although an identification with Herod has also been proposed. In 17:32 the Greek manuscript reading *christos kyrios* can be rendered “Messiah, Lord.” An editorial emendation reads *christos kyriou*, “the Lord’s Messiah.” Related language in Psalm 18 does not by itself settle the text or its exact force.
