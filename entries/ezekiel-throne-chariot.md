## Overview

Ezekiel's throne visions describe the glory of the God of Israel above four winged beings and wheels that move with them. In the book's sixth-century BCE setting, the prophet first sees the vision in Babylonia, later sees the glory leave Jerusalem, and finally sees it return to a restored temple. Images of winged guardians, sky-bearing figures, and processional vehicles in the ancient Near East provide comparisons for the vision. Its combination of familiar forms gives visible expression to the claim that God's presence can move beyond the sanctuary and return to it.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Ancient Near Eastern artists often combined animal and human forms in images of supernatural beings. The Assyrian [[lamassu|lamassu]], for example, could combine a human head with the body and wings of an animal. Reliefs also show winged figures with bird heads. These images supply a visual vocabulary for the features of Ezekiel's creatures. No one familiar guardian type combines all the features described in the vision [@anep] [@keel-symbolism].

Othmar Keel compared Ezekiel's vision with seals that show supernatural figures bearing a structure associated with heaven, with a deity or a winged emblem above. Ancient processions in which a divine image traveled on a vehicle also show how movement could be represented as an act of the deity. The vision combines a raised platform, composite bearers, and coordinated wheels. The parallels help explain individual elements without fixing a single model.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Ezek 1:4–28, a storm gives way to a vision of four living beings, each with human, lion, ox, and eagle faces. The wheels beside them move as the beings move. An expanse shines above them and supports the appearance of a throne with a humanlike figure. Ezekiel calls the whole sight the appearance of YHWH's glory. The language presents increasingly vivid impressions while repeatedly marking them as likenesses.

Ezek 10:1–22 identifies the beings as [[cherubim|cherubim]] and describes the glory moving to the temple threshold and then above the creatures at the east gate. The list of faces differs in detail from that in the earlier vision. The glory leaves the city and stands on the mountain to the east (Ezek 11:22–23). Ezek 43:1–5 later describes the glory returning from the east and filling the temple in the prophet's vision.

The description is often called the throne chariot. The term evokes the Hebrew *merkābâ* (“chariot”), used for the arrangement of the cherubim and the ark in 1 Chr 28:18. Other biblical visions portray different arrangements around a divine throne. Isa 6:1–3 has winged beings called seraphim, and Dan 7:9–10 gives the heavenly judge a fiery throne with wheels.

## Interpretive Significance

The departure and return of the glory have been compared with Mesopotamian accounts in which a god withdraws from a city and later returns. Examples include traditions about Marduk and the restoration of worship in Babylon. Ezekiel presents a vision of divine movement and makes it part of the judgment and renewal of Jerusalem. The temple can be abandoned and later filled because the divine presence can act beyond its walls [@rinap4] [@cos1].

Rev 4:6–8 adapts the throne-vision imagery, giving each of its four living creatures a different appearance. Later Jewish interpreters developed traditions of study and mystical contemplation known by the term Merkabah. Those traditions attest the enduring importance of the vision and belong to its reception history.
