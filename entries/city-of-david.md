## Overview

The City of David is the southeastern ridge of ancient Jerusalem, south of the Temple Mount and above the Gihon spring. It contains remains from the Bronze and Iron Ages, including major stone constructions, water installations and houses destroyed near the end of the Judahite monarchy. The name comes from the biblical account in which David takes a stronghold and lives there. Excavation illuminates the city's growth and last years under Judah's kings, while the date and identity of particular structures associated with David remain disputed.

## Location and Identification

The ridge lies above the Kidron Valley in present-day Silwan, in East Jerusalem. Its position and access to the Gihon spring made it important for early [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]]. The ancient urban area expanded beyond the ridge, so the archaeological site represents one part of the city at later dates. The term City of David in this context is used for the ridge and its ancient remains. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

## Archaeological History

The remains near the Gihon include large stone walls interpreted as defenses for the spring and an adjacent pool. Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron proposed a Middle Bronze Age date for the spring tower. Radiocarbon samples subsequently raised the possibility of Iron Age construction or renovation. Warren's Shaft, a natural opening connected to Jerusalem's network of water passages, has also been reassessed. The earlier reconstruction as a shaft used to draw water in the Bronze Age has lost support. [@neaehl]

On the upper slope, the Stepped Stone Structure is a mass of stonework laid across the steep hillside. Eilat Mazar excavated thick walls higher on the ridge, known as the Large Stone Structure, and proposed that the two formed part of a single complex built for [[david|David]]. Other archaeologists have proposed different phases or dates across the early Iron Age and question whether the walls belong to one building. The identification with a particular king remains debated. [@neaehl]

At the eastern slope, Yigal Shiloh's excavations in Area G uncovered buildings used at the end of the Iron Age. The House of Ahiel and nearby rooms yielded evidence of domestic life, while the House of the Bullae yielded clay seal impressions associated with administration. Their destruction is generally linked to the Babylonian capture of Jerusalem in 587 or 586 BCE. The site now lies within a living neighborhood, and the sponsorship, presentation and effects of excavations in Silwan are contested. [@neaehl] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Second Samuel 5:7–9 associates the City of David with the stronghold of Zion and describes building activity there. The difficult term rendered “water shaft” in 2 Sam 5:8 (NIV) has often been linked to Warren's Shaft. The reading of the word and the use of the shaft in the proposed period are both disputed. The Gihon appears more clearly in 1 Kgs 1:33–45 as the place where Solomon is anointed. Later accounts of royal water works are discussed under [[hezekiahs-tunnel|Hezekiah's Tunnel]].

Some of the [[bullae-of-jeremiah-era-officials|seal impressions]] found on the ridge bear names that can be compared with officials in Jeremiah, including the figures gathered in Jer 36:10–12. Similar names can make an identification plausible, especially where a father's name or office is also given. A match still requires assessment of the inscription and its excavation context. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]
