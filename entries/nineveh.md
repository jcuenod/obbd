## Overview

Nineveh was a major Assyrian city on the east bank of the Tigris, across from the historic center of modern Mosul in Iraq. Sennacherib made it his capital near the end of the eighth century BCE. Its palaces, reliefs, and tablet collections document the government and culture of the empire. A Babylonian chronicle records its capture in 612 BCE. The Bible remembers Nineveh as a royal city, a target of prophetic judgment, and the place whose inhabitants respond to Jonah’s warning.

## Location and Identification

Nineveh’s two principal mounds are Kuyunjik and Nebi Yunus. The former is the larger mound and held palaces and temples. The latter, whose Arabic name means “Prophet Jonah,” held an Assyrian arsenal and military palace beneath later buildings. Earthworks and excavated gates trace the wall around the extensive lower city. Royal inscriptions recovered on site identify Nineveh and its buildings, confirming the association independently of the later traditions linking Jonah with Nebi Yunus.

## Archaeological History

The mounds were occupied long before Nineveh became an imperial capital. [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] enlarged the city, enclosed it with a wall about twelve kilometers long, and sponsored a water-supply system. His Southwest Palace on Kuyunjik was called the Palace without Rival. Its carved reliefs included a sequence depicting the conquest of [[lachish|Lachish]]. The reliefs put one Judahite city’s experience within the public display of Assyrian royal power.

Excavations begun in the nineteenth century by Austen Henry Layard, Hormuzd Rassam, and others uncovered parts of the Southwest Palace and the North Palace of Ashurbanipal. Thousands of tablets and fragments from Kuyunjik include royal letters, rituals, scholarly texts, and works of literature. Many are conventionally grouped under the [[library-of-ashurbanipal|Library of Ashurbanipal]], although several collections and find contexts are represented. They preserve works such as the Epic of Gilgamesh and reveal the labor of the scholars who copied and organized these texts.

The [[fall-of-nineveh-chronicle|Babylonian chronicle]] describes the capture of Nineveh by the Babylonians and Medes in 612 BCE. Assyrian resistance continued elsewhere for several years. In the modern period ISIS destroyed the shrine at Nebi Yunus and damaged gates and other features. Subsequent surveys of tunnels and excavations beneath the shrine precinct documented rooms and reliefs from the military palace that Esarhaddon expanded.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 10:11–12 places Nineveh among cities in an account of early Mesopotamia. The Hebrew permits different readings of the builder’s identity. The narratives of Sennacherib’s campaign in 2 Kgs 18–19 provide another setting. Verse 18:14 puts Sennacherib at Lachish when Hezekiah seeks terms, while 19:36–37 locates the king’s return and eventual murder at Nineveh. The Lachish reliefs and Assyrian records independently document parts of the political and military background.

Nah 2:1–3:19 portrays the assault on Nineveh and interprets its fall as judgment on imperial violence. Zeph 2:13–15 likewise pictures the secure city reduced to a desolate place. The Babylonian chronicle gives chronological and military evidence for the conquest. Its purposes differ from prophetic poems, which assess the empire and its fall through images of devastation and reversal.

Jonah 1:2 calls Nineveh a great city, and 3:3 expresses its extent as a journey of three days. Archaeology confirms an unusually large city. The literary measurement may express magnitude or include the surrounding area, and there is no need to equate it with the length of the wall. Matt 12:41 draws its comparison from the Ninevites’ response to Jonah. Tob 1:3, 10 places a story of Israelite exile at Nineveh, using the city as a setting for life under Assyrian rule.
