## Overview

Astarte was a goddess worshiped in Syria, the Levant, and Egypt from the second millennium BCE onward. The sources connect her with warfare, royal power, and, in some settings, sexuality. In Iron Age Phoenicia she held an important place in the public religion of Sidon. The Bible calls her Ashtoreth when it describes Solomon's worship of the goddess of the Sidonians. Other passages use the plural Ashtaroth in lists of gods rejected by Israel. Inscriptions and ritual texts help distinguish these biblical uses from the ways Astarte's worshipers spoke about her.

## Sources and Iconography

Late Bronze Age texts from Ugarit call her Athtart. Ritual texts at Emar, farther inland on the Euphrates, also attest a goddess identified with her. In New Kingdom Egypt, texts and images associate Astarte with horses and chariots, reflecting the martial role she could assume there. Objects without an identifying inscription are harder to attribute, since several goddesses share visual features such as weapons or royal dress [@ddd, 109–114] [@parker-unp, 83, 101, 104, 193–196].

The [[eshmunazar-sarcophagus|sarcophagus inscription of Eshmunazar II]] reports royal temple building and names Astarte among the gods worshiped in [[sidon|Sidon]]. In one description she is Astarte “Name of Baal,” a title also found much earlier in the Ugaritic story of Kirta. The recurrence of the title demonstrates an association with Baal in different settings. Its exact meaning in each setting remains open, and it cannot establish a uniform relationship between the two gods across the Levant [@kai, no. 14] [@cos2, 2.57] [@parker-unp, 42].

## In the Hebrew Bible

1 Kgs 11:5, 33 identifies Solomon's Ashtoreth as the deity of the Sidonians. In 2 Kgs 23:13 Josiah makes unfit for worship the sites that Solomon is said to have built for Astarte, Chemosh, and Milcom. The Hebrew reading *ʿaštōret* (“Ashtoreth”) is often explained as a deliberate reshaping of the older name using vowels associated with *bōšet* (“shame”). The Greek form behind “Astarte” is closer to the older West Semitic forms. The explanation of the Hebrew vowels remains a proposal [@ddd, 109–114].

Judg 10:6 and 1 Sam 7:3–4 use Ashtaroth as a plural designation alongside the Baals. The expression can refer broadly to gods and goddesses or to local forms of those deities. In 1 Sam 31:10 the Philistines deposit Saul's armor in the house of the Ashtaroth. These accounts give different kinds of evidence from an inscription commissioned by Astarte's own worshipers. Deut 7:13 also contains an expression for the increase of flocks whose Hebrew wording may preserve an echo of the divine name.

## Scholarly Issues

Astarte and Mesopotamian [[ishtar|Ishtar]] have related names and were identified with one another in some ancient contexts. Their local traditions and functions still differed. [[asherah|Asherah]], known as Athirat at Ugarit, is a distinct goddess with a different name. Astarte has also been proposed as the [[queen-of-heaven|Queen of Heaven]] honored with cakes in Jer 7:18, but the verse gives a title and the identification remains debated.

Older scholarship often treated institutionalized sexual service as a feature of Astarte's worship. Modern reassessments emphasize that texts about attendants and later reports by outside writers do not establish a regular institution. The evidence for warfare, offerings, and royal patronage is more direct.
