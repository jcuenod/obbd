## Overview

The Laws of Ur-Nammu is the earliest surviving Mesopotamian law collection known today. It was written in Sumerian and is associated with the Third Dynasty of Ur, a line of rulers based in southern Mesopotamia around 2100 BCE. Its opening presents the king as a protector of people with little power, and several of its rules require payments in silver for bodily injuries. The work helps readers compare how early Mesopotamian and biblical texts express the duty to protect vulnerable people and how their laws respond differently to comparable injuries.

## Manuscripts and Date

The opening names Ur-Nammu, who reigned c. 2112–2095 BCE, but some scholars have proposed that the collection was composed under his son and successor Shulgi [@roth-law, 13–14]. Several surviving copies are later than either ruler and come from Nippur, Ur, and Sippar. They are clay tablets written in cuneiform, a script made up of wedge-shaped signs. In 2011 Miguel Civil published a more extensive copy on a clay cylinder with no known archaeological findspot.

## Contents

The prologue, or opening section, credits the king with establishing justice under divine authority. It says that he did not hand over widows and orphans to people with wealth or power and that he established standard weights and measures. The laws that follow are framed as if–then cases, each stating a situation and giving its legal consequence. For example, several cases assign fixed amounts of weighed silver for injuries, including the loss of an eye or a tooth [@roth-law, 15–17].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The prologue offers a royal counterpart to the biblical concern for the [[widow-orphan-sojourner|widow and orphan]]. In Exod 22:22–24 (MT 22:21–23, following the Hebrew numbering), God forbids their mistreatment and promises to hear their cry. This is a direct demand on those who might exploit them, not a king's account of what he has done. Similarly, Deut 25:13–16 forbids keeping two sets of [[weights-and-silver-money|weights and measures]], while the prologue credits the king with establishing reliable standards.

Exod 21:18–25 provides for payment for lost time and care after one kind of injury, then uses the language of talion, a response that matches the harm done, in another case. The biblical formula calls for a corresponding injury for the loss of an eye or a tooth, whereas the Sumerian cases prescribe silver payments for those injuries. The later [[code-of-hammurabi|Code of Hammurabi]] prescribes matching injury in some cases and payment in others. The earlier appearance of fixed payments challenges the idea that ancient law moved in a straight line from matching injury to monetary compensation; the written rules do not show which remedy courts used most often.

The Sumerian collection also describes a river test in a case involving a woman accused of adultery; if the river clears her, the accuser must pay. A [[trial-by-ordeal|trial by ordeal]] is a physical test whose outcome is understood as a divine decision. Num 5:11–31 likewise concerns suspected adultery without witnesses, but the woman drinks water under a priestly oath and curse rather than undergoing a river test. The comparison concerns the appeal to divine judgment; whether the biblical rite is best classified as an ordeal or a conditional curse is debated.

Deut 22:25–27 holds a man alone liable to death for forcing a woman pledged to marry and explicitly clears her of guilt. A Sumerian provision also prescribes death for a man who violates another man's bride, but the two cases should not be assumed to define her marital status in the same way. <!-- VERIFY: check Roth's rendering of the woman's status and confirm the provision numbers for the assault and adultery cases across editions. --> The overlap concerns the penalty and the protected relationship, not identical legal definitions.

## Scholarly Issues

The opening identifies the ruler credited with establishing justice; it does not settle who had the collection composed. The differences among surviving copies also mean that modern editions can number the same case differently. The later [[laws-of-lipit-ishtar|Laws of Lipit-Ishtar]] offers another example of royal claims framing specific rulings, but for both texts the extent of their use in courts remains uncertain. These distinctions matter when comparing [[ane-law-collections|ancient Near Eastern law collections]] with one another and with biblical law.
