## Overview

The Temple Scroll is a long Hebrew manuscript from Qumran Cave 11 containing laws for an ideal sanctuary and community. It reworks biblical instructions about temple courts, festivals, purity, and kingship, often presenting them in God’s own voice. The best-preserved copy is later than the likely date of composition. Its detailed prescriptions give evidence for how some Second Temple Jews interpreted Torah and envisioned worship. They also raise questions about the work’s claim to authority and its relationship to the community that preserved it.

## Description and Provenance

The largest surviving copy is 11Q19, generally described as the longest of the [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]]. Yigael Yadin acquired it in 1967 after it had passed through the hands of a dealer. The thin leather preserves long stretches of writing, with damage especially in some outer or end portions. Fragments of 11Q20 preserve another copy, and 4Q524 is sometimes associated with the work. Handwriting dates the surviving witnesses. Many place the composition earlier, probably in the second century BCE, while allowing for stages of formation.

## Contents

The scroll gives prescriptions about the design and use of the temple, the arrangement of courts, people’s access to them, and measures to protect purity. A calendrical program specifies festivals and offerings within a year of 364 days. Comparable arrangements occur in [[jubilees|Jubilees]] and other texts. The legal material also treats rulers, war, and offenses outside the sanctuary. Much of it is phrased in the divine first person, so laws known from biblical books can appear as direct commands from God.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 25–30 gives instructions for the sanctuary, and Ezek 40–48 presents an ideal temple and its surroundings. The scroll draws on sanctuary traditions while proposing a distinctive plan. It adapts Deut 12–26 in material about laws and communal life. The section on the king develops rules comparable with Deut 17:14–20, including restrictions on royal marriage. The calendar and the ordering of the festival of weeks extend practices discussed in Lev 23:15–21 into a larger system of dates and offerings.

Deut 21:22–23 regulates the display and burial of a person hanged on a tree. The Temple Scroll turns related material into an explicit penalty of hanging in particular cases. The wording raises debate over whether it describes execution by suspension, the display of a body, or a practice comparable to crucifixion. The Nahum pesher supplies separate evidence for punishment by hanging. Gal 3:13 applies the Deuteronomic curse in an argument about Christ. The scroll shows that hanging laws were being interpreted in different ways before or around the New Testament period. It is not required as a direct source for Paul.

## Scholarly Issues

Some interpreters place the work within the movement associated with [[qumran|Qumran]]. Others see a pre-sectarian composition subsequently collected or adapted there. Its authoritative voice has prompted proposals that it presents a new Torah, a supplement, or a rewriting that directs readers toward one interpretation of existing laws. Manuscript preservation at Qumran establishes that the work circulated there. It does not show that a historical temple was built according to its plan or that every reader treated it as replacing the Pentateuch.
