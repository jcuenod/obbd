## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern agreements were often confirmed through a ceremony that gave visible form to the parties' words. Some records describe killing an animal or cutting it in pieces. Other accounts mention blood, sacrifice, a shared meal, or setting up an object as a witness. Examples survive from second-millennium BCE Syria and Mesopotamia and from first-millennium Assyrian and Aramaic treaties. These practices illuminate the biblical expression “to cut a covenant” and help distinguish the meanings that animal rites can have in the stories of Abraham, in the covenant at Sinai, and in Jeremiah's account of a broken pledge.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Letters from [[mari-letters|Mari]] use the expression “kill a donkey foal” for concluding a treaty. The phrase reflects a ceremony known in the region. At Alalakh, a tablet records Abban making an oath to Yarim-Lim and cutting a lamb's throat while pledging that he would not take back what he had given. The two examples associate an animal act with an agreement, although the content of the oaths and rites varies [@cos2] [@cos3].

An eighth-century BCE treaty between Aššur-nērārī V of Assyria and Matiʾilu of Arpad explicitly interprets a severed ram's head as representing the vassal's own head if he violates the oath [@saa2]. The [[sefire-treaties|Sefire treaties]] use a similar comparison between a divided calf and the fate of Matiʿel if he breaks the treaty [@kai]. Such acts give concrete expression to a conditional curse. Sefire also uses an Aramaic expression for cutting a treaty comparable to Hebrew *kārat bərît* (“cut a covenant”).

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Jer 34:18–20, people who violated a covenant had cut a calf in two and passed between the pieces. The speech declares that they will be made like the calf. The movement between the pieces is commonly understood as a self-curse, a pledge that calls down harm on its makers if they violate it. Here the prophet explicitly connects the oath-breakers' fate to the animal's fate.

Gen 15:9–18 also describes animals divided in two. A smoking fire pot and a flaming torch pass between the pieces, and YHWH pledges land to Abram's descendants. Abram is not described as passing between the animals. The scene may therefore portray a solemn pledge undertaken by God. Whether the animal act expresses a self-curse, a ratifying sacrifice, or another form of confirmation remains debated.

In Exod 24:3–11, Moses reads a covenant document, applies blood to the altar and the people, and goes up the mountain with Israel's leaders, who eat and drink before God. In Gen 21:27–32, seven lambs witness Abraham's claim to a well. In Gen 31:44–54, Jacob and Laban heap up stones as a witness, swear an oath, and share a meal. Different actions express particular commitments without implying a uniform procedure.

## Interpretive Significance

A text can use the expression “cut a covenant” without describing an animal rite. Hos 12:1 (MT 12:2) refers to a covenant with Assyria and oil being carried to Egypt. The connection with a cutting ceremony is uncertain because the verse reports no ritual details. The meaning of a specific ceremony depends on what the participants say and do, as the contrast between the explicit curse in Jeremiah and the pledge in Genesis shows.
