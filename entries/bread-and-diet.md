## Overview

Bread was an everyday food in ancient Israel and Judah, as it was across much of the Levant. In the Iron Age, households processed wheat and barley and baked dough on heated surfaces or in clay ovens. Cereals formed part of a diet that also included legumes, oil, wine, fruit, and dairy products. Biblical accounts of hospitality, temple offerings, and food shortages assume that readers know how bread is made and distributed. They also use bread and leaven as images of sustenance, scarcity, and gradual change.

## Archaeological Evidence

Charred seeds and fruit remains help identify crops used in the ancient Levant. Animal bones give a different kind of evidence about the use of livestock and other animals. Wheat and barley were central to household diets, supplemented by foods such as beans, lentils, figs, dates, and grapes. [[olive-oil-production|Olive oil]] and wine were important products. Meat figures prominently in feasts and sacrificial meals, but its availability varied by household, region, and occasion [@king-stager-life] [@mazar-archaeology].

Household bread required repeated work before it was ready to eat. Grain was prepared with [[millstones-and-grinding|grinding stones]], and the meal or flour was mixed into dough and kneaded. A *tannûr* was a clay oven in which dough could be baked against a heated inner wall. Other types of ovens and hearths allowed cooks to bake on hot surfaces. A *tabun* is a later Arabic term for a type of oven, and archaeological reports use these terms with some variation [@king-stager-life].

Clay ovens are common at Iron Age sites and were often set in courtyards or other domestic work areas. Grinding and baking were time-consuming tasks commonly associated with women's household labor. Towns could also support specialized bakers, as the reference to the bakers' street in Jer 37:21 illustrates. Domestic installations and specialized production thus existed alongside one another.

## In the Bible

Sarah kneads fine flour for guests in Gen 18:6. At harvest in Ruth 2:14, Boaz offers Ruth bread to dip in a sour liquid, along with roasted grain. The provisions brought to David and his people in 2 Sam 17:27–29 include cereals, legumes, dairy products, and sheep. These passages show different kinds of meals or provisions, from food prepared for guests to supplies assembled for a group.

Lev 2:4–7 distinguishes grain offerings baked in an oven or prepared on a griddle or in a pan. Leaven could be made by keeping fermented dough to mix into a new batch. The departure narrative associates [[passover-origins|unleavened bread]] with the Israelites leaving Egypt before the dough had risen (Exod 12:34, 39). Lev 2:11 excludes leaven from the grain offering burned on the altar, while Lev 23:17 prescribes leavened loaves for a firstfruits offering. In Matt 13:33 the spread of leaven through dough illustrates the kingdom of heaven.

The Hebrew word *leḥem* can mean bread or food more generally. Lev 26:26 and Ezek 4:16 portray food shortages so severe that bread must be weighed out in rations. The women sharing one oven in Leviticus belong to this image of scarcity. Ezek 4:9–15 similarly portrays shortage and hardship through a measured diet and bread made from mixed grains and legumes.
