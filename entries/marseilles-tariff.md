## Overview

The Marseilles Tariff is a Punic inscription discovered in Marseille in 1845. It probably came from Carthage and is usually dated to the fourth or third century BCE. The text identifies a temple of Baal and sets out the charges and portions due for different sacrifices. A fragmentary tariff found at Carthage gives a close comparison. The inscription helps readers understand why biblical laws specify animal types, portions for priests, and the handling of hides. It also gives independent evidence for the use of written rules to regulate offerings.

## Description and Provenance

Workers found the stone during construction in Marseille. The script and language are Punic, the form of Phoenician associated with the western Mediterranean. Its similarity to inscriptions from Carthage provides the main basis for attributing it to that city. The opening line is damaged, and the sanctuary's god is generally read or restored as Baal-Ṣaphon. The object is now in the collections of the city of Marseille [@kai] [@cos1].

## Contents

The text classifies offerings by animal and by ritual category. It distinguishes cattle and other livestock as well as birds, and it specifies sums due to priests and the allocation of portions to priests and offerers. Hides are included in some provisions. A damaged passage prescribes a penalty for a priest taking a payment contrary to the tariff. A related text found at Carthage has similar language and is important for interpreting the tariff's terminology [@kai].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Lev 1–7 classifies offerings and describes which portions are used in rites and which belong to priests. Lev 7:8 assigns the hide of a burnt offering to the priest, and Lev 7:28–36 assigns the breast and right thigh of a well-being offering. Deut 18:3 lists other portions. The Punic tariff confirms the broader practice of stating what priests could claim, with differences in the shares and payment methods. In 1 Sam 2:13–16 the taking of meat illustrates how control of portions could become contested. The tariff's penalty shows another community addressing abuse through written rules.

Lev 5:7, 11 allows alternatives when a worshipper cannot afford a larger animal. The Punic tariff includes smaller offerings, such as birds, in its schedule. The two systems both distinguished offerings of different cost, while their reasons and rules for substitution differ.

## Scholarly Issues

Punic *kll*, *ṣwʿt*, and *šlm kll* name categories whose exact meaning is debated. The consonants *kll* can be compared with the Hebrew *kālîl*, a word associated with a whole offering in 1 Sam 7:9, Ps 51:19 (MT 51:21), and Deut 33:10. Similar vocabulary helps place the texts within related languages. The contexts are needed to determine what parts of an animal were burned, retained, or distributed. The strongest comparison concerns the existence of detailed regulation for worshippers and priests.
