## Overview

The Kilamuwa Stele is a Phoenician royal inscription from Samʾal, the Iron Age kingdom centered at Zincirli in what is now southern Türkiye. Kilamuwa claims that he overcame danger from neighbors with Assyrian aid and improved the lives of his people. The monument combines northern Syrian and Assyrian visual conventions with Phoenician writing. Its picture of the ruler as a parent and protector offers a comparison for biblical accounts of royal responsibility, and its claim about obtaining Assyrian assistance recalls later choices made by kings in the Levant.

## Description and Provenance

German excavators found the basalt monument at Zincirli in 1902 in an architectural setting associated with a royal building. The relief represents Kilamuwa before divine emblems, and the Phoenician text occupies most of the remaining surface. The inscription is usually assigned to the late ninth century BCE. Its Phoenician language is evidence for the use of a written idiom across political boundaries in a kingdom where Aramaic and Luwian traditions were also present. [@kai, no. 24] [@cos2, 2.30]

## Contents

Kilamuwa names earlier rulers and says they did little to improve conditions. He presents himself as surrounded by powerful neighbors and says he obtained help from the king of Assyria against the king of the Danunians. The account also depicts extreme deprivation with the exchange of people for modest goods. Kilamuwa claims that his rule brought wealth and security. He calls himself a father and mother to one of the groups under his rule. The last part of the inscription curses a successor who harms it. [@kai, no. 24] [@cos2, 2.30]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 16:7–9 relates that Ahaz sent silver and gold to the Assyrian king to obtain help against rival rulers. Kilamuwa’s claim comes from an earlier political setting and shows that a small kingdom could represent obtaining imperial support as a royal achievement. The image of the ruler as a parent has comparisons in Isa 49:23, where kings and queens are foster parents or nurses, and Num 11:12, where Moses questions whether he must carry Israel like a nursing child. [@cos2, 2.30] [@abd]

Job 29:15–16 uses fatherhood as an image of help for the needy, and Ps 72:12–14 gives such protection a central role in kingship. Amos 2:6 and Joel 3:3 (MT 4:3) describe human beings exchanged for comparatively little. Kilamuwa’s portrayal of desperation gives a regional comparison for that imagery. The biblical passages use the motif for their own critiques of injustice. [@cos2, 2.30] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

The inscription contrasts population groups whose precise identities remain uncertain. Some analyses distinguish older inhabitants from newcomers, while others propose differences in status or livelihood. The king’s concern for order and good relations among them is clearer than the basis of their distinction. The claims of prosperity and protection serve the purpose of a royal monument, so the measures taken and the extent of their effects are hard to reconstruct. [@kai, no. 24] [@cos2, 2.30]
