## Overview

Pergamon, also called Pergamum, was the capital of the Attalid kingdom in western Asia Minor and later a leading city of the Roman province of Asia. Its acropolis housed major Hellenistic buildings, including the Great Altar, and the city acquired a provincial temple for emperor worship under Augustus. Revelation addresses a congregation there, naming the city as the place of Satan’s throne. Its monuments and institutions provide several possible settings for that image, though the text does not name a particular building.

## Location and Identification

Ancient Pergamon lies at modern Bergama in western Türkiye. The high acropolis overlooks the lower city and the surrounding plain. Terraces accommodated temples and civic buildings on steep ground, while other complexes stood outside the core settlement. The association with modern Bergama is established by continuity of name, inscriptions, and the extensive remains. The Asklepieion, a healing sanctuary dedicated to Asclepius, lay below the acropolis and was approached separately.

## Archaeological History

The Attalid kings developed the city as their capital in the third and second centuries BCE. Their building program included the Great Altar with sculptures depicting combat between gods and giants. Much of the surviving sculpture and a reconstruction of the altar’s western front belong to the collection of the Pergamonmuseum in Berlin. The city became part of the Roman province of Asia after the last Attalid king’s death. Its older sanctuaries and public buildings continued to shape its identity under Roman rule.

Cassius Dio (*Roman History* 51.20.6–8) associates Pergamon with authorization to establish a provincial cult in 29 BCE. Coins depict and identify the temple of Roma and Augustus. Its standing in the [[imperial-cult|imperial cult]] gave Pergamon a visible role in the religious and political life of the province. The city was also known for scholarship. Pliny’s story about the invention of parchment at Pergamon (*Natural History* 13.70) connects it with rivalry between libraries, but earlier use of prepared skins makes a literal invention story doubtful.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Rev 2:12–17 addresses the congregation in Pergamon and commends its endurance, referring to Antipas, who was killed there. No independent source securely identifies Antipas or explains his death. The passage’s language of authority, judgment, and witness makes the provincial cult and the city’s civic and judicial roles plausible backgrounds to the throne image in 2:13. Rome’s presence and local cults were visible in the city’s public life. The letter gives too little information to connect the death to a particular magistrate or temple.

Another proposal identifies the throne with the Great Altar, sometimes called the Altar of Zeus. Its appearance and prominence make it an attractive association, but the text offers no feature that singles it out. The serpent symbolism of Asclepius’s sanctuary has also been proposed. The image of a throne can refer broadly to hostile authority and may work without a one-to-one architectural reference. In 2:14 the author invokes Balaam to criticize eating food associated with idols and illicit sexual conduct. The city’s religious institutions help explain the setting of the accusation while leaving the particular occasions in question unstated.
