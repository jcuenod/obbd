## Overview

A four-room house is a form of domestic building widespread in Iron Age Israel and Judah. Its name refers to a typical plan in which three long spaces opened onto a room across the back. Archaeologists use the arrangement of spaces and the objects found in them to study how households worked, stored food, sheltered animals, and organized daily life. The plan is especially common at Israelite and Judahite sites, and variations occur in neighboring regions. Its prominence has prompted debate about how architecture reflects social organization and group identity.

## Archaeological Evidence

In a typical example, a door at the front led into the central long space. Rows of pillars often separated it from the spaces on either side, sometimes with short walls between the supports. The back room ran across the width of the building. Houses of this type could contain further partitions or lack one of the long spaces, so archaeologists use the layout as a whole when identifying the form.

Features and finds on ground floors point to activities such as food preparation, storage, and stabling. Pillars could support an upper level, and scholars often reconstruct living and sleeping quarters above. Some central spaces may have been open to the sky. The survival of foundations and collapsed walls makes the ground plan much easier to recover than the arrangement above it. The distribution of [[millstones-and-grinding|grinding stones]], storage jars, and installations can help reconstruct what people did in an individual building.

Lawrence Stager associated the form and its clustering in settlements with extended families, the social unit called a *bêt ʾāb* or father's house. Avraham Faust and Shlomo Bunimovitz have related its direct access to ground-floor spaces to an ideal of relative equality among household members. Both arguments rely on interpreting how relatives used the space. Household size, wealth, and arrangements could vary even where builders followed the same plan.

Four-room houses are part of the material evidence for the [[emergence-of-israel|emergence of Israel]]. Pillared domestic buildings also occur in Transjordan and elsewhere, and their relation to the standard form and to earlier building traditions remains debated. Particular sites can yield clues to community identity where house plans converge with evidence such as settlement history, pottery, and food remains. See [[ethnicity-and-material-culture|Ethnicity and Material Culture]].

## In the Bible

A law requiring a parapet around a new roof assumes people use the roof and could fall from it (Deut 22:8). Rahab dries flax on hers (Josh 2:6), and the roof serves as a place of hospitality in the account of Saul and Samuel (1 Sam 9:25–26). The Shunammite woman proposes a furnished upper room for Elisha (2 Kgs 4:10). Proverbs imagines someone living at the corner of a roof (Prov 21:9), and Jeremiah describes offerings made on rooftops (Jer 32:29). Together these texts illustrate the range of domestic uses that a flat roof or accessible upper level made possible.

The woman at Endor has a fattened calf in her house (1 Sam 28:24), consistent with the practice of sheltering an animal in domestic space. When Jephthah vows to offer whatever comes out of his doors to meet him (Judg 11:31), stabling provides one possible background to the wording. The scene ends with his daughter coming out, and the verse leaves the intended referent of the vow open to interpretation. These passages concern building practices shared by more than one type of house.
