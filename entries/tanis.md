## Overview

Tanis, ancient Egyptian Djanet and biblical Zoan, is at modern San el-Hagar in the northeastern Nile Delta. It became a royal center in the early first millennium BCE and reused substantial monuments brought from the earlier Ramesside capital of Pi-Ramesses. Excavations uncovered temple enclosures and royal burials, including the tomb of Psusennes I. Biblical texts remember Zoan as an important Egyptian city. The Psalms set God's signs in the fields of Zoan, while Isaiah depicts the city's officials as representatives of Egyptian political authority.

## Location and Identification

The mounds at San el-Hagar lie near the former Tanitic branch of the Nile. The name and Egyptian and Greek sources connect the archaeological site with Tanis and with the Zoan of the Hebrew Bible. Tanis's position in a shifting river landscape gave it access to the Delta and routes toward the Mediterranean and the Levant. The river network in antiquity differed from the modern one, which complicates the reconstruction of individual routes and channels [@abd].

## Archaeological History

Auguste Mariette and Flinders Petrie conducted major early work at Tanis. Pierre Montet located royal tombs during twentieth-century excavations. Several burials preserved substantial funerary assemblages, and finds such as the silver coffin and gold mask of Psusennes I illustrate the resources of the kings who used the site. Royal patronage in the Twenty-first and Twenty-second Dynasties also appears in the temple precinct, especially in the complex associated with Amun.

Numerous statues, obelisks, and inscribed pieces carry names of Ramesside rulers or earlier kings. Many were transferred from Pi-Ramesses, identified at Qantir in the eastern Delta. The stones can therefore be older than their architectural setting in Tanis. This distinction explains why Ramesside inscriptions led early investigators to identify Tanis with the Ramesside capital. Moving monuments also allowed later rulers to appropriate the scale and prestige of earlier building. The finds must be assessed as evidence for both their first use and their later incorporation into Tanis.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Num 13:22 dates the building of Hebron by comparison with Zoan. The notice uses an Egyptian place as a chronological reference and offers no absolute date for an occupational phase at either site. Ps 78:12, 43 locates the signs of the exodus in the field of Zoan. The phrase can refer to a district remembered for its prominence in the eastern Delta. It provides no archaeological basis for equating Tanis with Qantir or assigning the exodus to a particular excavated phase.

Isaiah describes the princes and advisers of Zoan in an oracle against Egypt (Isa 19:11, 13). Another passage places Judah's officials at Zoan in the context of seeking Egyptian help (Isa 30:4). Ezek 30:14 includes the city among places under judgment. Its continuing place in biblical political geography accords with the importance of Tanis as a royal and regional center. The individual oracles use that importance to make different points about diplomacy and the limits of Egyptian power.
