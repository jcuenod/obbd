## Overview

Nabonidus was the final king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, ruling from 556 to 539 BCE. He restored sanctuaries and gave unusual prominence to Sîn, the moon god. He spent a long period at Tayma in Arabia, while his son Belshazzar and the army remained in Babylonia. His reign ended with the Persian capture of Babylon. Babylonian inscriptions, the reports of his opponents, and a Jewish story about his affliction preserve very different memories of the king. These traditions help explain the royal stories in Daniel.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Inscriptions associated with Nabonidus emphasize his piety and his work on temples. The inscription of his mother Adad-guppi at [[harran|Harran]] represents her as a lifelong devotee of [[sin-moon-god|Sîn]]. It ties the god's favor to the rise of her son and the restoration of his sanctuary. Such accounts articulate the royal family's claim to divine support and are valuable evidence for its priorities.

The [[nabonidus-chronicle|Nabonidus Chronicle]] records that Nabonidus stayed in Tayma while the crown prince and army remained in Babylonia. His residence in Arabia lasted roughly ten years. Other documents identify the prince as [[belshazzar|Belshazzar]]. His responsibilities were substantial, while official texts maintained Nabonidus's status as king. The royal inscriptions and later accounts give different grounds for explaining Nabonidus's absence.

The [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]] represents the conquest as the restoration of proper worship under Cyrus. The [[verse-account-of-nabonidus|Verse Account of Nabonidus]] mocks the former king's cultic policies and competence. These sources express the interests of people writing under the new regime and provide evidence for how the change of power was justified.

## Biblical Portrayal

Dan 4:28–37 (MT 4:25–34) tells of Nebuchadnezzar's expulsion from society for seven periods and restoration after he recognizes the Most High. The [[prayer-of-nabonidus|Prayer of Nabonidus]] from Qumran, 4Q242, tells of a king named Nabonidus afflicted for seven years at Tayma and helped by a Jewish diviner. The two accounts have different afflictions and settings. Their shared concern with a king's prolonged abasement and recognition of Israel's God suggests that traditions concerning Nabonidus are relevant to the formation of Daniel's story.

In Dan 5:2, 11, 18, Belshazzar is called the son of Nebuchadnezzar, a connection also made in Bar 1:11–12. Babylonian evidence gives Belshazzar as the son of Nabonidus. Dan 5:7, 16, 29 uses a designation commonly rendered “third ruler.” The suggestion that this wording reflects Belshazzar's subordinate rank under Nabonidus is possible, although the Aramaic and the narrative setting allow other explanations.

## Historical Assessment

Nabonidus's building inscriptions and his opponents' accounts yield very different pictures of his religious policies. The rejection of the king voiced in Persian-period sources is part of the rhetoric of succession. Isa 46:1–2, where Bel and Nebo bow and their images are carried as burdens, shares a concern with the consequences of a change of power. Its poetic images can be compared with records of cult statues being moved in 539 BCE, while these records describe more than one movement of statues.
