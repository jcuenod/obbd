## Overview

*Lex talionis* is Latin for the law of retaliation: the penalty should correspond to the harm done. The principle is expressed in the biblical formula “eye for eye, tooth for tooth” (Exod 21:24, NIV), and the Laws of Hammurabi from eighteenth-century BCE Babylonia prescribe a matching injury in some cases. Not every injury drew that response: ancient Mesopotamian laws could also require payment, and the biblical collections use the formula in more than one setting. The distinction between a matching injury and another penalty of equivalent severity matters for reading both the laws and their later interpretation.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] (§§196–201), a man who blinds or breaks a bone of another man of the same standing suffers the corresponding injury. The Akkadian term *awīlum* here designates a person of full legal standing in contrast to a lower-status free person or an enslaved person. For the loss of an eye or a broken bone in the latter two cases, the collection specifies payments in silver; payment for the enslaved person is reckoned as a share of that person's value. The remedy thus depends on status as well as on the injury [@roth-law].

The older Sumerian [[laws-of-ur-nammu|Laws of Ur-Nammu]] (§§18–22) already prescribe silver payments for injuries, including the loss of an eye. The sequence of surviving [[ane-law-collections|ancient Near Eastern law collections]] does not support a simple history in which payment first replaced an earlier stage of retaliation. Hammurabi's collection, the [[middle-assyrian-laws|Middle Assyrian Laws]], and the [[hittite-laws|Hittite Laws]] also set penalties for causing pregnancy loss, though they differ in how they assess it. Those cases are parallels to the fine provision in Exodus even when they do not prescribe like-for-like punishment [@roth-law].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Exod 21:22–25, men fighting strike a pregnant woman and bring the pregnancy to an early end. The first provision sets a fine if no harm follows; the next gives the retaliation formula if there is harm. The Hebrew wording is read either as a miscarriage with no further injury to the woman or as a premature birth, with the harm clause potentially applying to the woman or the child. The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation, instead distinguishes an unformed fetus from a formed one, assigning a fine in the first case and prescribing retaliation for the loss of life in the second. The next law, Exod 21:26–27, has a different remedy: an enslaved person goes free if the owner destroys an eye or knocks out a tooth.

Lev 24:17–22 distinguishes killing a person, injuring a person, and killing an animal: it prescribes death, a matching injury, and replacement, respectively. The passage states that the same rule applies to the resident foreigner and the native-born. In Deut 19:16–21, judges investigate a false witness, who is liable to the penalty the witness meant to bring on the accused. The point of comparison with Hammurabi's opening laws (§§1–3) is the liability of an accuser or witness, not an identical procedure [@roth-law].

Deut 24:16 forbids executing parents or children for one another's offenses. Some provisions of Hammurabi instead impose death on a member of the offender's household, as when a builder's son is put to death for the death of a householder's son (see [[goring-ox|the law of the goring ox]] for another comparison of liability). Whether Deuteronomy is deliberately answering laws of this kind is disputed; the contrast by itself does not establish a direct response. Outside case law, corresponding harm also expresses judgment: Gen 9:6 grounds the requital of bloodshed in the divine image, Adoni-bezek takes his injury as a return for what he did to others (Judg 1:6–7), and Obad 15 depicts people's deeds returning upon them.

## Interpretive Significance

The formula ties the severity of a penalty to the harm, but its wording does not establish how often courts inflicted a matching injury. Some interpreters read it as prescribing an actual injury; others understand it as setting a limit or a standard for an equivalent penalty. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.280) describes like-for-like punishment unless the injured person accepts money instead. The *Mekhilta of Rabbi Ishmael*, an early rabbinic commentary on Exodus, and the Babylonian Talmud (*Bava Qamma* 83b–84a) interpret the injury formula as requiring monetary compensation. These texts show how later readers understood the rule, not how every earlier court applied it.

Matt 5:38–42 quotes the rule and then gives examples of not returning harm, including turning the other cheek when struck. The examples address how someone responds to a wrong rather than how judges set a penalty. That shift distinguishes the passage from both the courtroom cases and the rabbinic discussion of what an injurer owes.
