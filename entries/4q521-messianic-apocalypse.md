## Overview

4Q521, often called the Messianic Apocalypse, is a fragmentary Hebrew manuscript from Qumran Cave 4. Its best-preserved passage combines signs of future deliverance, including release for prisoners, restoration of sight, good news for the poor, and the raising of the dead. Its use of Isaiah and the Psalms supplies a close Second Temple comparison for Jesus' answer to John's messengers in Matthew and Luke. The scroll's precise account of the anointed figure's role is debated.

## Description and Provenance

The manuscript is known by its Cave 4 catalogue number. The modern title reflects the mention of an anointed figure and the description of future acts of deliverance. The main comparison with the Gospels comes from fragment 2, column II. The state of the fragments leaves parts of the composition and some transitions between statements uncertain [@djd25, 4Q521].

## Contents

The column calls on heaven and earth to listen to God's anointed and then describes extraordinary acts of restoration. Ps 146:7–8 supplies close wording for freeing prisoners, opening blind eyes, and raising those who are bowed down. Isa 35:5–6 contributes language of bodily restoration, and Isa 61:1–2 provides the wording of good news and release. The scroll brings those themes together with raising the dead.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Matt 11:2–6 and Luke 7:18–23 John sends messengers to ask about Jesus' identity. Jesus answers by listing what they can observe and report, including restored sight, the raising of the dead, and good news to the poor. The combination is more specific than the presence of any one motif. The scroll shows that Jewish readers could already bring together these scriptural signs to describe future deliverance. The relationship among the texts can be explained without specifying a direct line of borrowing.

## Scholarly Issues

The column names the anointed figure near its opening, while the Lord is prominent in the description of the acts. The relationship between God's agency and the work of an anointed agent is open to interpretation. The title given to the text in modern editions does not settle that question. See [[messianic-expectations]] and [[afterlife-and-resurrection]] for other ways these expectations were expressed [@wold-4q521].
