## Overview

Levirate marriage is the custom of a man marrying his brother's widow to continue the dead man's family line. Laws from Assyria and Hittite Anatolia in the second millennium BCE describe related arrangements for marriage within a deceased husband's family. In ancient Israel, Deut 25:5–10 requires a surviving brother to marry the widow under specified conditions and provides a public procedure if he refuses. The custom helps explain why the stories of Tamar and Ruth connect a widow's prospects with the future of her late husband's household, even though neither story follows the law in every detail.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Several provisions in Tablet A of the [[middle-assyrian-laws|Middle Assyrian Laws]] allow a family to arrange a woman's marriage to another relative of the intended or deceased husband (sections 30, 33, and 43) [@roth-law, 167–172]. <!-- VERIFY: individual application of Middle Assyrian Laws A 30, 33, and 43 against Roth --> They address different situations, including a prior marriage arrangement and the position of a widow, rather than laying down a single rule for all widows. One provision of the [[hittite-laws|Hittite Laws]] names a brother and a father-in-law, followed by another male relative (section 193) [@roth-law, 167–172]. <!-- VERIFY: whether the final relative in Hittite Laws 193 is best described as a nephew, and whose nephew --> These texts show that the arrangements could reach beyond brothers, but they do not say, as Deuteronomy does, that a firstborn child is to carry on the dead man's name.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 25:5–10 sets out the case of brothers living together when one dies without a son. The firstborn of the new union is to carry on the deceased's name, a concern related to [[inheritance-and-birthright|inheritance]], although the law does not specify how property is to be divided. If the surviving brother refuses, the widow brings her case to the [[courts-and-legal-procedure|elders at the town gate]]. If he persists, she removes his sandal and spits in his face in their presence, publicly shaming him for not continuing his brother's household. Later Jewish law calls the release ceremony *ḥălîṣâ* ("removal").

In Gen 38:6–26, Judah tells Onan to provide offspring for his dead brother Er. Onan deliberately prevents conception because he knows the offspring would not count as his own. After Onan dies and Judah fails to give Tamar to his remaining son, Shelah, Tamar becomes pregnant by Judah himself. The Hittite provision helps explain why a father-in-law is worth considering in the comparison, but Judah does not marry Tamar, and the story does not say that he was legally obliged to do so.

Ruth appeals to Boaz as a *gōʾēl*, a relative who can act on the family's behalf, and he points out that there is a nearer relative (Ruth 3:9–13). At the gate Boaz links marrying Ruth with [[land-redemption|redeeming family land]] so that her late husband's name will remain associated with the property (Ruth 4:5–10). The nearer relative takes off his own sandal to confirm the transfer. Unlike the sandal removal in Deuteronomy, this action confirms an agreement rather than shaming the man who declines.

## Interpretive Significance

Boaz acts as a relative of the deceased, not as his brother. Some readers call the marriage a broader form of levirate because it preserves the dead man's name, while others see a combination of levirate-like concerns and land redemption rather than levirate in the strict sense. Genesis likewise depicts a family arrangement rather than the public procedure Deuteronomy prescribes. Neither story settles whether the narratives reflect different local customs, earlier forms of the practice, or literary adaptations of a shared obligation.

The duty in Deuteronomy stands in apparent tension with Lev 18:16, which prohibits sexual relations with a brother's wife, and Lev 20:21, which names childlessness as the consequence of taking her. Rabbinic law treats levirate as a specific exception, not a repeal of the general prohibition; the Mishnah, an early collection of rabbinic legal teachings, distinguishes eligible cases from ones where the prohibition still stands (*m. Yevamot* 1:1). The custom is not a general rule about [[marriage-and-bride-price|marriage and marital property]]. In Mark 12:18–27 the Sadducees use the case of seven brothers who all die without children to challenge belief in resurrection, not to clarify how to apply the marriage law.
