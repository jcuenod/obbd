## Overview

Mizpah of Benjamin was a fortified town north of Jerusalem and the place from which Gedaliah governed after the Babylonian conquest of Judah. It is usually identified with Tell en-Nasbeh, where excavations uncovered a large Iron Age wall and later buildings that fit its role as an administrative center. Biblical texts also remember Mizpah as a place of assembly, judgment, and prayer. The proposed identification helps bring together the site's physical evidence and written accounts of Judah's political life both before and after the fall of Jerusalem.

## Location and Identification

Tell en-Nasbeh lies on the ridge route north of Jerusalem, near modern Ramallah. Its position suits a town able to monitor travel into the highlands. Nebi Samwil, another prominent hill northwest of Jerusalem, has also been proposed. Tell en-Nasbeh's known sequence of Iron Age settlement, major fortification, and occupation through the Babylonian period favors the more widely accepted identification.

## Archaeological History

William F. Badè directed excavations at Tell en-Nasbeh between 1926 and 1935. The work exposed much of the fortified settlement. The large Iron Age wall, with towers and a substantial gate, belongs to the period when the town was part of Judah. 1 Kgs 15:22 attributes building at Mizpah to Asa, who used material from Baasha's works at Ramah. The biblical account provides a context for Judah's interest in fortifying the town, while the excavated wall cannot securely be assigned to a particular king.

Jeffrey Zorn's reanalysis of excavation records and finds distinguished a later stratum from the Iron Age town. New buildings and storage facilities belong to the Babylonian and Persian periods. Some jars carry stamps often read as a reference to Mozah, which may reflect production or distribution within a regional system. A [[seals-and-bullae|seal]] from a tomb names Yaazanyahu, servant of the king, and depicts a rooster. The biblical Jaazaniah visited Gedaliah at Mizpah (2 Kgs 25:23). The combination of name and findspot makes the identification possible, though the seal does not name the man's father or the ruler he served.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Judg 20:1–3 the tribes assemble at Mizpah before confronting Benjamin. In 1 Sam 7:5–16 Samuel convenes the people there, and the town also appears on his judicial circuit. 1 Sam 10:17–25 places the selection of Saul by lot at Mizpah. Together these accounts associate the town with gatherings that concern several tribes, beyond its role as a local settlement.

After the [[fall-of-jerusalem-587|fall of Jerusalem]], the Babylonian king appoints Gedaliah, who receives visitors at Mizpah and is killed there (2 Kgs 25:22–25). Jer 40:6–41:18 gives an extended account of his brief administration and the violence that followed. The later stratum at Tell en-Nasbeh gives archaeological context for the survival and reorganization of settlement in Judah during the [[babylonian-exile|Babylonian exile]].

Neh 3:7, 15, 19 associates builders and officials from Mizpah with repairs in Jerusalem, evidence for its importance within the Persian province. 1 Macc 3:46 describes the assembly of Judas Maccabeus's followers at a Mizpah near Jerusalem, remembered as a place of prayer. It illustrates the persistence of the town's association with gatherings.
