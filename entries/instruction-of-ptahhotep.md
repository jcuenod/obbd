## Overview

The Instruction of Ptahhotep is an Egyptian collection of maxims attributed to a high official of the Old Kingdom and best preserved in a Middle Kingdom manuscript. Its aging speaker prepares a son for life among rulers, peers and dependents. Proper speech, patience, listening and restraint are presented as marks of mature judgment. The instruction provides a close comparison for the father’s teaching and advice about social conduct in Proverbs, while its commitment to Egyptian ideas of right order and justice shapes its own account of consequences.

## Manuscripts and Date

The most complete copy is Papyrus Prisse, preserved at the Bibliothèque nationale de France. Other Egyptian manuscripts preserve parts of the work. The text introduces Ptahhotep as vizier, the highest royal administrator, under the Fifth Dynasty king Isesi. It is generally assigned to the Middle Kingdom, although the date of composition is debated. The Old Kingdom attribution sets the teaching in an authoritative past and may reflect an association with a historical officeholder. [@ael, 1:61–80] [@anet, 412–414]

## Contents

The prologue describes the weakening of the senses and strength in old age. The aging official seeks permission to pass his experience to a successor. His teaching is commonly divided into thirty-seven maxims, which include advice for disputes, meals and business. The advice changes with the status of the person addressed. A dispute with a powerful person calls for restraint, while a person with authority must allow a petitioner to give a full account of his case. Fine speech can be found among people of humble status. [@ael, 1:61–80] [@anet, 412–414]

The maxims appeal to [[maat|maat]], the Egyptian conception of truth, justice and order. Conduct in accordance with that order can preserve authority and the prosperity of a household. The closing emphasis on hearing joins ethical behavior to the ability to learn. A son who receives instruction can pass it on, and inherited knowledge continues when the learner applies it wisely. [@ael, 1:61–80]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The transmission of advice through generations recalls Prov 4:1–9, where the teacher received the wisdom he gives to his children. Table manners before a superior in Prov 23:1–3 fit the world of hierarchical etiquette described in Ptahhotep. Warnings about approaching women in another man’s household offer a comparison for the danger emphasized in Proverbs 7. Ptahhotep values excellent speech above a precious stone, and Prov 3:13–15 uses a related comparison with wealth to praise wisdom. The Egyptian description of weakening faculties also offers a comparison for Eccl 12:1–7 and its poetic account of aging and death. [@ael, 1:61–80] [@abd, 2:380–383, 2:394–399]

## Scholarly Issues

The correspondence lies in recurring topics and the form of a father’s instruction. These features were available in multiple forms of [[ane-wisdom-literature|ancient Near Eastern wisdom]]. Clusters of unusual images and motifs carry greater weight for literary contact, as in the relation between Proverbs and the [[instruction-of-amenemope|Instruction of Amenemope]]. Ptahhotep’s treatment of *maat* gives an Egyptian example of why wise conduct might be expected to yield favorable results. The maxims can thus inform comparison with Proverbs, where the connection between actions and consequences is also an important matter for interpretation. [@ael, 1:61–80] [@abd, 2:380–383, 2:394–399]
