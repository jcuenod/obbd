## Overview

Hebron lies in the southern hill country of the West Bank. The ancient mound at Tell Rumeida preserves evidence of a fortified Bronze Age town and later Iron Age occupation. Genesis associates Hebron with Abraham’s family and the cave of Machpelah, and Samuel presents it as David’s first seat of rule. A monumental enclosure of the Herodian period marks the traditional location of the patriarchal tombs. The mound and the later enclosure preserve evidence from different periods, each bearing on how the town’s past was remembered.

## Location and Identification

Ancient Hebron occupied Tell Rumeida, also called Jebel er-Rumeidah, on a ridge above part of the present city. The continued use of the name and the match with biblical geography support the identification. The monumental enclosure over the traditional Machpelah site stands away from the summit of the tell. The site conventionally called Mamre, at Ramat el-Khalil north of the city, is another distinct location. The identification of the ancient town is firmer than the association of any of these later monuments with a particular event or person in Genesis.

## Archaeological History

Excavations on the tell have exposed Early Bronze fortifications, a substantial Middle Bronze wall, and occupation remains from the Iron Age. A Middle Bronze cuneiform tablet belongs to an administrative context, giving evidence of scribal practice at the settlement. Iron Age deposits include structures and stamped jar handles. The archaeological record confirms that the hill was occupied and fortified in several periods. It offers limited evidence for identifying a particular ruler with a building phase.

The enclosure known as the Haram al-Ibrahimi or Cave of the Patriarchs has monumental masonry usually attributed to the age of [[herod-the-great|Herod the Great]]. It preserves the prominence of the tomb tradition in the late Second Temple period. The tombs beneath the building have a separate history from the structure above them. At the traditional Mamre site, a monumental enclosure also belongs to the Herodian era, and later building includes a church associated with Constantine’s period. These sites show how successive communities gave architectural form to the biblical memory of Hebron.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Gen 13:18 Abram settles by the oaks of Mamre at Hebron. In Gen 23 he negotiates a public transaction for the cave of Machpelah as a burial place for Sarah. The account makes rights to land and burial central to the story. The Herodian monument shows that the tomb tradition later received elaborate commemoration. Num 13:22 places the founding of Hebron seven years before that of Zoan, often identified with Tanis. The statement asserts antiquity in relation to an Egyptian city and is difficult to convert into an absolute archaeological date. Josh 14:13–15 associates the place, also called Kiriath-arba, with Caleb.

In 2 Sam 2:1–4 David goes to Hebron and is anointed king over Judah. In 2 Sam 5:1–5 Israel’s elders meet him there, and the account gives his reign at Hebron as seven and a half years. Absalom later uses Hebron to launch his challenge to David (2 Sam 15:7–10). These narratives make the town a recurring center for claims to kingship. The name Hebron also occurs on [[lmlk-seal-impressions|lmlk jar stamps]], which document a later Judahite administrative system. Second Chronicles 32:28 assigns storehouses to Hezekiah but does not name Hebron or describe the stamped jars. First Maccabees 5:65 reports that Judas attacked Hebron during a campaign against the Idumeans, reflecting a different political landscape in the Hellenistic period.
