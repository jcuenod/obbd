## Overview

Meals in Jewish and Greco-Roman settings enacted social relationships through invitations, seating, food service, and the distribution of portions. Some meals took place in households, others in sanctuaries or among members of an association. The same practices could communicate welcome, rank, or exclusion. They help explain why seating and service matter in the Gospels and why the circumstances of eating become a point of disagreement in 1 Corinthians and Galatians.

## Jewish and Greco-Roman Evidence

Formal diners could recline on couches around low tables, although the arrangement depended on the room, the occasion, and the host's means. Pliny describes a dinner at which a host served inferior food and wine to guests of lower standing. His criticism makes sense only if the choice of portions could publicly express the host's assessment of his guests [@pliny-letters, 2.6] [@raff-roman-banquet].

Meeting and dining could serve religious or occupational groups. The Athenian Iobacchoi, a group devoted to Dionysus, regulated speech, conduct, and enforcement at gatherings. The [[tebtynis-salt-merchants-papyrus|Tebtynis salt merchants]] prescribed a monthly drinking occasion alongside trade rules. The [[sarapis-dinner-invitation|invitation from Oxyrhynchus]] names a sanctuary dinner in second-century Egypt. Jewish meals could also express belonging through food and company. The evidence comes from different places and communities, with different reasons for eating together [@agrw-7] [@agrw-302] [@poxy-110].

## In the Bible

Luke 14:7–14 puts choice of seat and choice of guests together. Places near the host could signal rank, and invitations could create an expectation of repayment. John 13:1–30 describes reclining at a meal and uses the action of washing feet to give service particular force. The narrative gives readers a sense of the physical arrangement without requiring a reconstruction of a particular room.

Paul distinguishes several circumstances of eating at [[corinth]]. In 1 Cor 8:7–13 the setting is a temple. In 10:14–33 he distinguishes participation at a cult meal, purchases in the market, and an invitation to a private meal. In 11:17–34 the problem arises within the assembly when some are hungry and others have consumed more than enough. Ancient distinctions of rank and access give context for the imbalance. The passage does not specify an architectural plan for the gathering.

## Interpretive Significance

Gal 2:11–14 shows that withdrawing from shared meals could mark a boundary as publicly as a seating arrangement or an invitation. A meal's setting and participants help explain what eating communicated in each passage. The entries on [[hospitality]], [[patronage-and-benefaction]], and [[greco-roman-associations]] trace related practices beyond the table.
