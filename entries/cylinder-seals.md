## Overview

A cylinder seal is a small engraved cylinder that leaves a continuous design when rolled over damp clay. Cylinder seals appeared in Mesopotamia in the late fourth millennium BCE and were used for many centuries, including under the Persian Empire. They marked containers, doors, and documents, and their images preserve evidence for religious and social life. The Hebrew Bible frequently refers to seals, but its terms generally leave the shape open. Cylinder seals help explain the technology and meaning of sealing alongside the stamp seals that were more common in Iron Age Israel and Judah.

## Archaeological Evidence

The earliest Mesopotamian cylinders are associated with the late Uruk period, when clay tablets and other devices for keeping accounts developed rapidly. Seal cutters engraved designs into stone so that they stood out on clay in reverse. A rolled impression could form a strip longer than the seal itself and repeat the design when rolled farther. Many cylinders were drilled through the center to allow a cord or another support to pass through them. Stamp seals instead left a single design when pressed onto a surface. [@anep, figs. 239–241]

People pressed or rolled seals on clay attached to containers, bundles, or doors. Opening a sealed closure often disturbed the clay, revealing that it had been opened. Impressions on clay tablets could identify the people or institutions responsible for a transaction. Some seals give the name and sometimes the office or affiliation of the person associated with them. The [[persepolis-fortification-tablets|Persepolis Fortification tablets]] show both stamp and cylinder seals still in administrative use under the Persians. [@cos3]

Seals also preserve small-scale images of gods, worshipers, rulers, animals, and mythical beings. In a presentation scene, a worshiper approaches an enthroned figure, often guided by another figure. In a contest scene, human, animal, or composite figures struggle with one another. Many details vary across periods and workshops. Archaeologists compare styles and inscriptions to establish a seal's likely date and place, recognizing that a seal could be carried far from its place of manufacture or used after it was carved. [@anep, figs. 513, 518] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig]

## In the Bible

Job 38:14 likens a change in the appearance of the earth to clay under a seal. The comparison depends on a familiar fact about the material. A seal reveals a distinct design by changing the surface of the clay. Gen 38:18, 25 makes Judah's seal and cord recognizable personal possessions. The Hebrew word for the seal does not distinguish a cylinder from a stamp, and either type could be carried with a cord. [@king-stager-life, 305–310]

In Iron Age Israel and Judah, [[seals-and-bullae|stamp seals]] predominate in the surviving evidence. Jer 32:10–14 depicts witnesses and the sealing of a purchase document, a practice explored under [[sealed-deeds|sealed deeds]]. Exod 28:11, 21, 36 compares the engraving of names and lettering with the work of a seal cutter. These passages show the authority and craftsmanship associated with sealing even where the type of object is unknown.
