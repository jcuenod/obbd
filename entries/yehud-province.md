## Overview

Yehud was the province of Judah under Achaemenid Persian rule. Its governors oversaw a small, largely rural territory that included Jerusalem and its rebuilt temple. The biblical books preserve names of governors, references to districts and accounts of temple and civic affairs. Aramaic documents from Elephantine and stamped jar handles provide independent evidence for provincial titles and administration. The exact borders, size of the population and relationship between the governor and the temple authorities remain subjects of study.

## Sources

Ezra 5:8 calls Judah a province, and the [[elephantine-temple-petition|Elephantine petition]] of 407 BCE addresses Bagavahya as governor of Judah. [[yehud-seals-and-coins|Yehud stamps]] with forms of the province's name occur on storage-jar handles, some with personal names and a title for governor. Their concentration at [[ramat-rahel|Ramat Rahel]] points to an important administrative center, while other finds map the movement of stamped jars. Small silver coins from the late Persian and early Hellenistic periods add evidence about officials and local economic activity. The circulation of objects provides indications of administrative reach, though it cannot define the border. [@tad, A4.7–A4.9] [@neaehl] [@avigad-sass-wsss]

## History

[[mizpah|Mizpah]] was a center of Babylonian administration after the fall of Jerusalem. With the establishment of [[persia|Achaemenid]] rule after 539 BCE, Jerusalem recovered its temple and its central place in the province's religious life. The functions of nearby sites changed over time. The older proposal that Judah was governed as part of Samaria until Nehemiah is difficult to sustain given finds and texts pointing to an earlier series of local governors. Their complete succession remains uncertain. [@neaehl] [@abd]

Settlement surveys establish that the province was less densely settled than late monarchic Judah, with a relatively small Jerusalem. Oded Lipschits's reconstruction places the population of Persian-period Judah at about 30,000, while Charles Carter and other scholars have proposed lower estimates. These estimates use the size and distribution of sites and assumptions about population per built-up area. The results depend on provincial limits, the dating of surface finds and which settlements were occupied at the same time. [@neaehl] [@abd]

## In the Bible

Ezra 5:14 calls Sheshbazzar governor. Hag 1:1 gives the title to Zerubbabel. Neh 5:14–15 discusses the governor's allowance and prior levies. In Mal 1:8, the expectation that a governor would reject a poor gift makes a point about defective sacrifices. The wall list of Neh 3:9–18 refers to officials of districts, and Ezra 2:1–70 organizes returnees by family or home town. The lists give evidence for ideas of local organization alongside the recovery of settlement. [@abd]

Neh 12:26 places Ezra and Nehemiah in a shared setting. A common reconstruction dates Ezra's arrival to 458 BCE under Artaxerxes I, while another dates it to 398 BCE under Artaxerxes II. Ezra 7:25–26 presents royal support for applying divine and royal law. Scholars debate whether it reflects a general Persian practice of [[persian-imperial-authorization|authorizing local law]] or a particular literary and political claim. [@abd] [@tad]
