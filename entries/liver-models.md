## Overview

Clay models of sheep livers survive from Bronze Age Mesopotamia, Syria, Anatolia, and the southern Levant. Diviners inspected the organs of sacrificed animals for signs and used some models to record or teach the interpretation of markings and shapes. Models with Akkadian writing from Hazor and examples from Megiddo show that the practice was familiar in Canaan. Ezekiel's description of the king of Babylon inspecting a liver is the clearest biblical reference. The objects help readers understand the specialized knowledge assumed by that scene.

## Archaeological Evidence

The inspection of entrails for signs is called [[extispicy]], and the examination of the liver specifically is sometimes called hepatoscopy. Clay models mark features of the organ and can connect a particular feature or irregularity with an ominous result. Old Babylonian examples from [[mari|Mari]] and Babylonia use different schemes for representing the organ. Some carry extensive writing, and others mark features without an inscription. These differences suggest more than one teaching or recording purpose.

Several models found at [[hazor|Hazor]] are inscribed in Akkadian. Archaeological context and writing place examples in the Middle Bronze Age and around the transition into the Late Bronze Age. Uninscribed models from [[megiddo|Megiddo]] and examples from Ugarit and Anatolia broaden the comparison. The objects make clear that knowledge of liver divination reached Canaan. The exact extent of local practice, and the proportion of imported and locally made objects, are separate questions [@anep, fig. 595] [@neaehl, 2:596–599].

A much later bronze model from Piacenza in Italy marks divisions with Etruscan divine names. The similarities make it a useful comparison for the way a model could organize divinatory knowledge, while its date and context distinguish it from the Bronze Age finds.

## In the Bible

In Ezek 21:21 (MT 21:26), the king of Babylon uses several inquiries at a crossroads, including inspecting a liver. The narrative places the practices together as ways to decide between alternatives. The archaeological evidence shows how one part of that inquiry could have had a specialized technical tradition. Deut 18:10–14 condemns a range of practices for seeking hidden knowledge without listing liver inspection by name. Num 24:1 says Balaam no longer goes to seek omens, leaving the technique unspecified.

Some scholars see a further connection in Lev 3:4, 10, 15, where a part associated with the liver is burned with other portions of a sacrifice. One proposal is that removing the part discouraged divination. The passage gives rules for the offering and states no such purpose. In 2 Kgs 16:15 Ahaz sets apart the bronze altar for his own use, expressed by a difficult Hebrew verb that can suggest inquiry. Extispicy is one proposed interpretation. Neither passage describes the inspection of a liver explicitly.
