## Overview

The Arameans were peoples of Syria and upper Mesopotamia whose kingdoms became prominent in the early first millennium BCE. Damascus was the Aramean kingdom with the closest ties to Israel and Judah. Other states governed parts of northern Syria and the middle and upper Euphrates regions. Assyria incorporated many of these kingdoms, while Aramaic spread far beyond their territories. Biblical accounts portray the Arameans both as neighboring rivals of Israel and as relatives of Israel's ancestors. Inscriptions from the Aramean states and their neighbors help explain the different settings of these traditions.

## Sources

The first generally accepted Assyrian reference that explicitly names Arameans comes from the reign of Tiglath-pileser I, around 1100 BCE. His inscriptions recount campaigns against groups he calls Aramean Ahlamu. Earlier texts mention Ahlamu or names resembling Aram, but their identification with the Arameans of the Iron Age is uncertain. Royal annals tell the story from the viewpoint of the king who commissioned them and tend to emphasize victory [@rima2, A.0.101.1].

Inscriptions from Syria add local perspectives. The [[tell-fekheriye-statue|Tell Fekheriye statue]] from the upper Khabur region carries both Akkadian and Aramaic versions of a dedication. The [[sefire-treaties|Sefire treaties]] preserve agreements involving Arpad. The [[zakkur-stele|Zakkur Stele]] records conflict between kingdoms in Syria, and the [[tel-dan-stele|Tel Dan Stele]] is usually attributed to a king of Damascus [@kai, nos. 222–224, 309] [@cos2, 2.82].

## History

The kingdoms existed alongside one another and had different political histories. Bit-Adini commanded territory around the middle Euphrates, while Bit-Agusi was centered on Arpad in northern Syria. Guzana lay near the upper Khabur, and Damascus controlled a kingdom farther south. Zobah appears as another power in the David narratives, though its geography is less secure. Sam'al in the northwest shows the interaction of Aramaic-speaking and Luwian-speaking groups. Luwian was a language of Anatolia, also used in inscriptions in parts of Iron Age Syria.

Assyrian expansion proceeded in stages. Shalmaneser III subdued Bit-Adini in the ninth century BCE. The armies of Tiglath-pileser III took Arpad and later Damascus in the eighth century. The fall of their capitals ended local dynasties or reduced their independence. The [[aramaic-language|Aramaic language]] continued to spread, eventually becoming a means of administration and communication across the Assyrian and Persian empires [@rima3, A.0.102.14 lines 30–43] [@rinap1, nos. 20–21, 35, 42].

## In the Bible

In 2 Sam 8:3–12, David fights Hadadezer of Zobah, and Arameans from Damascus come to Hadadezer's aid. The books of Kings portray different stages of the long rivalry with Damascus. The account in 1 Kgs 20 concerns Ahab's wars with [[ben-hadad|Ben-hadad]]. In 2 Kgs 13:3–7, 22–25 the kings of Israel lose territory under [[hazael|Hazael]] and his son before recovering some towns. Assyrian inscriptions independently establish that Hazael ruled at Damascus and fielded forces against Assyria [@rima3, A.0.102.14 lines 92–102].

Genesis calls Rebekah's father Bethuel an Aramean of Paddan-aram (Gen 25:20). The firstfruits confession in Deut 26:5 describes Israel's ancestor as an Aramean. Its wording can be understood as describing him as wandering or as perishing or in danger, and interpreters differ about the force of the expression. The traditions establish that biblical writers remembered a kinship with Aram. Whether the term in the ancestral stories reflects the time in which they are set or the language of later Israelite storytellers remains debated.
