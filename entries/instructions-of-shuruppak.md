## Overview

The Instructions of Shuruppak is a Sumerian collection of a father’s advice to his son. Surviving copies from the middle of the third millennium BCE make it one of the oldest known literary compositions. A version copied in the early second millennium BCE names the son as Ziusudra, the hero of a Sumerian flood story. The advice concerns obligations, household conduct, speech and practical dangers. Its combination of short maxims and a parental teaching frame supplies an early comparison for similar literary forms in Proverbs.

## Manuscripts and Date

Early Dynastic tablets from Abu Salabikh and Adab preserve shorter forms of the work. The Abu Salabikh manuscript belongs to the middle of the third millennium BCE. Old Babylonian copies from the early second millennium preserve a longer version with a fuller literary frame. The composition continued to circulate in scribal settings, and fragments of an Akkadian version survive. The surviving copies provide evidence of the period in which a form of the text circulated. Its earliest oral or written formulation cannot be dated precisely. [@cos1, 1.176] [@lambert-bwl, 92–95]

## Contents

The longer Sumerian text presents Shuruppak as the son of Ubara-Tutu and as father of Ziusudra. The father repeatedly calls his son to attention and gives advice on choices such as where to build, when to travel and whether to enter a quarrel. A warning about dishonest speech gives a possible consequence, as does the advice to avoid guaranteeing someone else’s obligations. References to debt, theft, agriculture and family relationships place the sayings in the daily life of households. Other admonitions stress the value of instruction from a father, a mother or siblings. [@cos1, 1.176]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Prov 1:8 asks the pupil to hear the father’s instruction and attend to the mother’s teaching. Prov 4:1–4 places the speaker in a chain of transmission from one generation to the next. Shuruppak’s advice against vouching for others resembles the counsel about assuming liability for another’s debt in Prov 6:1–5. Prov 6:24–29 and the Sumerian warnings about misconduct with a married woman share concern about the consequences of sexual behavior, with differing motives and imagery. Comparable instruction frames appear in Egyptian works such as the [[instruction-of-ptahhotep|Instruction of Ptahhotep]] and in the Aramaic tradition of [[ahiqar|Ahiqar]]. [@cos1, 1.176] [@tad, C1.1] [@abd, 6:920–932]

## Scholarly Issues

The expanded text places practical counsel in the distant era before the flood. Ziusudra’s place in the [[eridu-genesis|Sumerian flood narrative]] lends authority to the transmission of ancient knowledge, but this association is explicit in the later form and should not be assumed for the oldest witnesses. The variants show how scribes could reshape the material while retaining the idea of wisdom passed from father to son. The family setting and advice about familiar problems are evidence of widespread literary forms and practical concerns. No specific chain of transmission to Proverbs has been established. [@cos1, 1.176] [@lambert-bwl, 92–95]
