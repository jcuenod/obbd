## Overview

Seraphim are the six-winged attendants of God’s throne in Isaiah 6. Their name is related to the Hebrew word used for fiery serpents elsewhere. Winged cobras appear in Egyptian royal imagery and on Iron Age Levantine seals, providing a possible visual background for Isaiah’s beings. Many scholars understand the seraphim as serpent-like, although the vision describes their wings and actions without specifying their entire shape. Comparing the texts and images helps explain how a threatening creature could also serve within a depiction of divine kingship.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Egyptian *uraeus* is an upright cobra often shown on a crown or above a royal figure. It marks royal power and can represent the protection of a deity, with the cobra’s venom or fire threatening opponents. Some versions add wings spread to shelter a ruler or sacred space. Adaptations reached the Levant through exchange and local production, so the same motif could appear with different meanings in different settings.

Some Iron Age seals and impressions from Israel and Judah depict winged cobras, including four-winged examples. They are material evidence that the motif was available in places relevant to Isaiah. Their images do not consistently supply the same number of wings or the same actions as the biblical description. Readers can compare the combination of a serpent-like figure, wings, and royal protection without reconstructing a single object that the prophet must have seen.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 6:2–7 describes beings above the enthroned Lord. Each has six wings, used to cover its face and feet and to fly. Their calls proclaim holiness and fill the setting with sound and smoke. One seraph takes a burning coal from the altar and touches Isaiah’s lips. The gesture addresses the prophet’s fear that his lips are unclean. The passage locates the beings in a throne and temple setting and gives them a role in the encounter, while saying nothing explicit about scales or a serpent body.

Num 21:6–9 describes the serpents whose bites afflict Israel and the bronze object erected when Moses intercedes. The Hebrew text uses a form of *śārāp* in this episode. Deut 8:15 similarly mentions a fiery serpent among the wilderness dangers. Isa 14:29 and 30:6 refer to a flying fiery serpent in poetic contexts. The root is associated with burning, which may evoke the effect of a bite or the character of the creature. These uses strengthen the possibility of a serpent image in Isaiah 6.

## Interpretive Significance

A common reading connects the seraphim with the visual tradition of protective winged cobras. A different interpretation understands them more generally as fiery beings. The combination of the vocabulary and Levantine iconography makes the serpent comparison strong, while the absence of a bodily description leaves some uncertainty. Isaiah’s six wings and the actions assigned to them belong to the vision’s own design. The images help readers see how royal protection, danger, and reverence could converge, even though the vision gives the attendants functions beyond those visible on seals.
