## Overview

Circumcision is the removal of part or all of the foreskin. Ancient Egyptian evidence for the practice reaches back to the third millennium BCE. Biblical writers also associate circumcision with several peoples in the eastern Mediterranean, but give it a particular place in Israel as a sign of the covenant with Abraham to be performed on male infants. Other biblical stories place it in settings of marriage, danger or military preparation. These differing scenes show that the same bodily practice could express family ties, religious obligations or boundaries between peoples.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

A relief in the Sixth Dynasty tomb of Ankhmahor at Saqqara is often interpreted as showing circumcision. A stela of a man named Uha, conventionally dated to the First Intermediate Period, is usually read as referring to his participation in a group circumcision. Both identifications have been discussed, and the evidence leaves questions about how the procedure was performed and how widely it was practiced. The Egyptian examples commonly discussed concern people beyond infancy. [@anep, fig. 629] [@abd, 1:1025–1031]

Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, includes the Syrians of Palestine among peoples whom he understood to have learned circumcision from Egypt. His account gives an ancient explanation for the distribution of the practice. It cannot establish who first performed it or how it moved between communities. Secure evidence for the practice in ancient Mesopotamia is lacking, and biblical writers characterize the [[philistines|Philistines]] as uncircumcised. [@abd, 1:1025–1031]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 17:9–14 makes circumcision the sign of the covenant between God and Abraham and specifies the eighth day of life for boys. The practice includes males born in the household and those acquired from outside it. Its regular performance in infancy therefore marks membership in the household and covenant from an early age. Gen 34:13–24, by contrast, portrays it as a condition for intermarriage with the people of Shechem, and Exod 4:24–26 connects an emergency circumcision with Zipporah's difficult expression about blood and kinship. Some scholars regard these scenes as preserving older associations with marriage, though the setting and purpose of the rite in Exodus are disputed.

Josh 5:2–9 describes the circumcision of the generation born during the wilderness journey before Israel proceeds from Gilgal. Yahweh then says that Egypt's reproach has been removed. Proposed interpretations connect the reproach with slavery in Egypt, with an Egyptian taunt, or with Israel's uncircumcised condition. The narrative does not define it explicitly. In Judg 14:3 the description of the Philistines as uncircumcised helps express an objection to marriage across a group boundary.

## Interpretive Significance

Jer 9:25–26 (MT 9:24–25) groups Judah with several peoples who knew circumcision. The difficult Hebrew opening has been understood as a reference to people physically circumcised who nevertheless count as uncircumcised. The contrast with Israel's uncircumcised heart makes the bodily sign by itself insufficient. Ezek 32:19 reverses the point rhetorically by assigning Egypt a place among the uncircumcised dead, a dishonor in the context of the lament. [@abd, 1:1025–1031]
