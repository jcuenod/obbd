## Overview

Assyrian eponym lists record the officials after whom the Assyrians named successive years. Copies from the first millennium BCE together preserve a continuous sequence from 910 to 649 BCE, and some also record events such as campaigns and eclipses. The record of a solar eclipse in the year assigned to Bur-Saggile can be matched with an eclipse in 763 BCE. This match allows scholars to date many Assyrian inscriptions precisely, including accounts that name the Israelite rulers Ahab and Jehu or describe campaigns mentioned in the books of Kings.

## Manuscripts and Date

Assyrian scribes wrote the lists in wedge-shaped characters on clay. Copies from several sites overlap, so a name missing in one copy can sometimes be supplied from another. Eponym dating was already in use in earlier periods, including among Old Assyrian merchants in the early second millennium BCE. The continuous Neo-Assyrian series from 910 to 649 BCE is a reconstruction from the surviving lists. Other dated documents name later officials through the end of the empire, although some of those names are harder to put in order.

## Contents

An eponym is a person whose name is given to something else, in this case a year. The Assyrian term was *limmu*. A reigning king could serve as eponym, as could governors and senior court or military officials. A document dated in the year of a named person could therefore be assigned a place in the sequence even when it gave no regnal year.

Lists that give short notices in addition to names are commonly called the Eponym Chronicle. The surviving event notices cover only part of the full sequence. They record events such as royal campaigns, revolts, and an eclipse. Their terse wording contrasts with the fuller claims made in [[royal-annals-and-historiography|royal annals]] about what happened on a campaign [@glassner-chronicles].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The notice for Bur-Saggile's year records an eclipse of the sun. Its identification with the eclipse of 763 BCE gives a calendar date to that entry. Counting backward and forward through the ordered list gives dates to other entries, which can then be combined with the reigns and campaign years in royal inscriptions.

The Assyrian king Shalmaneser III names Ahab among the opponents at the [[battle-of-qarqar|battle of Qarqar]] in 853 BCE. He also reports receiving tribute from Jehu in a campaign dated to 841 BCE. These are among the earliest firm calendar dates that can be tied to named rulers of Israel [@rima3]. The chronicle lists campaigns to Damascus in 733 and 732 BCE, which contextualize 2 Kgs 16:9 and 15:29 without restating the details of those verses [@rinap1]. The same dating framework places Sennacherib's campaign in 2 Kgs 18:13 in 701 BCE [@rinap3-1].

## Scholarly Issues

Assyrian dates give an external check on the [[chronology-of-kings|chronology of Israel and Judah]]. The lengths of reign and synchronisms within Kings can be difficult to reconcile, and different reconstructions make different allowances for overlapping reigns or calendar conventions. The external dates constrain those reconstructions without resolving every biblical chronological problem.

Amos 8:9 pictures the sun setting at noon and the earth darkening in daylight. Some readers have linked the image to the eclipse of 763 BCE. The verse presents it as future divine judgment and provides no date by which to identify a particular eclipse. The suggested association remains open.
