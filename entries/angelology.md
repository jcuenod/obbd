## Overview

Angelology is the study of ideas about angels, including their names, ranks and roles. Jewish texts of the Persian and Hellenistic periods give more detail about the organization of heaven than earlier biblical narratives usually do. Daniel names heavenly figures associated with visions and nations, while Tobit and 1 Enoch describe groups close to God. The New Testament uses several of these names and roles. The evidence points to several ways of imagining the heavenly realm, developing through scriptural interpretation and contact with other traditions.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian gods had attendants who carried messages or represented them in the divine court. One title was *sukkallu* (“minister” or “vizier”), used of figures such as Nusku. Pairs of divine messengers also appear in Ugaritic literature. These roles illustrate how ancient writers imagined divine officials, while the Jewish lists of names and offices require more specific explanation. [@ddd] [@parker-unp]

Some scholars have compared the seven angels near God in Tobit with the beneficent beings of [[zoroastrianism|Iranian religious tradition]], called the *Aməša Spəntas* (“Bounteous Immortals”). A connection between the two groups is debated because similarity in number gives little indication of a route of transmission or exact correspondence. A more specific linguistic connection is Tobit's demon Asmodeus (Tob 3:8), whose name is generally traced to Iranian Aēšma, a figure associated with wrath. The case for that name is stronger than the case for deriving an entire angelic hierarchy from Iran. [@ddd]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Named and ranked angels become prominent in later Jewish literature. Earlier stories often describe their figures by role, as with the [[angel-of-yhwh|angel of Yahweh]] who declines to give his name in Judg 13:17–18. Zech 1:9–14 advances the picture of heaven by portraying an angel who interprets what the prophet sees and another exchange involving Yahweh's messenger. The world of these visions is closely related to the wider biblical image of a [[divine-council|divine council]].

Gabriel interprets visions in Dan 8:16 and 9:21. In Dan 10:13, 20–21, Michael contends with heavenly figures linked with Persia and Greece and is called the prince associated with Daniel's people. Dan 12:1 again presents him as their protector. Tob 12:15 instead identifies Raphael as one of seven angels who approach God's presence. The two works give these named figures different duties. [@abd]

## Interpretive Significance

[[first-enoch|First Enoch]] includes groups of four in its ninth and fortieth chapters and a longer list associated with seven in its twentieth chapter. The names and their forms vary among passages and textual witnesses. The [[war-scroll|War Scroll]] from Qumran assigns the names Michael, Gabriel, Sariel and Raphael to the shields of the towers in a future battle, with some wording reconstructed from damaged text. These lists reflect overlapping traditions with room for variation. [@abd]

Saul Olyan argues that many names and classes can be explained by interpreting biblical expressions. The Jerusalem Talmud, in tractate Rosh Hashanah 1:2, also preserves a tradition associating the names of angels with Babylonia. The statement belongs to a later period and expresses a recollection about origins. In the New Testament, Gabriel appears as God's messenger in Luke 1:19, 26, while Jude 9 calls Michael an archangel and Rev 12:7–9 gives him command in a heavenly battle.
