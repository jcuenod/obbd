## Overview

Nebuchadnezzar II ruled the Neo-Babylonian Empire from 605 to 562 BCE. He defeated Egypt at Carchemish as crown prince, captured Jerusalem in 597 BCE, and presided over the campaign that destroyed the city and temple a decade later. His inscriptions chiefly record building work, including projects that transformed Babylon. Biblical writers variously portray him as a conqueror, the agent of God's judgment, and a ruler compelled to recognize divine power. Those perspectives can be compared with contemporary chronicles and inscriptions.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The Akkadian name *Nabû-kudurrī-uṣur* invokes [[nabu|Nabû]] to protect an heir. The biblical form Nebuchadrezzar, found especially in Jeremiah and Ezekiel, retains a consonant closer to the Akkadian form than the familiar variant Nebuchadnezzar. Records of his reign include chronicles, royal inscriptions, and administrative texts.

The [[nebuchadnezzar-chronicle|Nebuchadnezzar Chronicle]] describes the defeat of Egypt at [[battle-of-carchemish|Carchemish]], his accession after Nabopolassar's death, and the capture of Jerusalem in 597 BCE. Ration documents later attest Jehoiachin and members of his household in Babylonia. Many of Nebuchadnezzar's own surviving inscriptions commemorate construction. His building projects at [[babylon|Babylon]] included walls, temples, and the [[ishtar-gate|Ishtar Gate]]. Like other ancient royal texts, the inscriptions represent the king's achievements in terms of obligations to the gods.

## Biblical Portrayal

2 Kgs 24:1–17 recounts Jehoiakim's submission and revolt against Babylon, followed by the capture of Jerusalem, Jehoiachin's deportation, and the installation of Zedekiah. 2 Kgs 25:1–21 describes the later siege, the destruction of the temple, and further deportation. These episodes form the immediate background for the [[babylonian-exile|Babylonian exile]]. Jer 46:2 links the king's rise to the Egyptian defeat at Carchemish. Jer 27:6 calls him God's servant, giving Babylonian rule a place within the prophet's account of divine action.

Ezek 26:7–14 describes Nebuchadnezzar's attack on Tyre. Ezek 29:17–20 acknowledges that the long effort against Tyre yielded no reward for the Babylonian army and presents Egypt as compensation. [[josephus|Josephus]] also reports a siege of Tyre lasting thirteen years, drawing on accounts of Phoenician history (Ag. Ap. 1.156). These sources illuminate the scale of the campaign and the difference between mounting a siege and taking a wealthy city.

## Historical Assessment

Dan 1:1–2 places a siege in the third year of Jehoiakim. The surviving Babylonian chronicle records western activity around that time but specifically describes the capture of Jerusalem in 597 BCE. The proposed earlier siege lacks comparable external attestation. Much of Nebuchadnezzar's later military history has to be pieced together because the chronicle is incomplete and royal building inscriptions have different priorities.

Dan 4:28–33 (MT 4:25–30) uses the king's boast over Babylon to introduce his downfall. The historical building program helps explain the setting. The motif of a Babylonian king suffering a prolonged affliction is also associated with [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] in a Qumran text, 4Q242. Scholars commonly compare the two traditions. Their different names and settings are evidence of how royal stories were reshaped as they circulated.
