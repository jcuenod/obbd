## Overview

Athtar was a male deity worshiped in parts of Syria, the Levant, and southern Arabia. His name is related to those of Astarte and Ishtar, and he is commonly associated with the planet Venus. A Late Bronze Age story from Ugarit tells how Athtar takes the vacant throne of the storm god Baal, discovers that it is too large for him, and descends. Some scholars compare this episode with the fall of the shining one in Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon. The relationship between the two texts remains debated.

## Sources and Iconography

In the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]] from Ugarit, Baal's death leaves the kingship vacant. Athirat proposes Athtar for the position, and he ascends Mount Zaphon to sit on Baal's throne. In H. L. Ginsberg's translation, his “feet reach not down to the footstool” [@anet, 129–142]. His head also fails to reach the top of the seat. He comes down from the mountain and rules the earth, though interpreters differ on the exact realm meant. In another damaged episode, he complains that he has no palace [@parker-unp, 96–97, 154] [@ktu3, 1.6 I 43–65].

In the ninth-century BCE [[mesha-stele|inscription of Mesha]], the king of Moab says that he devoted the people of captured Nebo to Ashtar-Chemosh. The compound may name a single divine figure or associate Ashtar with [[chemosh|Chemosh]], Moab's principal god. In inscriptions from southern Arabia, Athtar can head a list of gods invoked by a kingdom. Some scholars associate him there with rain and the supply of water, although the inscriptions give few narratives explaining his roles [@kai, no. 181] [@ddd, 186–188].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 14:4 introduces a taunt against the king of Babylon. Its address to the shining one, son of dawn, pictures a figure who plans to ascend above the stars and sit on the [[cosmic-mountain|mountain of assembly]] but goes down to the realm of the dead (Isa 14:12–15). The figure is often understood as Venus appearing as the morning star, and the reference to dawn can be compared with the Ugaritic figure [[shahar-and-shalim|Shahar, or Dawn]]. The sequence of ascent and descent on a divine mountain also recalls Athtar.

In Ezek 28:2–10, the ruler of Tyre claims a divine seat and is condemned to die. Its use of divine kingship and descent to the pit provides a thematic parallel. The oracle gives the ruler a different setting and motivation, and no identification with Athtar can be established from the text.

## Scholarly Issues

Hugh Rowland Page Jr. proposed that parts of the Ugaritic and biblical evidence reflect an older West Semitic story of rebellion against a supreme god. The underlying story is a scholarly reconstruction. In the surviving Ugaritic account, Athtar's accession follows a proposal from another deity and his descent follows his failure to fit the throne. Isaiah's figure claims the highest position for himself and is brought low. Other proposals emphasize imagery from observation of Venus or compare the Greek myth of Phaethon's brief ascent and fall.

The feminine [[astarte|Astarte]] and Mesopotamian [[ishtar|Ishtar]] are related in name to the male deity Athtar. Similar names and astral associations provide evidence of connections among regional traditions. The surviving stories and inscriptions still give each deity a distinct place in local worship.
