## Overview

The Instruction of Amenemope is an Egyptian father’s advice to his son in thirty chapters. Probably composed near the end of the New Kingdom, it teaches self-control, honest administration and regard for people whom officials could easily exploit. It describes the quiet, self-possessed person as a better model than someone driven by anger. A sequence of close parallels in the biblical Words of the Wise, especially in Proverbs 22–23, makes it a principal example of Israelite scribes adapting Egyptian wisdom.

## Manuscripts and Date

The fullest surviving copy is British Museum Papyrus EA 10474, written in hieratic, an Egyptian cursive script. Other witnesses preserve shorter portions, including writing boards and a text on a fragment called an ostracon. Scholars commonly put the composition in the Ramesside period or near the end of the New Kingdom. Proposed dates for the complete papyrus differ, and its copying and the initial composition are separate questions. The shorter witnesses help establish circulation beyond the particular manuscript in London. [@ael, 2:146–163] [@cos1, 1.47]

## Contents

The speaker offers practical instruction for life as a scribe and official. He describes the dangers of moving field boundaries, exploiting the poor, falsifying measures and pursuing wealth. The pupil is urged to avoid quarrels and to exercise restraint at the table of a superior. A contrast between the quiet person and the hot-tempered person connects composure with ethical conduct under pressure. The number thirty has explicit support in the Egyptian text, which refers near the close to the chapters of the teaching. [@ael, 2:146–163] [@cos1, 1.47]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The closest correspondences occur in Prov 22:17–23:11. The introductory exhortations to listen are followed by warnings against robbing the poor (Prov 22:22), keeping company with the hot-tempered (Prov 22:24–25) and moving longstanding [[boundary-markers|boundary stones]] (Prov 22:28 and 23:10–11). Prov 22:29 values skill in a person who will stand before kings. The next sayings concern eating in the presence of a ruler (Prov 23:1–3) and riches that acquire wings and fly away (Prov 23:4–5). The closeness of particular images and the density of correspondences together support literary dependence. [@ael, 2:146–163] [@abd]

The Hebrew collection adapts the material to its own concerns. Trust in the Lord and the Lord’s defense of the poor give particular force to the prohibitions (Prov 22:19, 23). Prov 15:16–17, on the advantages of modest means over wealth with trouble or hatred, reflects comparable values. The well-watered tree of Jer 17:5–8 and Ps 1:3 provides a broader comparison with Amenemope’s use of trees to represent contrasting lives. These more widely shared expressions carry less weight for questions of textual dependence. [@cos1, 1.47] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Adolf Erman drew attention to the connections in 1924. The dominant view gives priority to the Egyptian instruction and locates the closest contact in the opening portion of Prov 22:17–24:22. Some read Prov 22:20 as referring to “thirty” sayings, with the Egyptian divisions as a correlate. The Hebrew reading and the proposed division of Proverbs into thirty units remain disputed. Transmission through [[scribes-and-scribal-education|scribal training]] or a translation into a language such as Aramaic or Phoenician has been proposed. The parallels give firmer evidence of literary contact than of the particular route by which it occurred. [@ael, 2:146–163] [@abd]
