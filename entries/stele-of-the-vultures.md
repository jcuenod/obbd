## Overview

The Stele of the Vultures is a fragmentary limestone monument of Eannatum, ruler of Lagash in southern Mesopotamia in the mid-third millennium BCE. The reliefs portray the ruler and his troops and show a deity holding defeated enemies in a net. Its Sumerian inscription describes victory over Umma in a boundary conflict and records oaths imposed afterward. The monument gives a comparison for biblical images of a divine net, particularly where capture follows the breach of a solemn obligation.

## Description and Provenance

Fragments were recovered in excavations at Tello, ancient Girsu, beginning in 1881. The Louvre holds the reconstructed remains. The two carved faces contain scenes arranged in horizontal bands, or registers. Some portions are missing and their relationships are open to reconstruction. The modern name refers to the birds shown carrying parts of bodies from the battlefield [@anep, figs. 298–302].

## Contents

One face portrays Eannatum and his troops in scenes of victory and its aftermath. The other includes a large net filled with defeated people, held by a figure usually identified as Ningirsu, the god of Lagash. The inscription presents victory in the conflict with Umma and describes oaths connected with the contested boundary. The net appears in the monument's combination of divine victory and threats against breach of oath. The border dispute is among the earliest documented conflicts between neighboring states.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ezek 17:19–20 connects a ruler's broken oath with God's act of spreading a net over him. The stele offers a comparison because it likewise brings together the net, divine power, and an oath. Ezek 12:13 uses a net for the capture of Judah's ruler, and Ezek 32:3 announces the casting of a net over Pharaoh. Hos 7:12 compares Ephraim to birds brought down by God's net, while Lam 1:13 pictures a net spread for the speaker's feet. These poems use capture imagery that can be understood against both hunting and royal art.

Hab 1:15–17 gives net imagery a different subject, the Chaldean conqueror gathering nations. In 1 Sam 17:44, 46, both sides threaten to leave bodies to birds and animals. The vultures on the stele make visible the humiliation expressed by such language. These details belong to different accounts of warfare, each with its own rhetorical purposes.

## Scholarly Issues

The religious face and the scenes of human action present complementary claims about Eannatum's victory. The evidence is valuable for comparing how divine action authorizes enforcement of an oath. The chronological gap and absence of evidence for transmission mean that the stele and Ezekiel are best discussed as separate uses of related imagery.
