## Overview

The Instruction of Merikare is an Egyptian royal teaching cast as a king’s advice to his son and successor. Its setting is the period of divided rule before the Middle Kingdom, although the date of composition is debated and the surviving copies are later. The father advises Merikare on just government, military threats and accountability after death. A concluding passage praises the creator for sustaining human beings, whom it describes as divine images. That passage is especially important for comparison with biblical accounts of human dignity and creation.

## Manuscripts and Date

The main manuscript is Papyrus Hermitage 1116A. Other witnesses include fragments in Moscow and the Papyrus Carlsberg collection. The preserved witnesses belong to the New Kingdom, with the main manuscripts assigned to the Eighteenth Dynasty. The instructional voice is traditionally assigned to a king named Khety at Herakleopolis, but the father’s name is lost. A Middle Kingdom composition is widely favored. A setting contemporary with the royal speaker and a date nearer the surviving copies have also been proposed. [@ael, 1:97–109] [@anet, 414–418]

## Contents

The king urges his successor to cultivate effective speech, appoint capable people and administer justice. He discusses the eastern frontier and presents past dealings with rebels and enemies as lessons for future policy. The obligations of kingship are grounded in [[maat|maat]], the Egyptian idea of truthful, just and sustaining order. The speaker expects deeds to come before a tribunal after death. In the closing praise of the creator, sunlight, air, food and protection are gifts for human life. Humans belong to the creator and are described as images that came from the divine body. [@ael, 1:97–109] [@anet, 414–418]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 1:26–27 explicitly gives the divine image to humankind. Much [[egyptian-kingship|Egyptian royal ideology]] gives special prominence to the king as an embodiment or representative of a god. Merikare shows that Egyptian literature could also apply image language to humans collectively. In Ps 8:5–8 (MT 8:6–9), humans receive honor and responsibility for other creatures. Merikare’s creator supplies the world for human life, and its reference to breath in human nostrils also invites comparison with Gen 2:7. The biblical accounts give different accounts of how God creates and what bearing the image entails. [@ael, 1:97–109] [@abd, 2:380–383, 2:394–399]

The royal father’s counsel to his successor resembles the form of David’s final instructions to Solomon in 1 Kgs 2:1–9, especially in its mixture of broad principles and specific concerns about political rivals. Prov 16:12 and 25:15 similarly connect royal affairs with moral conduct and patient speech. Merikare prefers an upright person’s offering, however modest, to an evildoer’s more valuable sacrifice. Prov 15:8 and 21:3, 27 likewise assess ritual in light of the worshiper’s conduct. [@anet, 414–418] [@abd, 2:380–383, 2:394–399]

## Scholarly Issues

Historical details in the teaching are filtered through its literary purpose. The work develops a model of accountable kingship by having an experienced ruler reflect on his predecessor’s world and his own decisions. Its association of conduct with judgment after death should be read in its own terms. The tribunal passage lacks the explicit weighing of the heart found in the later [[book-of-the-dead|Book of the Dead]]. The parallels with Genesis and Proverbs are comparisons of images and ethical concerns. [@ael, 1:97–109] [@abd, 2:380–383, 2:394–399]
