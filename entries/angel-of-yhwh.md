## Overview

The angel of Yahweh is a divine messenger who appears in Hebrew Bible narratives about Hagar, Abraham, Moses and several of Israel's judges. The Hebrew word for angel also means a human messenger. In some stories the divine messenger speaks in Yahweh's own voice, and the person who encounters the messenger responds as though they have met Yahweh. Ancient Near Eastern practices of sending envoys help explain the transfer of voice. The stories also raise a question about how biblical writers present the presence of Israel's God.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Ancient messengers could transmit their sender's words in the first person. An envoy might identify who had sent him and then speak as that person, so that “I” in the message referred to the sender. Comparable conventions occur in correspondence and in stories about gods. In the Ugaritic [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]], for example, a pair of messengers named Gapn and Ugar carry Baal's words to another deity. The convention helps explain how a messenger could speak with authority understood to belong to someone else. [@parker-unp, 83, 109–114, 138–141] [@moran-amarna]

## In the Hebrew Bible

The Hebrew *malʾāk* (“messenger”) describes a role. In Hag 1:13, the human prophet is called Yahweh's messenger because he transmits God's words. In Gen 19:1 the two messengers arriving in Sodom are heavenly figures. Later works describe named or ranked angels, a development treated under [[angelology]]. By contrast, the angel in Judg 13:3–23 declines to disclose his name to Manoah.

In Gen 16:7–13, the angel finds Hagar in the wilderness and promises in the first person to multiply her descendants. The narrative then refers to Yahweh as the one who spoke to her. In Exod 3:2–6, the angel appears in the burning bush, and God calls to Moses out of the bush and identifies himself as the God of his ancestors. In Judg 6:11–24 the account alternates between Yahweh and his messenger in the encounter with Gideon. The shifts appear in narration as well as in speeches. [@ddd, 53–58]

## Interpretive Significance

One interpretation explains the shifts through representation. The messenger is authorized to speak for Yahweh, and the voice of the one making the promise is heard through the envoy. In Exod 23:20–23, Yahweh speaks of sending a messenger, tells Israel to obey him and says that his name is in him. The passage gives one biblical account of a messenger's authority, though it does not resolve every way that the narratives identify speaker and sender.

Another interpretation holds that scribes added “messenger” to some earlier accounts of God's direct appearance. This would account for transitions in which the messenger appears briefly and Yahweh otherwise acts or speaks. A third interpretation treats the messenger as a manifestation of Yahweh, an expression of the divine presence sometimes called a hypostasis. The choice among these approaches depends partly on whether a reading seeks to explain the way the final narrative works, the possible history of its wording, or the understanding of divine presence that it conveys. [@ddd, 53–58]
