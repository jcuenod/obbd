## Overview

The Nuzi texts are several thousand cuneiform tablets from Yorghan Tepe in northeastern Iraq, mostly from the fifteenth and fourteenth centuries BCE. Nuzi belonged to the kingdom of Arrapha, and its records were written in Akkadian in a setting with a strong Hurrian presence. Private archives contain contracts about inheritance, marriage, adoption, property, and labor. Scholars once used these documents to date the patriarchal narratives. They remain valuable for comparing family strategies in Genesis, but the parallels are too widely distributed or too inexact to establish the narratives’ date.

## Manuscripts and Date

Excavations at Yorghan Tepe recovered tablets in houses as well as in public buildings. Groups found together often document a household’s transactions over generations. The tablets use Akkadian, while many people named in them bear Hurrian names. That combination reflects the linguistic and political setting in which the scribes worked. The archives provide close evidence for individual households in a particular Late Bronze Age community.

## Contents

The tablets include sales, loans, wills, contracts of service, court records, and marriage and [[adoption|adoption agreements]]. Some adoptions provided a household head with an heir who was expected to give support during the adopter’s life and perform duties after death. Other transactions used adoption forms in connection with transfers of land. Marriage agreements sometimes specify arrangements if the principal wife bears no children. Their terms depend on the terms negotiated by the parties and on local legal practice.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 15:2–3 has Abram ask whether a member of his household will inherit from him. Adoption contracts offer cases in which an heir’s status was established by agreement, although Genesis does not describe Abram executing such a document. The arrangements made by Sarai in Gen 16:1–4 and by Rachel and Leah in Gen 30:1–13 can be compared with contracts contemplating children through another woman. Similar provisions occur beyond Nuzi. The notice that Laban gave attendants to his daughters in Gen 29:24, 29 also fits a broader setting in which household dependents might accompany a bride.

Some Nuzi documents connect the transfer of household gods with a privileged familial position. That led to the suggestion that Rachel seized Laban’s [[teraphim|household gods]] in Gen 31:19, 30–35 to secure inheritance rights. Moshe Greenberg and others showed that the texts do not establish possession of the images as a universal legal title to property. The biblical narrator does not give Rachel’s motive. Gen 25:29–34 shares with some documents the idea that entitlements can be transferred, but Esau’s transaction is a narrative exchange about the birthright. The service and household arrangements regulated in Exod 21:2–6 invite broader comparison with ancient law collections and contracts. No single Nuzi document duplicates the biblical rule.

## Scholarly Issues

E. A. Speiser and Cyrus Gordon used family customs at Nuzi to explain features of Genesis, often treating agreement as evidence of an early historical setting. Thomas L. Thompson, John Van Seters, and others showed that the parallels were often indirect or known from other places and periods. The older theory that the wife-sister episodes of Gen 12, 20, and 26 reflect a Nuzi legal practice of making a wife an adoptive sister has generally been abandoned. Those narratives concern a claim made to outsiders in a time of danger. Nuzi remains useful for comparing how households solved problems of succession and labor. Those comparisons do not supply a chronological test for Genesis.
