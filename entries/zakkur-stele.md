## Overview

The Zakkur Stele is an Old Aramaic monument from Tell Afis in Syria, generally dated around the turn of the ninth to the eighth century BCE. Zakkur, king of Hamath and Luʿash, reports that Bar-Hadad son of Hazael led a coalition against him. When he appealed to Baalshamayn, the god answered through religious specialists and promised protection. The text supplies evidence for political conflict among Syrian kingdoms and for a West Semitic form of divine reassurance comparable to several biblical prophetic passages.

## Description and Provenance

The stele was recovered in fragments at Tell Afis, southeast of Aleppo, and is now in the Louvre under the number AO 8185. Part of a relief figure and portions of the inscription survive. The writing names Zakkur as king of [[hamath|Hamath]] and Luʿash. The findspot is commonly identified with Hazrach, the city besieged in the account. The damaged lines of the monument have been reconstructed differently in places. [@kai, no. 202] [@cos2, 2.35]

## Contents

Zakkur credits Baalshamayn with making him king. He reports that [[ben-hadad|Bar-Hadad]], son of [[hazael|Hazael]], gathered other kings against him and laid siege to Hazrach. In response Zakkur raises his hands to Baalshamayn. The god answers through seers and another group of religious specialists whose title is translated in several ways. The oracle reassures Zakkur, recalls his installation as king and promises protection. Other portions mention building work and protect the monument through a curse. The narrative presents survival as a vindication of rule under divine favor. [@cos2, 2.35] [@kai, no. 202]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 13:3, 24–25 names a Ben-hadad son of Hazael who ruled Aram and fought Israel. The match in names and patronymic gives a basis for identifying this Ben-hadad more securely than others who bear the name in Kings. Zech 9:1 names the land of Hadrach, usually connected with Hazrach. Amos 7:12 calls Amos a seer using a term cognate with that applied to the seers in the inscription. [@abd, 1:663–665] [@cos2, 2.35]

Isa 41:8–13 uses divine presence and protection to reassure Israel. In Isa 7:1–9, prophetic reassurance comes as a coalition threatens Judah. In 2 Chr 20:1–17, a king facing an alliance prays and an inspired speaker answers with a promise of divine intervention. These passages help situate Zakkur's oracle among forms of [[prophecy-in-the-ane|inspired reassurance]]. The speakers, audiences and rhetorical settings vary. [@nissinen-prophets, 203–206] [@saa9, no. 1.1]

## Scholarly Issues

The siege is usually situated in the early eighth century BCE. Some historians infer a role for Assyria under Adad-nirari III from the regional political situation. The preserved account attributes rescue to Baalshamayn and gives no explicit account of Assyrian intervention. The inscription remains an important West Semitic witness to prophecy alongside the Deir ʿAlla evidence and the much larger bodies of material from Mari and Assyria. [@rima3, A.0.104.7 lines 4–13] [@nissinen-prophets, 203–206]
