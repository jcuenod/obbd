## Overview

Sennacherib's annals are Akkadian inscriptions on cylinders, prisms and other objects that celebrate the king's military and building projects. Their earliest surviving account of his third campaign, conducted in 701 BCE, appears on the Rassam Cylinder of about 700 BCE. Later prisms also describe the capture of fortified towns, the confinement of Hezekiah at Jerusalem and his payment of tribute. The inscriptions provide an Assyrian perspective on events described in Kings, Isaiah and Chronicles, and their versions differ in detail and emphasis.

## Description and Provenance

The Rassam Cylinder of [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] belongs to an early edition from Nineveh. It is distinct from the ten-sided [[rassam-cylinder|Rassam Prism]] of Ashurbanipal. A group of later hexagonal prisms includes the Taylor Prism in London and the Jerusalem Prism, both dated to 691 BCE, and the Oriental Institute or Chicago Prism dated to 689 BCE. They represent closely related texts. The editions continued to add campaigns and adapt the accounts for new objects and building contexts. Some later versions shorten details of the payments listed on the cylinder. [@rinap3-1, nos. 4, 22–23]

## Contents

The third-campaign accounts describe operations in the Levant, including Assyrian action against cities in Philistia and the engagement with Egyptian and Kushite forces at Eltekeh. The texts say that [[ekron|Ekron]]'s opponents of Assyria gave their king, Padi, to [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]], and that Sennacherib later restored him. The Assyrian king claims to have taken forty-six fortified cities of Judah and removed 200,150 people. The figures come from the royal account. He describes confining Hezekiah at Jerusalem using the image of a caged bird, and claims tribute without saying that he captured the city. [@rinap3-1, nos. 4, 22–23] [@cos2, 2.119B]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 18:13–16 and the annals agree on thirty talents of gold. Kings gives three hundred talents of silver and sets the demand at Lachish. The inscriptions give eight hundred talents and place delivery at Nineveh after the king's departure. The difference may reflect accounting, rhetorical selection or different stages of payment. The sources give no decisive explanation. 2 Kgs 18:17–19:37 and Isa 36–37 present Assyrian threats and Jerusalem's deliverance, and 2 Chr 32:1–23 gives its own account of the invasion. [@rinap3-1, nos. 4, 22–23] [@abd, 3:190–193]

The mention of Taharqa in 2 Kgs 19:9 and the annals' report of Egyptian and Kushite forces supply different perspectives on outside involvement. The annals do not name Taharqa. Padi's detention gives context to Hezekiah's activity in Philistia in 2 Kgs 18:8. Isa 1:7–9 and Mic 1:10–16 depict devastation in Judah and have frequently been read in the context of the campaign. [@rinap3-1, no. 22 ii 37–iii 49] [@abd, 5:71–75]

## Scholarly Issues

Most reconstructions put the biblical narratives of the Assyrian siege in the context of one campaign in 701 BCE, often distinguishing several accounts woven together in Kings. A second campaign has been proposed to explain chronological and narrative difficulties, including Taharqa's title, but has no secure external attestation. The destruction at [[lachish|Lachish]] and the separate palace reliefs are important controls on the royal accounts, which do not give that battle the prominence it receives in the visual record. [@rinap3-1, no. 22 ii 37–iii 49] [@cos2, 2.119B–C] [@neaehl, 3:897–911]
