## Overview

A clay contract from the twentieth year of Darius I, 502 BCE, names Tattannu as governor of Across-the-River, the Persian administrative region west of the Euphrates. His title is given in connection with one of the contract's witnesses, described as his servant. The combination of name, office, and reign supports the identification with Tattenai in Ezra 5–6, where a governor of the same region investigates work on the Jerusalem temple. The tablet supplies independent evidence for the official and his title. The contract itself concerns a transaction in Babylon.

## Description and Provenance

The tablet carries Babylonian cuneiform and is housed in the Vorderasiatisches Museum in Berlin as VAT 4560. Its principal edition is *Vorderasiatische Schriftdenkmäler* 4, no. 152. A dated formula places the transaction in the second month of the twentieth year of Darius I. The tablet's acquisition history goes through the antiquities trade, and an excavated findspot is unknown. The document names Babylon as the place of writing. Place of composition and findspot are different kinds of evidence.

## Contents

The text concerns a silver obligation secured on a house. Near the end of the witness list, Bēl-ēṭiranni is called a servant of Tattannu. The description goes on to give the official's title, *pīḫatu* of Ebir-nāri, or governor of Across-the-River (line 25). A witness's affiliation served to identify him in the contract. It has preserved an official title without the formal rhetoric of a royal inscription.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ezra 5:3, 6 gives Tattenai the equivalent Aramaic title of governor of Across-the-River. The narrative presents him investigating who authorized the rebuilding of the temple and reporting the matter to Darius. Ezra 6:6, 13 depicts him receiving and carrying out the king's answer. The name, title, and chronological setting together provide grounds for identifying the men. The Babylonian tablet is later than the temple inquiry as dated in Ezra, which is consistent with the same man continuing to hold office [@abd, 6:335–337].

Nehemiah likewise refers to governors of Across-the-River in connection with letters and travel (Neh 2:7, 9). Such notices give a sense of the territory's administrative importance for journeys and building projects. The title in a Babylonian contract confirms its use outside biblical Aramaic. It gives no terms of appointment or jurisdictional boundaries.

## Scholarly Issues

Other Babylonian documents mention people with the same personal name. Finding that name in more than one record does not establish whether the official can be linked to every document in a proposed family archive. The title in this text provides the strongest reason for connecting it to Ezra. Distinctions among administrative titles also matter. “Governor of Across-the-River” and “governor of Babylon and Across-the-River” could denote different levels or scopes of authority.
