## Overview

The *Eridu Genesis* is a fragmentary Sumerian account of a flood survived by a king named Ziusudra. It is known chiefly from a clay tablet found at Nippur and copied in the early second millennium BCE. The text also preserves passages on the establishment of early cities and the gods' grant of extraordinary life to the survivor. Its place among Mesopotamian flood traditions makes it valuable for comparison with Noah, although the gaps require caution about the story's original shape and details.

## Manuscripts and Date

The best-preserved witness is still badly broken. It was found at Nippur, a Mesopotamian city, and is usually assigned to the Old Babylonian period. Its modern name comes from Thorkild Jacobsen, whose reconstruction emphasized creation, first cities and flood. Miguel Civil's edition uses the title “The Sumerian Flood Story.” Large gaps occur between preserved episodes, so the sequence includes elements that editors reconstruct from partial evidence. [@lambert-millard-atrahasis, 138–139] [@cos1, 1.158]

## Contents

Early parts concern the beginnings of human life and the establishment of cities, including Eridu. The passage about the earliest cities has affinities with the [[sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]], which places several centers of kingship before a flood. The poem turns to the gods' decision to send the flood. Ziusudra receives warning in a passage involving a voice speaking by a wall. Much of the transition to the building and use of the vessel is lost. [@lambert-millard-atrahasis, 140–143] [@cos1, 1.158]

After the flood the sun appears and Ziusudra offers animals in sacrifice. The gods grant him a life like that of a god and settle him in Dilmun. Other Mesopotamian flood traditions give related roles to [[atrahasis|Atrahasis]] and to Utnapishtim in the [[gilgamesh-epic|Epic of Gilgamesh]]. The later author Berossus gives a form of the name Ziusudra as Xisouthros. [@lambert-millard-atrahasis, 144–145] [@george-gilgamesh, 1:152, 700–725]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 6:5–9:17 recounts a flood survived by Noah and the occupants of his vessel. The survivor's animal sacrifice in Gen 8:20–21 gives a particularly clear point of comparison. The Sumerian gods grant Ziusudra extraordinary life, while the biblical account presents a covenant and a promise concerning the earth and its living inhabitants. Both place the survivor's encounter with the divine after the flood, but the outcome has different implications for the future. [@cos1, 1.158] [@abd, 2:798–800, 4:1123–1132]

## Scholarly Issues

The place of the flood within a larger account of beginnings has led to comparison with Gen 1:1–9:17. Textual gaps limit how confidently that structure can be assessed. The first city in Gen 4:17 and Enoch's departure in Gen 5:24 have also been compared with motifs in the Sumerian poem. These comparisons are debated. The clearest relationship is with the wider tradition of a great flood, a survivor in a vessel and his subsequent offering. [@lambert-millard-atrahasis, 138–139] [@abd, 2:798–800, 4:1123–1132]
