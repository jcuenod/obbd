## Overview

The garden of God in Genesis is a well-watered place of trees, food, and human work planted by God in Eden. Its descriptions of rivers, precious materials, and guarded access have parallels in biblical images of sanctuaries and royal gardens. Ezekiel brings the garden together with God's holy mountain and pictures abundant life flowing from the temple. The garden becomes a way to speak about God's provision, the privilege and limits of access to his presence, and the restoration of land devastated by human or natural forces.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Ancient Near Eastern rulers maintained gardens and parks for trees, cultivation, and recreation. The Assyrian king Sennacherib described a garden associated with his palace at [[nineveh|Nineveh]], and a relief from the North Palace depicts Ashurbanipal and his queen banqueting under vines. Creating and watering such a landscape displayed control over resources and the work that made a garden possible. Royal parks also belonged to the world of the Persian court. An Old Iranian term for an enclosure lies behind Greek *paradeisos* and Hebrew *pardēs*.

The name *Eden* has several proposed explanations. One connects Hebrew *ʿēden* to Sumerian *edin*, which passed into Akkadian as a term for open country. Another connects it with a West Semitic root for luxuriance or abundance, attested in the Aramaic text of the [[tell-fekheriye-statue|Tell Fekheriye statue]]. The second proposal fits the narrative's association with fertile soil and plenty, while the origin of the name remains debated.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 2:8–3:24, God plants the garden, puts the human there to work and keep it, and makes its trees available for food with one prohibition. The story ends with the humans expelled and with [[cherubim|cherubim]] and a flaming sword guarding the way to the [[tree-of-life|tree of life]]. One river waters the garden and divides into four, including the Tigris and Euphrates (Gen 2:10–14). The mixture of familiar names, less readily located rivers, and a single source for all four gives the passage the character of cosmological geography.

Ezekiel addresses the king of Tyre in imagery of Eden, precious stones, the cherub, and God's holy mountain (Ezek 28:12–19). The lament combines privilege and expulsion to describe a king's loss of status. The allegory in Ezek 31:8–9, 16–18 compares an imperial tree with the trees of Eden and portrays it going down to the underworld. Isa 51:3 uses Eden as an image of the renewed Zion, while Joel 2:3 puts the garden before an approaching force whose passage leaves a desolate wilderness.

Biblical descriptions of ordinary estates and royal property help explain the image's associations. Manasseh is buried in the garden of Uzza (2 Kgs 21:18). The royal speaker of Eccl 2:4–6 plants parks and trees and builds pools to water them. In Neh 2:8 a keeper of the king's *pardēs* controls access to timber. These uses range from royal commemoration to cultivation and supply.

## Interpretive Significance

The carved cherubim, palms, and flowers of Solomon's temple give it some of the visual features of a divine garden (1 Kgs 6:29–35). In Ezek 47:1–12 the temple river brings life wherever it goes, supporting trees that bear food and provide healing leaves. A river also gladdens God's city in Ps 46:4 (MT 46:5). Together with Eden's eastern entrance and guarded tree, these passages support readings of the garden in light of [[temple-as-cosmos|temple symbolism]]. Scholars differ over how fully Genesis maps the institutions and duties of a sanctuary onto the garden.
