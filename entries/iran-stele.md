## Overview

The Iran Stele is a fragmentary monument of the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser III, probably erected around 737 BCE. The inscription describes a campaign into Media and lists rulers who delivered tribute, including Menahem of Samaria and Rezin of Damascus. It was reportedly found in western Iran, but its archaeological context is unknown. The list agrees in important details with inscriptions from the Assyrian palace at Nimrud and gives an independent basis for placing the biblical rulers in the eighth century BCE.

## Description and Provenance

The monument survives in three fragments. Its upper portion depicts the king with divine symbols, and the inscription runs across three faces. Two fragments are in the Israel Museum and the third was documented in a private collection. Louis D. Levine published the stele in 1972. Its reported origin in western Iran is consistent with the campaign and the monument’s account of erecting stelae in the region. An excavation record would be necessary to establish its precise original location. [@rinap1, no. 35] [@cos2, 287]

## Contents

The inscription opens with divine invocations and royal titles. After accounts of earlier campaigns, it describes an expedition into Media, including tribute in horses. The western tribute list includes [[menahem|Menahem]] of Samaria, [[rezin|Rezin]] of Damascus, Zabibe, queen of the Arabs, and rulers of Byblos, Carchemish, Hamath and Samʾal. Panamuwa represents Samʾal. The list also names Tubaʾil, the Assyrian rendering of a name equivalent to Ithobaal, as ruler of Tyre. A related list in the Nimrud material names Hiram of Tyre. A succession between tribute episodes could account for the difference. [@rinap1, nos. 14, 32, 35]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 2 Kgs 15:17, Menahem son of Gadi rules in Samaria. In 2 Kgs 15:19–20 he pays Pul of Assyria a thousand talents of silver and raises the funds by levying wealthy subjects. Pul is the biblical form associated with the name Tiglath-pileser used in Babylonian tradition. The stele confirms that Menahem paid tribute and situates his payment among those of several regional rulers. Its list gives no separate total for Menahem. Kings gives the local fiscal and political consequences, including the support Menahem sought for his hold on the kingdom. [@rinap1, no. 35] [@abd, 4:692–694]

Rezin, who also appears in the list, later joined Pekah of Israel in opposing Judah. Compare 2 Kgs 15:37 and 16:5–9 with Isa 7:1–9. Rezin’s appearance as a tributary gives evidence for his earlier relationship with Tiglath-pileser. His later defeat belongs to a subsequent phase of Assyrian expansion. [@rinap1, no. 35 ii 9′–10′] [@abd, 6:283–285]

## Scholarly Issues

The latest campaign on the monument was in 737 BCE according to the Assyrian year lists. Menahem’s tribute is commonly dated to 738 BCE, with an earlier date of about 740 BCE argued in some reconstructions. The sources give useful anchors for the [[chronology-of-kings|chronology of the kings]], although the precise synchronism depends on how the tribute records are correlated. Correspondence with the [[tiglath-pileser-iii-inscriptions|Nimrud inscriptions]] helps establish the textual setting even though the stele’s exact findspot remains unknown. [@rinap1, nos. 14, 32, 35]
