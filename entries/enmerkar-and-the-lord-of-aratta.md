## Overview

*Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta* is a Sumerian poem about a rivalry between the ruler of Uruk and the lord of a distant land. It survives in copies from the early second millennium BCE. The story follows a messenger carrying demands and replies, and it includes an explanation for writing a message on clay. It also contains a difficult spell about the language of different peoples. Scholars have compared that passage with the opening of the biblical Tower of Babel story, although its meaning remains disputed.

## Manuscripts and Date

The surviving copies come principally from the Old Babylonian period. The poem may have taken shape earlier, possibly in the late third millennium BCE, though its original date is uncertain. Its hero Enmerkar appears among the rulers of Uruk in the [[sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]]. The presence of his name in both works reflects traditions about early kingship, while the setting and events of the poem belong to a literary account. [@cos1, 1.170] [@glassner-chronicles, no. 1, 117–126]

## Contents

Enmerkar seeks precious materials from Aratta for sanctuaries in his own land. The location of Aratta is uncertain, and in the poem it lies beyond mountains. A messenger travels between the two rulers as they assert their authority and issue difficult demands. The goddess Inana and the favor she gives to Uruk are important to Enmerkar's claims. [@cos1, 1.170]

The messenger eventually faces a speech too difficult to repeat. Enmerkar puts the message on clay, and the lord of Aratta sees the signs when the messenger arrives. The episode explains writing through the practical problem of transmitting a ruler's words accurately. It shows how scribes could imagine the origin of their craft and the effect written communication could have in a contest of authority. [@cos1, 1.170]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Gen 10:10, Erech, the biblical form of Uruk, is associated with the kingdom of Nimrod. The Sumerian poem also makes Uruk a center of ambitious kingship. The specific resemblance to Gen 11:1–9 concerns the language of humanity. Genesis starts with people using one language and explains their dispersion through a divine intervention that confuses speech. The Sumerian account places the language motif inside a spell addressed to another ruler. Its plot concerns negotiation between cities, with the building program in Uruk serving a different role from the [[ziggurat-and-babel|tower at Babel]].

## Scholarly Issues

The spell is associated with Nudimmud, another name of the god Enki. Samuel Noah Kramer understood it as recalling an earlier time of one language followed by divine disruption. Bendt Alster and Herman Vanstiphout offered other readings, including one in which shared speech is a result expected in the future. The grammar and shifts in time are difficult. The comparison with Babel identifies a shared question about how peoples speak, and a closer relationship between the two works remains disputed. [@cos1, 1.170]
