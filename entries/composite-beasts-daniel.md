## Overview

The visions in Daniel 7 and 8 portray kings and empires as animals, often with features no ordinary animal possesses. In the first vision, four creatures rise from a storm-tossed sea. In the second, a ram with unequal horns is defeated by a goat with a single large horn. The visions reached their present setting in the second century BCE, but they draw on imagery familiar across the ancient Near East. Images of hybrid creatures, omen literature, and stories of divine conflict with the sea provide comparisons for understanding why Daniel gives imperial rule such disturbing forms.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Assyrian palaces used monumental figures such as the [[lamassu|lamassu]], a winged bull or lion with a human head, to guard entrances. Babylon's [[ishtar-gate|Ishtar Gate]] displayed a dragon whose body and limbs combined attributes of different animals. Similar inventions occur in the carving of seals, the small objects whose designs could be pressed into clay. The images give evidence of a regional vocabulary for creating beings with exceptional power [@anep, fig. 646].

The Mesopotamian omen series *Šumma izbu* (“if an anomalous birth occurs”) describes unusual features of newborn creatures and interprets them as signs. Paul Porter compared those descriptions with the wings, heads, and horns mentioned by Daniel. Another proposal, associated with Franz Cumont, links the ram and goat to ancient systems that assigned regions to zodiacal signs. These proposals identify ways in which observed or imagined animal features might have come to signify human affairs. Their application to any particular creature in Daniel remains debated.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Dan 7:2–8, the four winds stir the sea and a succession of creatures emerges. The first looks like a lion with eagle's wings. A bear follows it, then a leopard with four heads and four wings. The fourth animal has iron teeth, ten horns, and a new horn that has human eyes and speaks arrogantly. The interpretation identifies the animals with four kings and contrasts their rule with a kingdom given to the holy ones (Dan 7:17–18).

In Dan 8:3–12, a fast-moving goat comes from the west and defeats a ram, after which its prominent horn is replaced by four others. The vision later names the ram as representing the kings of Media and Persia and the goat the king of Greece (Dan 8:20–22). The horns distinguish rulers, divisions, and changing forms of power. Unlike the powerful animal imagery of Ezek 1:5–14, which surrounds a manifestation of God, these animals represent rival earthly rulers.

## Interpretive Significance

A longstanding interpretation connects the sea from which the beasts arise with the West Semitic [[chaoskampf|tradition of divine conflict with the sea]]. Ugaritic poems describe Sea and other formidable foes in conflicts over divine rule. The beasts of Daniel are different from those in the surviving texts, but the older imagery helps make sense of their emergence from violently disturbed water [@parker-unp, 85–86, 102–105, 111, 141–142] [@day-dragon, 151–155].

Daniel uses multiple associations to portray empire as a threat to order. Specific sculptures or omen entries remain comparisons because the visions may have received the imagery through several routes. The images could be adapted again. Rev 13:1–2 gives a single beast features comparable to several of Daniel's beasts, applying the language of violent rulership to another imperial setting.
