## Overview

The winged sun disk combines a round solar disk with outspread wings. In Egypt it appears over temple doorways and in royal imagery, where it evokes divine authority and protection. Related forms appear on monuments and seals across western Asia. In late Iron Age Judah, the motif occurs on Hezekiah's royal seal impression and a type of stamped royal storage jar. These objects provide a visual comparison for biblical language about the sun and divine protection. The meaning of the emblem varied by setting, and its presence on a seal leaves the identity of any deity it represented open.

## Archaeological Evidence

In Egypt the disk is often associated with Horus of Behdet. Its wings and accompanying cobras could frame access to a temple or shelter a royal scene. Its architectural position and setting matter to its meaning. A disk above a doorway or king is part of a larger composition in which the deity, the ruler, and the protected space can be distinguished. Egyptianizing forms traveled through the Levant and took on local uses, including in the imagery of seals and portable objects [@anep; @keel-uehlinger-ggig].

Assyrian art depicts a winged emblem that sometimes contains a human figure. Interpretations often identify the figure as Ashur in royal contexts, and other identifications including Shamash have been proposed. Achaemenid works employ both a figure in a winged emblem and disks without a human figure. Examples include the winged disk over paired sphinxes on a glazed-brick panel from Susa. Some see the winged figure as Ahura Mazda, while others interpret it in connection with royal glory. Formal similarity across these settings does not ensure that the emblems had an identical referent [@anep].

The [Hezekiah bulla](hezekiah-bulla.md) recovered in the Ophel excavations at Jerusalem depicts a disk with two downturned wings and ankhs. Its inscription identifies the royal owner of the seal. [LMLK stamps](lmlk-seal-impressions.md) also include two-winged types with an emblem usually understood as a winged disk. Their placement on storage jars attests administrative as well as visual uses. The motifs establish that Judahite kings could use Egyptianizing symbols. A connection with Yahweh's protective authority is possible, but the royal inscriptions do not name a divine figure [@avigad-sass-wsss; @keel-uehlinger-ggig].

## In the Bible

The “sun of righteousness” rises with healing in its wings in Mal 4:2 (Hebrew 3:20). The combination of the solar body and wings makes the emblem an apt visual comparison. It also admits a literary reading in which sunlight spreads like wings and brings relief. Ps 84:11 calls Yahweh a sun and shield, combining solar language with protection. In Ps 19:4–6 the sun has a tent and runs its course. The poem depicts the sun's splendor within the heavens made by God [@keel-symbolism].

Deut 4:19 warns against worship of the heavenly bodies. Second Kings describes Josiah removing horses and chariots dedicated to the sun (2 Kgs 23:11), and Ezek 8:16 depicts worshippers turning toward the rising sun. These passages witness to disputes about solar worship. They provide more explicit polemic than the seal impressions, whose purpose and symbolism have to be assessed from their own contexts.
