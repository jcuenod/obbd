## Overview

Sargon II ruled Assyria from his accession in 722 BCE to his death in 705 BCE. He claimed the conquest of Samaria, defeated western revolts at Qarqar and Ashdod, and built the royal city of Dur-Sharrukin. Isaiah names him as the king who sent a commander against Ashdod. Royal inscriptions recovered in the nineteenth century established the scale of his reign and renewed interest in the biblical notice. The inscriptions and other ancient sources leave the sequence of Samaria's fall and its aftermath open to debate.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[sargon-ii-inscriptions|royal inscriptions]] from Khorsabad, Nimrud and other sites document campaigning, the administration of conquered territories and building projects. The palace complex at Dur-Sharrukin, modern Khorsabad, included stone reliefs and large protective figures at gateways. Paul-Émile Botta's excavations in 1843–1844 brought the site to scholarly attention. Before excavations and the decipherment of cuneiform, Sargon was known chiefly from the isolated notice in Isaiah. The inscriptions also document struggles outside the Levant, including conflict with Urartu and the loss and recovery of control over Babylonia in rivalry with [[merodach-baladan|Merodach-baladan]]. ## Biblical Portrayal

Isa 20:1 names Sargon in connection with an Assyrian commander, the *tartān*, capturing [[ashdod|Ashdod]]. Assyrian sources describe resistance at Ashdod and appeals for support in the region. The campaign is usually dated around 712–711 BCE. Isa 10:9–11 depicts imperial boasting over a wider group of conquests including Hamath, Arpad and Samaria, cities that also occur in Assyrian accounts. [@cos2, 2.118A, 2.118E] [@abd]

2 Kgs 17:6, 24 and 18:10–11 report the fall of [[samaria|Samaria]], the removal of inhabitants and the settlement of people from elsewhere in the empire. The passages refer to the king of Assyria but do not name Sargon. His claims to have conquered and reorganized the city provide a comparison with the biblical account and a separate source for the movement of people. [@cos2, 2.118A, 2.118D–E] [@abc]

## Historical Assessment

Sargon's path to the throne is unclear. His succession to [[shalmaneser-v|Shalmaneser V]] and the presentation of his legitimacy have led many historians to describe him as a probable usurper. The Babylonian Chronicle credits Shalmaneser with ravaging Samaria. Some reconstructions distinguish a first capture under Shalmaneser around 722 BCE from the suppression of renewed resistance under Sargon in 720. Others give Sargon's statements different weight. [@abc] [@cos2, 2.118]

Sargon died campaigning in Anatolia, and his body was apparently never recovered. The so-called *Sin of Sargon* text shows later anxiety about what offense could explain such a death. Some scholars link the lack of burial to Isa 14:18–20. The taunt's present setting addresses the king of Babylon, and an allusion to Sargon's fate remains disputed. [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]], his successor, established his royal seat at Nineveh. [@rinap3-1] [@abd]
