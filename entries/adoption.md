## Overview

Adoption establishes a parent-child relationship independently of biological descent. Mesopotamian laws and contracts document arrangements for household membership, support, and inheritance during the second millennium BCE and later. The Hebrew Bible contains adoption-like narratives but no explicit adoption law or unambiguous technical term for the institution. This background helps distinguish the recognition of heirs from the care of orphaned children and the designation of a king as God's son. In Paul's first-century CE letters, adoption becomes a metaphor for belonging to God's family and receiving an inheritance.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[code-of-hammurabi]], from eighteenth-century BCE Babylonia, regulates adoption, child-rearing, and the termination of adoptive relationships. Its adoption provisions address competing family claims, instruction in a craft, and compensation when an adopted son is dismissed. They show that adoption created obligations as well as benefits, although the rules vary with the persons and circumstances involved. These provisions should not be reduced to a single model of adoption as the rescue of an orphan. [@roth-law, 119–121]

Contracts from the Old Babylonian period, in the early second millennium BCE, and the later [[nuzi-texts]] provide evidence of negotiated family relationships. The Nuzi documents use Akkadian, a Mesopotamian language, in a predominantly Hurrian cultural setting. Adoption could secure an heir and arrange support for an adopting parent. Legal declarations identifying someone as a son, or denying a parental relationship, could establish or challenge family status. Such language makes adoption a useful comparison for biblical sonship language, but a shared formula does not establish literary dependence. [@cos3, 3.77]

Adoption must also be distinguished from acknowledging one's biological children and arranging for another woman to bear children within a household. Recognition could affect a child's standing without creating kinship between previously unrelated people. These distinctions matter for [[inheritance-and-birthright]] and [[surrogacy-and-concubinage]]. Comparable arrangements across ancient societies do not prove that Israel followed one foreign legal system.

## In the Hebrew Bible

The clearest narratives describe someone becoming another person's child. Moses becomes the son of Pharaoh's daughter in Exod 2:10, while Mordecai takes his orphaned cousin Esther as his daughter in Esth 2:7, 15. Both stories are set outside Israel, in Egypt and the Persian court respectively. They describe family incorporation but do not explain the legal procedures or establish a general Israelite adoption institution. [@abd, 1:76–79]

Jacob's recognition of Ephraim and Manasseh in Gen 48:5-6 places Joseph's sons alongside Jacob's own sons for inheritance purposes. This is adoption-like recognition within an existing family, not the acquisition of unrelated children. Their position between the knees in Gen 48:12 has been interpreted as part of a recognition ceremony, but the verse does not identify such a ritual. Likewise, the birth of Machir's children on Joseph's knees in Gen 50:23 may indicate acceptance into the family or participation in their upbringing; formal adoption remains disputed.

Abram's complaint in Gen 15:2-3 identifies Eliezer as his expected heir because Abram has no children. Earlier interpreters compared this situation with Nuzi adoption arrangements that provided heirs for childless households. The passage, however, does not say that Abram adopted Eliezer or specify a contract. The once-popular slave-heir reconstruction is now widely questioned and cannot securely date the narrative.

## Interpretive Significance

In 2 Sam 7:14, God's fatherhood toward the Davidic ruler includes the possibility of discipline. In Ps 2:7, the king recounts a divine decree identifying him as God's son. A common adoptive interpretation understands this as the conferral of status rather than a statement of biological descent, but its identification with a particular legal ceremony is debated. The broader [[king-as-son-of-god]] tradition belongs within [[royal-ideology]], not ordinary household law.

Paul's *huiothesia*, meaning adoption or placement as a son, connects family belonging with inheritance in Gal 4:5-7 and Rom 8:15-17, 23. Roman *adoptio* transferred a person under paternal authority to another father, while *adrogatio* brought a legally independent person under a new father's authority. This legal background illuminates the metaphor without making Paul's language a description of a Roman procedure. Romans presents adoption as both a present relationship through the Spirit and a future fulfillment in bodily redemption. Since Rom 9:4 also assigns adoption to Israel, Greco-Roman practice should not exclude Israel's scriptural sonship traditions from the interpretation.
