## Overview

The sarcophagus of Ahiram is a carved limestone coffin recovered from the royal cemetery of Byblos, on the coast of present-day Lebanon. Its Phoenician inscription, often dated to around 1000 BCE, names Ahiram and warns future rulers against disturbing his burial. The date of the carved coffin itself is debated. A relief shows a seated figure on a throne supported by winged creatures. The scene supplies a close visual comparison for biblical descriptions of the God of Israel enthroned above cherubim, while the inscription illustrates how the coastal kingdoms protected royal burials through written curses.

## Description and Provenance

The coffin was excavated in 1923 in a shaft tomb at [[byblos|Byblos]]. It is held at the National Museum of Beirut. Its long sides carry figures in relief, images carved so that they project from the stone. In one scene, people approach a ruler seated before a table. The throne incorporates winged sphinxes, creatures with lion bodies and human heads, and the seated ruler's feet rest on a low support. Figures on a short end are commonly interpreted as mourners. ## Contents

The main inscription names Ittobaal, Ahiram's son, as the maker of the coffin for his father. This establishes the relationship expressed by the dedication, while the history of the stone itself remains open to investigation. The warning addresses future holders of authority who might disturb the coffin. Its penalties include the breaking or removal of a ruler's staff and the overturning of his throne. Political power is at stake in the wording of the curse, which invokes against an intruder the loss of the very standing that a royal coffin commemorated. [@kai] [@ahituv-echoes]

A separate, short inscription on the tomb shaft is commonly taken as a warning addressed to people who entered the shaft, although its precise wording is debated. The two texts belong to a regional tradition of discouraging interference with burials. The royal tomb inscription at Silwan in Jerusalem likewise curses an intruder. Later Phoenician royal inscriptions from Sidon, such as that of Tabnit, warn against opening a coffin and threaten consequences for anyone who does so. [@kai] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The winged figures provide a concrete example of the [[cherub-throne|sphinx-flanked throne]] familiar in Levantine art. In 1 Sam 4:4, the ark is associated with YHWH enthroned upon the [[cherubim]]. Ps 80:1 (MT 80:2, using the Hebrew numbering) addresses God with the same image. The relief helps explain how composite creatures could be understood as throne supports. It offers a comparison for the imagery while leaving the appearance of particular Israelite cherubim open. [@keel-symbolism]

The seated figure's low [[footstool]] and the animal figures of the throne also provide context for royal furniture, such as the throne with lions described in 1 Kgs 10:18–20. The threat against a ruler's staff can be set beside Isa 14:5, where YHWH breaks the staff of the wicked and the scepter of rulers. The broken staff is an image of power lost in both settings, while its role in each work follows from the immediate context.

## Scholarly Issues

The early Iron Age date of about 1000 BCE rests chiefly on comparisons between the shapes of the inscription's letters and those of other early texts. Some scholars date the carved coffin to the Late Bronze Age, possibly the thirteenth century BCE, and understand the lettering as an addition when the coffin was reused. Others place the carving closer to the date of the inscription. The script provides evidence for dating the inscription, while the reliefs and the context of the burial bear on the history of the coffin. [@kai]
