## Overview

Hebrew *śāṭān* means an adversary or an accuser. It can describe a political rival, an opponent in a legal setting or a figure in the heavenly court. Job and Zechariah portray a heavenly adversary whose role remains subject to God's authority. Chronicles offers a more ambiguous case, often read as an early use of Satan as a name. Second Temple Jewish works and the New Testament assign more decisive opposition to figures associated with evil, without presenting a single account of the development.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Gods acting in assemblies and officials reporting to rulers provide settings for comparing the biblical [[divine-council|heavenly court]]. A. Leo Oppenheim proposed that the roving figure in Job could reflect Persian officials described in Greek sources as the king's eyes. The validity of the institutional analogy and its bearing on Job are debated. Some scholars have also proposed an influence from [[zoroastrianism|Iranian dualism]] on later conceptions of a chief evil power. The dating and transmission of the relevant traditions make the relationship difficult to demonstrate. [@ddd, 726–732] [@ddd, 726–731]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Num 22:22, 32, YHWH's messenger opposes Balaam on the road. In 1 Sam 29:4 the Philistine commanders fear that David will become their adversary in battle. 1 Kgs 11:14, 23, 25 uses the word of Hadad and Rezon as opponents of Solomon. Ps 109:6 places an accuser at the right hand in an adversarial legal scene. The word applies to different agents according to their actions. [@ddd, 726–732]

The figure in Job 1:6–12 and 2:1–7 appears before YHWH among the [[sons-of-god|sons of God]] and challenges Job's motives. He can act within limits set by God. Zech 3:1–2 gives the accuser a similar standing in the trial-like scene involving Joshua the high priest. Both accounts use the definite form *haśśāṭān*, often understood as a designation of the role. In 1 Chr 21:1 the noun lacks the article. Some readers take it as a name, others as an indefinite adversary. The corresponding passage in 2 Sam 24:1 attributes the impulse to order the census to YHWH's anger. [@ddd, 726–732] [@abd, 5:985–989]

## Interpretive Significance

Wis 2:24 connects death with the envy of a figure called *diabolos*. It may allude to the serpent in Eden, although the figure's identity is disputed. Gen 3:1–5 introduces the serpent as a creature among the animals. Isa 14:4, 12 addresses the king of Babylon through a taunt, and Ezek 28:2, 12–19 addresses the ruler of Tyre using mythic imagery. Their use in later portrayals of Satan belongs to their reception. Rev 12:9 explicitly identifies the dragon, the ancient serpent, with the Devil and Satan. These readings draw together images that had different settings in earlier literature. [@ddd, 726–732] [@abd, 2:183–186]
