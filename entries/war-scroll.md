## Overview

The War Scroll is a Hebrew text from Qumran Cave 1 that describes a final conflict between the sons of light and the sons of darkness. It combines directions for assembling forces with speeches, prayers, and blessings. Human and heavenly participants act together, and divine intervention determines the outcome. Related fragments from Cave 4 show that the material existed in more than one literary arrangement. The scroll is important evidence for how Second Temple Jews developed biblical images of war and deliverance into a vision of the end time.

## Description and Provenance

The main surviving manuscript, 1QM, was among the first [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] recovered at Qumran. It is written on leather and preserves nineteen columns, with missing portions. Cave 4 yielded related war texts, commonly designated 4Q491–496. Some preserve versions of material in 1QM and others contain material arranged differently. The date of the copy and the history of the work's composition have to be considered separately.

## Contents

The work describes a forty-year struggle involving Israel and its opponents. The opponents include the Kittim and groups placed under Belial. The armies use formations, signals, banners, and other arrangements set out as part of the vision. Priests lead speeches and prayers, and the language of the scroll presents the war as an act governed by God. Heavenly powers share in the struggle.

The opposition between light and darkness resembles language found in the [[community-rule|Community Rule]]. Both texts describe human conduct and destiny in relation to divided camps. The War Scroll develops that opposition in the concrete terms of a conflict whose final victory belongs to God.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Num 2:1–34 supplies a model for organizing tribes around camps and standards. Num 10:1–10 describes trumpets used for assembly and war, a practice the scroll elaborates into designated signals and inscriptions. The scroll also expands the priestly address before battle in Deut 20:1–9. Its use of Num 24:17–19 places the star and scepter of Jacob within a hymn about victory (1QM 11). These examples show the use of biblical instruction and poetry in building the scroll's imagined conflict.

Dan 11:40–12:3 anticipates a final war and Michael's intervention, while Ezek 38–39 pictures God's defeat of Gog. These visions provide comparisons for the scope of the War Scroll. Rev 19:11–21 also depicts a heavenly army involved in final judgment. The contrasts between light and darkness and between Christ and Beliar in 2 Cor 6:14–15 show that comparable language could be used for ethical and communal distinctions as well as battle.

## Scholarly Issues

The scroll probably developed by combining material from more than one source or stage. Scholars have dated aspects of it to the late Hasmonean or early Roman period. The identity of the Kittim, including the references to Kittim of Assyria and Egypt, and the historical background of the military descriptions are central to the discussion. Yigael Yadin argued that Roman military practices help explain parts of the account. Other interpretations emphasize earlier Hellenistic background or the way older materials could be reapplied.
