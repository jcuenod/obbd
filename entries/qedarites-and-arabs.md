## Overview

Qedar was a northern Arabian group attested in Assyrian, Babylonian and Persian-period sources, as well as in the Hebrew Bible. Ancient writers also used broader names translated as Arabs for several peoples of inland Arabia and neighboring regions. The evidence includes royal campaigns, tribute payments, caravan trade and an inscribed silver bowl associated with a king of Qedar. These sources help explain the setting of biblical references to pastoral wealth, desert routes and Geshem the Arab, an opponent of Nehemiah.

## Sources

The [[kurkh-monoliths|Kurkh Monolith]] of Shalmaneser III lists Gindibu the Arab with one thousand camels among the king’s opponents at Qarqar in 853 BCE. It is one of the earliest securely dated uses of the name. Royal inscriptions from later centuries name Arab leaders including Zabibe, Samsi, Yatie and Teʾelhunu. They also mention kings associated with Qedar, including Hazael and Yautaʾ or Uaiteʾ. The terminology varies by inscription. The evidence for a named ruler is generally firmer than an inference about the extent of his or her rule. [@rima3] [@rinap1] [@rinap4]

A [[qedarite-silver-bowl|silver bowl]] from Tell el-Maskhuta in Egypt names Qaynu son of Gashmu and includes the title king of Qedar. A North Arabian inscription from Dedan also names a Gashm. The Gashmu on the bowl is commonly identified with the Geshem of Nehemiah, and the inscription from Dedan has also been connected with him. The names and political setting make the identification plausible, while the evidence does not establish a continuous biography. [@kai] [@abd]

## History

The inscriptions show Arabian participation in alliances, tribute relations and control of routes. Assyrian authors emphasize submission, captured goods and royal victories. The same evidence points to regional political leaders who could negotiate, resist and assemble substantial resources. Camel transport sustained movement between oases and markets. [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] spent about ten years at Tayma, illustrating the importance of inland Arabia to imperial affairs. Ancient labels for Arab groups reflect the categories of their own period and should not be equated directly with modern ethnic identities. [@abc] [@rima3] [@rinap1]

## In the Bible

Gen 25:13 gives Qedar a place among Ishmael’s sons. Isa 21:13–17 refers to caravans and predicts the end of Qedar’s military strength. Isa 42:11 names villages associated with Qedar, and Isa 60:7 portrays its flocks as an offering. Ezek 27:21 names livestock supplied to Tyre by Arabia and Qedar’s leaders. Song 1:5 uses Qedar’s tents as a comparison, drawing on a familiar feature of pastoral life. [@abd]

Jer 49:28–33 announces an attack by Nebuchadnezzar against Qedar and neighboring groups. 2 Chr 26:7 refers to conflict between Uzziah and Arabs at Gur-baal. In Neh 2:19 and 6:1–6, Geshem appears alongside other local opponents of the rebuilding of Jerusalem. The biblical references span several centuries and portray distinct groups and circumstances. The evidence for trade and politics makes sense of their range. [@abd] [@kai]
