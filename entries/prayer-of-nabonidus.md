## Overview

The Prayer of Nabonidus is a fragmentary Aramaic composition from Qumran Cave 4. It presents Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, as suffering for seven years while at Tayma and then acknowledging the Most High God. The surviving passages connect his recovery with the advice of a Jewish specialist, though damaged wording has led to different readings of forgiveness and healing. The prayer is an important parallel to Daniel 4, where Nebuchadnezzar experiences humiliation and restoration. It shows that related stories circulated with different kings and circumstances.

## Description and Provenance

The manuscript is identified as 4Q242. It survives in a small group of fragments and was written in [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]]. Its script places the copy in the late Second Temple period, much later than the king whose name it bears. The composition may be older than this copy. The manuscript cannot date the first telling of the story or the individual stages through which it took shape.

## Contents

The narrator speaks as Nabonidus and describes an affliction in Tayma. A span of seven years is attached to his suffering. A Jewish figure called a *gāzar*, often rendered diviner or exorcist, appears in the report, telling the king to acknowledge what the Most High has done. The damaged wording allows different accounts of who forgives the king’s sin. In one reconstruction the specialist is the grammatical subject, while another understands God to be the one who forgives. The heaviest gaps affect the precise sequence of advice, forgiveness, and recovery.

The surviving text also describes prayer to gods made of materials such as silver, gold, wood, and stone. The contrast between these gods and the Most High gives the episode a religious argument. Historical evidence for [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] in northern Arabia includes his own inscriptions and Babylonian records of his prolonged absence. Those records do not describe the affliction or the Jewish specialist in this prayer.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Dan 4 presents [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar]] losing normal royal life for a period described as seven times and recovering after recognizing divine sovereignty. A king’s first-person testimony, the long humiliation, an interpreter, and acknowledgment of God invite comparison with 4Q242. The kings, places, and afflictions are different. In Dan 5:4, 23 the praise of gods made from material substances provides another close thematic comparison. Dan 2:27 and 4:7 describe specialists in royal service and help place the term used for the Jewish figure in its broader narrative setting.

Mark 2:5–12 likewise brings healing and forgiveness together, but the comparison is much more general. Mark makes the authority to forgive central to a public dispute. The Qumran text’s grammar is too fragmentary to assign the same argument or role to the Jewish specialist.

## Scholarly Issues

Many scholars think traditions about Nabonidus’s stay at Tayma contributed to the story told of Nebuchadnezzar in Daniel. The Qumran prayer may preserve a form closer in some respects to that earlier association. This does not establish that the author of Daniel read the exact text represented by 4Q242. Shared motifs may have circulated in several forms, and only one damaged copy of this composition survives.
