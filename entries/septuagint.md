## Overview

The Septuagint is the collection of ancient Jewish Greek translations of the Scriptures, beginning with the Pentateuch probably in third-century BCE Alexandria. The books were translated at different times by people with different approaches. Some Greek readings reflect interpretation or translation choices, while others preserve evidence of Hebrew forms different from those in later standard manuscripts. New Testament writers frequently quote Greek forms of Scripture. Comparing the Greek, Hebrew, and New Testament witnesses helps identify which wording an author used and how it functions in a new setting.

## Manuscripts and Date

The [[letter-of-aristeas|Letter of Aristeas]] tells how a group of Jewish translators produced the law in Alexandria. It is a literary account of the translation’s authority, written later than the project it describes. Many scholars nevertheless place the Greek Pentateuch in third-century BCE Egypt. Other books followed over a long period. The name Septuagint, associated with the story of the translators, came to cover a wider body of Greek Jewish scripture.

Early surviving witnesses include the Rylands papyrus of Deuteronomy, Greek Leviticus fragments from Qumran, and a Greek scroll of the Twelve Prophets from Naḥal Ḥever. The last witness shows revision toward a Hebrew text and illustrates how early revision and translation history can overlap. Later major codices preserve much larger collections. Their order, contents, and readings differ in ways that caution against reconstructing one bound or uniform edition from the earliest period.

## Contents

The Old Greek is the earliest recoverable translation of a book, as distinguished from later revision. Some translations follow the syntax and vocabulary of their Hebrew source closely. Others reshape expressions more freely. The Greek of Jeremiah is substantially shorter than the traditional Hebrew form and arranges material differently. Samuel, Job, Esther, and the account of the sanctuary’s construction in Exodus also show significant differences. Some Hebrew fragments from the [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] support forms comparable to the Greek, including readings in shorter forms of Jeremiah. This is evidence that Greek differences can reflect an underlying Hebrew text, sometimes called a Vorlage.

Later revisers included translators or editions conventionally called Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion. The term *kaige* names an earlier revising tendency recognized through characteristic renderings. Origen’s Hexapla arranged Hebrew and Greek witnesses in columns and affected subsequent transmission. Some later manuscript groups also preserve forms traditionally called Lucianic. The Greek version of Daniel called Theodotion displaced the Old Greek in much Christian use. Ancient names and modern group labels do not always identify a single consistent layer.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Matt 1:23 quotes a Greek form of Isa 7:14 using *parthenos*. The Hebrew *ʿalmâ* commonly means “young woman.” The Greek term often implies virginity but has a wider range, as its use in Greek Gen 34:3 illustrates. Matthew’s immediate narrative gives the word a particular force. That force and the vocabulary’s earlier range should be assessed in their own contexts.

In Deut 32:8 the Greek tradition and a Hebrew witness from Qumran have readings involving divine beings, while the traditional Hebrew text refers to the sons of Israel. Acts 15:16–18 resembles the Greek form of Amos 9:11–12 in presenting peoples seeking the Lord. The wording contributes to the argument in Acts. Heb 10:5–7 likewise uses a Greek form of Ps 40:6 (MT 40:7, Greek 39:7) that refers to a prepared body where the traditional Hebrew text refers to ears. The contrast shows why a New Testament argument can be difficult to follow from an English translation based solely on the Hebrew form.

Some Greek witnesses to Samuel lack material included in 1 Sam 17:12–31. The difference invites questions about the sources and growth of the narratives, with more than one explanation proposed. New Testament quotations too can differ from later Greek witnesses. They may reflect another form of the wording, an adaptation to a new context, or a combination of both.

## Scholarly Issues

A difference between a Greek and a Hebrew text can arise through translation technique, textual transmission, revision, or a different Hebrew source. Establishing which explanation fits requires comparing more than individual words. The earliest surviving fragment need not preserve the original wording at every point. Textual criticism asks how a reading arose within the history of a book and what independent witnesses can support the reconstruction.
