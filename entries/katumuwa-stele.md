## Overview

The Katumuwa Stele is a basalt funerary monument found in its original setting at Zincirli, ancient Samʾal, in 2008. It dates to the eighth century BCE and depicts a royal official seated before food and drink. Its inscription arranges recurring offerings, including an offering for Katumuwa’s self or soul, which it locates within the monument. The find illuminates West Semitic commemoration of the dead and provides a comparison for biblical references to memorial stones, offerings and the departure of life at death.

## Description and Provenance

The Neubauer Expedition of the University of Chicago discovered the stele in a small room interpreted as a mortuary chapel. The setting is unusually informative because the stone was found in place. Its relief shows Katumuwa at a table, and the text above and beside him records how he established the monument. No human remains were found in the room or beneath it. That evidence distinguishes the place where offerings were made from whatever location was used to dispose of the body. [@herrmann-schloen-remembrance, 45–52]

## Contents

Katumuwa calls himself a servant of a king named Panamuwa and describes setting up the stele during his lifetime. His name is Anatolian, commonly described as Luwian. The inscription uses a local Northwest Semitic variety with traits shared with Samʾalian and Old Aramaic. Scholars differ over how to classify it. The text names deities honored at the ceremony and provides for regular offerings. One offering goes to Katumuwa’s *nbš*, his self or soul, said to reside in the stone. [@cos4, 4.23, pp. 95–96]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Deut 26:14 includes a declaration that none of the sacred portion was given to the dead. Ps 106:28 refers to eating sacrifices of the dead. The Katumuwa monument makes intelligible how offerings could sustain an ongoing relationship with a deceased person, without resolving what specific rites these biblical passages address. Gen 35:20 describes a pillar set over Rachel’s grave, while 2 Sam 18:18 mentions a pillar to perpetuate Absalom’s name. Isa 56:5 uses the language of an enduring memorial and name. These passages share concerns about commemoration that may be expressed in different ritual or literary forms. [@herrmann-schloen-remembrance, 69–74]

## Scholarly Issues

The description of the *nbš* in the monument is unusual evidence for the relation between selfhood and a material object after death. Some connect it with cremation, which was practiced in the wider region. The excavated room does not establish what happened to Katumuwa’s body. Later Aramaic and Nabataean use a related word for a funerary monument. Gen 35:18 also speaks of Rachel’s *nepeš* departing at death, but Hebrew usage varies by context. The shared vocabulary does not establish that the biblical authors understood the fate of the person in the same terms as the stele. [@herrmann-schloen-remembrance, 49–52] [@steiner-disembodied-souls, 14–16, 69–72]
