## Overview

In ancient Near Eastern literature, mountains could mark the dwelling place of a god, the site of a divine assembly, or a place where humans approached heaven. The Late Bronze Age poems from Ugarit locate Baal's palace on Mount Zaphon, a mountain north of the city. Biblical writers use related imagery for Sinai, Zion, and other high places of encounter. They can assign the features of a divine mountain to a place whose physical height is less important than its status. The comparison helps explain why Zion is called the far north and why the prophets can picture it rising above other mountains.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

At Ugarit, the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]] presents Mount Ṣapānu, or Zaphon, as the place where the storm god's palace stands. The name is associated with Jebel al-Aqraʿ, known in classical sources as Mount Casius, on the coast north of Ugarit. The mountain's physical setting and its place in the stories about [[baal|Baal]] give it geographic and religious significance [@parker-unp].

The Ugaritic texts situate [[el|El]] at the sources of rivers and springs where the waters of the deep are found. That setting is distinct from Baal's palace on Zaphon. Both offer points of comparison for traditions in which the gods gather in council or dwell at a source of water. The poems can give different gods places suited to their roles without laying out the geography of a single system.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ps 48:1–2 (MT 48:2–3) calls Zion God's holy mountain and applies to it a Hebrew phrase that can refer either to the remote north or to the heights of Mount Zaphon. Since Zion lies in the central hill country, many scholars take the expression as assigning to Zion the status of the northern divine mountain. In Ps 68:15–16 (MT 68:16–17), the mighty mountains of Bashan look with envy at the mountain God has chosen. The psalm makes divine choice the basis of that mountain's importance.

Exod 24:9–18 depicts Moses and the leaders of Israel approaching God on Sinai. Exod 15:17 looks toward another mountain as God's sanctuary and the place where the people will be planted. Isa 2:2–4 pictures the mountain of God's house raised above other mountains and draws the nations there to learn. In a temple vision, Ezekiel is taken to a very high mountain (Ezek 40:2). A mountain can thus mark encounter with God, an established sanctuary, or a future place of justice and worship.

In Isa 14:13 (NIV), the speaker claims he will sit on the “mount of assembly” on Mount Zaphon. The claim invokes the meeting place of a [[divine-council|divine assembly]]. Ugaritic pictures of the gods' gathering and of Baal's mountain help explain the language. The passage does not assign the mountain to a named Ugaritic god.

## Interpretive Significance

Richard J. Clifford's comparative study traces how texts in the region use mountains as dwellings, gathering places, and centers of rule. Those uses should be examined in each text. The familiar idea of a universal world axis can obscure the differences between Baal's palace, El's watery abode, and the biblical meanings of Sinai and Zion. Likewise, the elevated form of the Mesopotamian [[ziggurat|ziggurat]] has often invited mountain comparisons, but the indigenous evidence varies and cannot give every temple tower one symbolic meaning.
