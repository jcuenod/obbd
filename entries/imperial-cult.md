## Overview

The imperial cult comprised rituals and honors for Roman emperors, their families, and the personified city of Rome. Provincial councils, city governments, and private associations organized these honors in different ways. A cult could honor a living emperor or a predecessor formally deified after death. The institutions were visible in temples, festivals, and public honors, especially in the Greek East. They help explain why Revelation treats worship as a question of allegiance and why other New Testament writings distinguish the emperor's authority from claims made for God or Christ.

## Greco-Roman Evidence

The province of Asia established a temple for Roma and Augustus at [[pergamon|Pergamum]] in 29 BCE. Smyrna obtained a provincial temple under Tiberius, and a temple of the Flavian Sebastoi, the Greek title for the Augusti, was built at Ephesus in the late first century CE. Provincial shrines were only one level of activity. Cities organized their own festivals or priesthoods, while associations and individuals could dedicate objects. The [[priene-calendar-inscription|Priene calendar inscription]] shows how political loyalty and religious language could be joined in a public honor.

In Roman usage, *divus* was a title for a deceased ruler formally deified. Living emperors received different forms of honor according to locality and occasion. A ceremony could include prayer or sacrifice for a ruler's well-being, worship addressed to the emperor, or worship directed to another deity on the emperor's behalf. Conflating these actions obscures the choices ancient participants faced.

The [[lysanias-abila-inscriptions|Abila dedication]] by Nymphaeus names the safety of the Augusti and their household as its occasion. The dedication itself is to Kronos and the fatherland. First Timothy 2:1–2 illustrates another distinction, asking believers to pray for rulers [@savignac-abila, inscription A].

Judeans sometimes secured arrangements protecting their own practices. [[josephus|Josephus]] reports sacrifices on behalf of the emperor and Roman people in Jerusalem and describes their termination in 66 CE as a decisive step toward revolt (*War* 2.197, 409). This episode establishes one accommodation and its political significance. The ways that Jewish communities related to imperial cults varied by time and place.

## In the New Testament

Rev 13:4–18 describes worship of the beast and its image and connects the beast's mark with commerce. Rev 14:9–11 warns against that worship. Cultic monuments and civic ceremonies help set the scene for this critique. The visions use symbolic language and do not document an empire-wide licensing system for buying and selling. Rev 2:13 calls Pergamum the place of Satan's throne. The prominence of imperial worship there is one explanation, among several proposed identifications.

Acts 12:21–23 describes Agrippa I being hailed as divine and dying soon afterward. Josephus describes a comparable acclamation and death (*Antiquities* 19.343–350), with differences in the circumstances and explanation. In Acts 17:7, an accusation presents the proclamation of Jesus as a rival claim to kingship. Mark 12:13–17 uses a coin bearing Caesar's image to bring public authority into the discussion of tax. In 1 Pet 2:13–17 honor for the emperor appears alongside a distinct injunction to fear God.

## Interpretive Significance

Steven Friesen relates Revelation to the politics and religion of cities in Asia, including the expectations produced by ordinary civic life. Evidence for a systematic empire-wide persecution of Christians under Domitian is weak. The book's imagery can address pressures to participate in local institutions without requiring a single centrally imposed campaign. Phil 2:9–11 imagines universal acclamation of Jesus as Lord. Readers debate whether it also evokes imperial claims, while its close scriptural parallel in Isa 45:23 makes the Jewish context essential.
