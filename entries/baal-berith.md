## Overview

Baal-berith and El-berith are divine titles associated with the ancient city of Shechem in the book of Judges. Their final element is the Hebrew word for “covenant,” a formal agreement or binding pledge. In the story of Abimelech, one title is attached to a temple treasury and the other to a sanctuary that serves as a refuge. The text has led scholars to ask whether the names refer to one god or two, how the god might have guaranteed agreements, and how the story relates to other biblical traditions about Shechem as a place of covenant-making.

## Sources and Iconography

Judges is the principal source for the titles. Baal-berith means “Lord of the Covenant” and El-berith can be rendered “God of the Covenant.” The first element in each title can function as a designation, but it can also be a divine name. [[baal|Baal]] means “lord” and is also the name or title of the West Semitic storm god. [[el|El]] means “god” and is also the name of a major West Semitic deity [@ddd, 141, 280].

Excavations at [[shechem|Shechem]] uncovered a large sanctuary with thick walls, often called a fortress temple. The remains include Bronze Age phases, and the building's form makes it a natural point of comparison for the temple refuge in Judges. Its identification with the sanctuary or stronghold in that story remains debated. No inscription from the building identifies its deity as Baal-berith or El-berith [@neaehl, 4:1345–1347].

## In the Hebrew Bible

The narrator says that after Gideon died, the Israelites turned to the Baals and made Baal-berith their god (Judg 8:33–35). In the ensuing story, the people of Shechem give Gideon's son Abimelech silver from the house of Baal-berith. He uses the money to hire followers, kills his brothers except for Jotham, and is made king at Shechem (Judg 9:1–6). The sanctuary thus serves as a source of public wealth in the story of a bid for power.

Later the Shechemites celebrate the grape harvest in the house of their god and curse Abimelech there (Judg 9:27). When Abimelech turns against the city, people associated with its tower take refuge in the stronghold of the house of El-berith. He destroys it with fire (Judg 9:46–49). The narrative connects the city's religious institutions to both the rise of its ruler and the final collapse of their alliance.

## Scholarly Issues

A common reading is that both titles designate one local deity. Under this reading, the title may describe the god's role as witness and guarantor of a political agreement, possibly among communities joined in a league. Ancient Near Eastern treaties often invoke gods to witness an agreement and uphold its terms, a practice discussed under [[covenant-and-treaty-form|covenant and treaty form]]. Judges preserves the titles without explaining the terms or participants of any such covenant. Some scholars allow that the names refer to two cults or represent different layers of tradition about the city.

Shechem appears elsewhere as a place of covenant and worship. Joshua makes a covenant with the people and erects a stone as witness beside the sanctuary of YHWH, the God of Israel (Josh 24:25–27). In an earlier story, Jacob erects an altar at Shechem and gives it a name associating El with the God of Israel (Gen 33:18–20). The shared location makes comparison worthwhile. These accounts describe different ceremonies and leave the historical relationship among the city's cults open.
