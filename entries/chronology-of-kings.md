## Overview

The chronology of the kings of Israel and Judah assigns calendar dates to rulers known from Kings, Chronicles and ancient Near Eastern records. Biblical formulas give lengths of reign and often date a king's accession by a year in the neighboring kingdom. Assyrian and Babylonian writings provide fixed points in the ninth through sixth centuries BCE. Combining those sources requires decisions about when ancient years began and how scribes counted partial years or overlapping reigns. Several reconstructions agree on important events while differing on individual accessions and the date of Jerusalem's final fall.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Assyrians named each year after an official listed in an [[assyrian-eponym-list|eponym list]]. An eclipse noted under Bur-Saggile is identified with the solar eclipse of 15 June 763 BCE. Counting from that anchor locates campaigns that mention rulers of Israel. The [[battle-of-qarqar|battle of Qarqar]], in which Ahab appears in the Assyrian record, dates to 853 BCE, and the tribute of Jehu in Shalmaneser III's eighteenth year dates to 841 BCE. The latter appears in connection with the [[black-obelisk|Black Obelisk]]. [@rima3] [@abc]

The [[nebuchadnezzar-chronicle|Babylonian chronicle of Nebuchadnezzar's early reign]] places the capture of the city of Judah on the second day of Adar in the king's seventh year. This corresponds to March 597 BCE, conventionally 16 March in the Julian calendar. The text goes on to record the appointment of a king of Babylon's choosing. The biblical account describes Jehoiachin surrendering and Zedekiah being installed (2 Kgs 24:10–17). [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles]

## In the Hebrew Bible

A synchronism dates one ruler by another ruler's regnal year. For example, 1 Kgs 16:23 places Omri's accession in the thirty-first year of Asa of Judah. Combining such statements with total reign lengths exposes tensions. Some could reflect shared authority of a king and his heir, a situation called a coregency. Second Kings 15:5 describes Jotham administering the country while his father still lived, though the way such overlap entered the year count is open to interpretation.

The calendar and manuscripts pose other problems. An accession-year system treats the partial first year as a separate period and starts the first numbered year at the next New Year. Other systems count the partial year as year one. A year beginning in spring and one beginning in autumn also assign some months to different numbered years. The ancient Greek versions of Kings transmit regnal numbers that sometimes differ from the Hebrew, including around the Omride dynasty. [@abd]

## Interpretive Significance

Edwin Thiele reconciled many of the dates by using different counting systems for Israel and Judah and positing coregencies. Critics question whether individual overlaps have independent support or were inferred to resolve the arithmetic. Later reconstructions by Hayim Tadmor and Gershon Galil make different choices about reconciling the sources. Jeremy Hughes gives greater weight to schematic patterns in the biblical figures, or the possibility that numbers were arranged for a literary or chronological purpose.

The final [[fall-of-jerusalem-587|fall of Jerusalem]] is usually assigned to 587 or 586 BCE. The difference reflects the way interpreters align Zedekiah's and Nebuchadnezzar's years and the calendars used to count them. The year 597 for the earlier capture rests on a dated Babylonian record. The later destruction is less precisely anchored because no comparable surviving Babylonian record supplies its day and year. [@abc] [@abd]
