## Overview

Jewish texts of the Second Temple period describe several ways God might bring deliverance and establish lasting rule. Some expect a king descended from David, others give priests or prophets a central part, and some picture a heavenly agent. The word *messiah* means “anointed one” and began as a designation for someone consecrated or appointed, such as a reigning king. Later authors drew on royal promises, descriptions of prophets, and visions of God's court to describe the future. Many Jewish writings from the period express hope for deliverance without naming a messianic figure.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the ancient Near East, monarchs could claim appointment and protection by a deity. Egyptian and Mesopotamian literature uses familial language for the relationship between a ruler and the gods, although it does so in different ways. The [[king-as-son-of-god|royal language of divine sonship]] in the Bible belongs in that wider setting. Jewish texts then applied inherited language about kingship to future deliverance in circumstances where direct rule by descendants of David had ended.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Ps 2:2, 7 the Lord's anointed king is addressed as God's son. The promise in 2 Sam 7:12–14 establishes a relationship between God and the descendants of David in similar language. These uses concern kingship, while priestly texts can also speak of an anointed priest (Lev 4:3). Isa 11:1–5 looks toward a ruler from the family of Jesse who judges justly. Num 24:17 speaks of a star and scepter from Israel, while Zech 4:14 describes two figures standing in attendance on God, literally “sons of oil.” Later interpreters connected such passages with future royal and priestly figures.

In Dan 7:13–14 someone like a human being receives dominion in the heavenly court, and the chapter's interpretation gives the kingdom to the holy ones. The vision stands within a [[four-kingdoms-schema|sequence of kingdoms]] brought to an end by divine judgment. The figure acquired a more explicitly individual role in some later readings, notably the Similitudes of [[first-enoch|1 Enoch]].

## Interpretive Significance

The Qumran commentary known as 4QFlorilegium (4Q174) reads the promise to David in relation to a future Branch of David. Psalms of Solomon 17 looks for a Davidic king who cleanses Jerusalem, judges justly, and strikes down wrongdoing with his word. The [[community-rule|Community Rule]] (1QS 9:11) looks for a prophet and the anointed ones of Aaron and Israel. The Damascus Document (CD 7:18–21) distinguishes the star and scepter of Num 24:17, and the [[war-scroll|War Scroll]] (1QM 11:6–7) applies the oracle to a future victory. Particular texts organize these roles in different ways.

John 1:19–25 distinguishes the Messiah, Elijah, and the prophet as candidates for John's identity. In Mark 8:27–30 Peter identifies Jesus as the Messiah. Matt 11:2–6 responds to a question about Jesus through acts of healing, resurrection, and good news to the poor. The Qumran text 4Q521 likewise mentions an anointed figure and describes a time of such works. Scholars debate how much agency 4Q521 assigns to this figure. Luke 1:32–35 gives Jesus titles that have verbal parallels in the Aramaic text 4Q246, including Son of God and Son of the Most High. The identity and role of the figure bearing these titles in 4Q246 remain disputed.

[[josephus|Josephus]] describes several leaders who attracted followers through royal claims or promised signs. They include figures who took up kingship after Herod's death and prophets such as Theudas and the Egyptian (Ant. 17.271–285, 20.97–99 and 20.169–172). His accounts show that some hopes took public and political form. The evidence gives each movement its own setting and leaves open how its followers would have described their expectations.
