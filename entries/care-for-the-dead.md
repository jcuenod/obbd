## Overview

Families in the ancient Near East remembered the dead through burial, memorials, and sometimes recurring offerings of food and drink. Texts from Mesopotamia and Ugarit describe obligations to deceased relatives, and an eighth-century BCE monument from Zincirli in southern Anatolia directs descendants to make annual offerings. The Hebrew Bible gives glimpses of similar practices in laws, narratives and criticisms. Scholars disagree about how often such rites were performed in Israel and Judah and about when care for dead relatives should be described as worship.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In Mesopotamia, *kispu* was a practice of bringing offerings and maintaining ties with the dead. Food and water could be supplied at regular intervals, and the dead could be remembered by name. A person responsible for such duties could be called a *pāqidu*, a caretaker. These obligations placed care for the dead within the continuing life of a household. [@abd] [@cos3]

The Ugaritic [[aqhat-epic|Tale of Aqhat]] describes duties expected of an ideal son. One concerns a standing stone whose connection with a divine ancestor remains debated. A separate Ugaritic ritual calls on ancestral figures in the context of royal death and succession. These texts show how both a family's future and royal continuity could be understood in relation to prior generations. [@ktu3] [@parker-unp]

The [[katumuwa-stele|Katumuwa Stele]] from Zincirli provides clearer instructions about a particular person's commemoration. Its inscription places Katumuwa's *nepeš* (“soul” or “life”) in the monument and arranges for an annual offering. The carved scene portrays him seated before a table. The instructions show how an inscribed monument could serve as a place for repeated acts of remembrance and provision.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Deut 26:14 an Israelite declares that none of the sacred portion set aside as a tithe was given to the dead. The declaration shows that the author knew the possibility of such an offering. Tob 4:17 commends placing bread at the burial of the righteous, and Sir 30:18 uses food laid on a grave as an image. The passages refer to practices in different settings and express different evaluations of them.

Gen 35:20 describes Jacob setting up a pillar over Rachel's grave, and 2 Sam 18:18 associates Absalom's pillar with preserving his name. [[bench-tombs|Family tombs]] add evidence about continuing burial practices and the objects left with the dead. In 1 Sam 28:13–15 Saul uses a medium to speak with Samuel. This belongs to [[necromancy|consultation of the dead]], which seeks knowledge and can be distinguished from commemorating someone through a monument or meal. [@king-stager-life]

## Interpretive Significance

Elizabeth Bloch-Smith and Theodore J. Lewis see the material and textual evidence as pointing to an established pattern of rites for the dead. Brian B. Schmidt interprets the evidence more narrowly and dates some ideas about helpful ancestors relatively late. The debate turns partly on what counts as an offering and whether food, grave goods or remembrance imply worship. Karel van der Toorn's proposal that [[teraphim]] were images of ancestors has to be weighed against other explanations, including household gods and instruments of divination. [@abd] [@ddd]
