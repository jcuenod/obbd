## Overview

Before minted coins became common, people in the Levant could pay with silver measured by weight. Excavations have recovered balances, stone weights, and hoards of cut pieces of silver. Inscriptions on some weights show how local systems marked standard units while actual pieces varied. These finds clarify biblical accounts in which people weigh silver when buying land or reckon offerings in shekels. They also give a practical setting for laws and prophetic complaints about inaccurate scales and deliberate manipulation of measures.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

A balance compared the mass of goods or metal with known weights. The shekel, mina, and talent were weight names used in several areas, with proportions or actual masses that could differ. Judahite inscriptions mark a shekel conventionally reconstructed at about 11.3 or 11.4 grams. Some stones bear Egyptian-derived numerical signs, while others name units such as the *pim* and *beqaʿ*. Measurements vary among surviving objects. The system supplied a recognizable standard without making every individual weight identical.

Hoards at sites including Dor, Eshtemoa, and ʿEin Ḥofez contain cut pieces of silver, commonly called *Hacksilber*. Such pieces could be collected, weighed, and divided without being minted. Some groups preserve traces of packaging or sealing. Studies comparing lead isotopes with reference samples from ores suggest that silver reached the Levant from several regions, including Anatolia and parts of the central and western Mediterranean. Recycling and mixing can obscure the source of a given piece. The types and amounts found in different hoards also vary.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 23:16 depicts Abraham weighing silver in a land purchase. Jer 32:9–10 explicitly joins weighing the price with writing, witnesses, and sealing. These passages distinguish the payment itself from the documentation that establishes the transaction. Exod 30:13 specifies the sanctuary shekel for a contribution, and 2 Sam 14:26 mentions a royal weight. The contexts show why a text might say whose standard it used. They do not establish that a single conversion to grams applies to both.

First Samuel 13:21 uses the term [[pim-weights|pim]] in connection with a charge for work on implements. Inscribed stones make its interpretation as a weight unit plausible. Hebrew *kesep* can mean silver and, by extension, payment or money. Rendering it as “money” is appropriate where the function is at issue. Rendering it as “coins” in a setting that describes weighing unminted silver is misleading. The text and the period should guide that choice.

Lev 19:35–36, Deut 25:13–16, and Prov 11:1 call for honest measures or condemn false ones. Amos 8:5, Hos 12:7, and Mic 6:10–11 describe commercial wrongdoing through balances and measures. An inaccurate weight can move value between parties without changing the price as stated. The legal and prophetic texts address that possibility, although archaeological variation alone does not prove deliberate deception.

## Interpretive Significance

Weighed silver functioned as a means of payment and could also be kept as a store of value. The evidence includes metal set aside in hoards, standardized instruments, and written recognition of transactions. [[coinage|Coinage]] added official designs and denominations. Its appearance did not immediately eliminate weighing or end the need to check the value of unfamiliar pieces.
