## Overview

The Phoenician cities of the eastern Mediterranean traded by sea and established communities as far west as the Atlantic approaches. Archaeological evidence includes cargoes lost at sea, inscriptions in western settlements, and representations of ships. Biblical accounts associate Tyre with timber shipments and voyages undertaken with Israelite kings. Ezekiel pictures its trade through the image of a richly furnished ship. The evidence helps explain the scale of maritime connections while leaving many specific routes, dates, and destinations open to reconstruction.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Egyptian [[report-of-wenamun|Report of Wenamun]] describes a mission to Byblos for timber. Although its date and literary character require care, it illustrates the longstanding movement of Levantine wood by sea. A lost Assyrian palace relief known through an early drawing shows ships in a scene commonly associated with the flight of the ruler Luli during Sennacherib’s campaign. Such images are evidence for how ships were represented. They reveal only selected aspects of construction and navigation.

Two eighth-century BCE wrecks off Ashkelon are conventionally called Tanit and Elissa. Their surviving cargoes contain many transport jars interpreted as wine amphorae. The wrecks show ships carrying a large quantity of a particular commodity, giving direct evidence for organized trade. The cargoes and findspots offer clues to routes without identifying the final ports. Other kinds of trade left little trace on the seabed or survive chiefly through writing.

Phoenician settlement and trade reached Kition on Cyprus, Carthage in North Africa, Sardinia, and Iberia. The traditional date for Carthage’s founding is 814 BCE. Pottery and radiocarbon results have prompted reconsideration of the dates of some western developments. Finds at Huelva can indicate a Phoenician presence before the creation of fully established colonies elsewhere. The [[nora-stone|Nora Stone]] attests Phoenician writing on Sardinia. Evidence for early contact, permanent settlement, and political foundation needs to be assessed at each site.

## In the Hebrew Bible

First Kings 5:8–9 (MT 5:22–23) describes Hiram sending timber by sea as rafts. First Kings 9:26–28 describes Tyrian sailors joining Solomon’s enterprise from Ezion-geber to [[ophir|Ophir]]. These passages assume different maritime environments and depend on Tyrian experience. First Kings 10:22 associates ships of Tarshish with goods brought at intervals, while 22:48 applies the designation to an unsuccessful fleet at Ezion-geber. This allows the possibility that the phrase could describe vessels equipped for long voyages.

Ezek 27 presents Tyre as a ship built and staffed with products and people from many places. Verses 5–9 name materials, sailors, and craftsmen. The rest of the chapter develops the extensive network in order to lament its collapse. The oracle belongs to the sixth-century prophetic setting, and its catalogue is shaped by the poem’s purpose. Isa 23:1–14 also calls for mourning over Tyre and the disruption of maritime exchange. Jonah 1:3–5 uses a sea voyage as a means of escape, and Ps 107:23–30 uses seafarers’ experience of storms to portray danger and relief.

## Interpretive Significance

[[tarshish|Tarshish]] has been identified with places including Tartessos in Iberia and Tarsus in Cilicia. Several passages place the name in maritime contexts without specifying its geography. The range of known Phoenician voyages makes distant trade plausible but cannot resolve the name’s location. Archaeological cargoes and inscriptions supply particular cases. Biblical poems and narratives show how such commerce was remembered and given literary meaning.
