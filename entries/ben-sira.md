## Overview

The Wisdom of Ben Sira, also called Sirach, is a Jewish book of teachings composed in Hebrew, probably in Jerusalem in the early second century BCE. The author's grandson translated it into Greek after traveling to Egypt. Hebrew manuscripts recovered from the Judean Desert and the Cairo Genizah preserve much of the work. Ben Sira takes up familiar themes from Proverbs, such as instruction and the discipline of speech, and connects Wisdom explicitly with the law of Moses. His praise of Jerusalem's high priest also gives a portrait of temple worship before the Maccabean revolt.

## Manuscripts and Date

The book takes its name from Jesus, or Yeshua, son of Eleazar son of Sira. The Greek translator's prologue dates his arrival in Egypt to the thirty-eighth year of a king called Euergetes, generally identified as Ptolemy VIII. This places his arrival in 132 BCE. The praise of the high priest Simon in Sir 50:1–21, usually identified as Simon II, and the book's apparent setting before the persecution under Antiochus IV support the usual date of c. 190–175 BCE for the Hebrew composition. [@abd]

About two thirds of the book survives in Hebrew. Six manuscripts conventionally called A through F come from the Cairo Genizah, a collection of worn or discarded writings preserved in a synagogue. Earlier material survives in a scroll from [[masada|Masada]] and in [[dead-sea-scrolls|manuscripts from Qumran]], including a copy of part of chapter 6 and a poem preserved in the large Psalms Scroll. A Syriac translation also survives. The Greek tradition has a shorter and an expanded form, and the order of part of chapters 30–36 differs among editions owing to an early displacement in the Greek transmission. [@abd]

## Contents

Sir 24 describes Wisdom as speaking in her own voice and dwelling in Israel. The chapter identifies what it has described with the book of the covenant, the law Moses gave Israel (Sir 24:23). The book's praise of the ancestors (Sir 44:1–50:24) reaches its climax with the high priest Simon at the altar. Aaron and Phinehas also feature in this survey, which gives religious instruction, Israel's past, and temple worship a place within the same account of wise living.

Ben Sira commends the study and transmission of instruction. In Sir 38:24–39:11, artisans keep society going, while the scribe's opportunity for study equips him to interpret inherited wisdom. The teacher invites learners into his house of instruction in Sir 51:23. The book places much of its emphasis on what people do and experience in this life, and Sir 17:27–28 contrasts the living who can praise God with the silence of the dead. Sir 25:24, commonly taken to refer to Eve, comes within sayings about wives and household relations that address men as their expected readers. The reference to Eve is implicit, and readings that connect it more closely to the surrounding portrayal of a harmful wife have also been proposed.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The figure of Wisdom in Prov 8:22–31 provides a comparison for Sir 24. In both passages Wisdom speaks about her relationship to creation, and Sirach connects that account with the law of Moses. The contrast between the scribe and the trades in Sir 38:24–39:11 is also comparable to Egypt's [[satire-of-the-trades|Satire of the Trades]], a work that praises scribal education by depicting other occupations. In Matt 11:28–30, the invitation to take up a yoke and find rest recalls language of instruction in Sir 51:23–27 and 6:24–30. Whether the Gospel draws directly on Sirach or uses familiar wisdom language remains open. [@ael] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Rabbinic works cite Ben Sira, although the book falls outside the Jewish biblical canon. It is received as scripture in Catholic and Orthodox traditions and generally appears among the Apocrypha where Protestant editions include it. The Latin title Ecclesiasticus means “church book.” These different histories of reception help explain both the book's preservation in several languages and its varying place in Bible editions.
