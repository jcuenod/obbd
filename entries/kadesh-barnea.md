## Overview

Kadesh-barnea was an oasis remembered in the Hebrew Bible as a principal stopping place during Israel’s wilderness journey and a landmark on the southern boundary of Canaan. It is usually identified with the spring and mound at ʿAin el-Qudeirat in northeastern Sinai. Excavations have revealed Iron Age structures and inscriptions, including evidence for a fortified site in the later monarchy. The relation of the earlier remains to the proposed forts is debated, and archaeology has provided no secure evidence for the Late Bronze Age encampments sometimes proposed for the wilderness narratives.

## Location and Identification

ʿAin el-Qudeirat is a well-watered site on the western edge of the Negev highlands. The adjacent mound is called Tell el-Qudeirat. The nearby spring at ʿAin Qedeis may preserve the name Kadesh. The quantity of water at Qudeirat and its position near routes across northeastern Sinai make it an attractive identification for the biblical oasis. The Kadesh fought over by Egypt and the Hittites was on the Orontes in Syria. [@neaehl, 4:1385–1393] [@abd, 4:1–4]

## Archaeological History

Rudolph Cohen’s excavations in 1976–1982 distinguished three superimposed Iron Age fortresses. The excavators dated the earliest, reconstructed with an oval plan, to the tenth century BCE. Other researchers interpret the earliest remains as a settlement and date the earliest securely identifiable fortress later. Radiocarbon dates can indicate early activity without settling the function of the structures to which they are assigned. Defenses of the upper phase belong to the later monarchy, broadly the seventh to early sixth centuries BCE. [@kadesh-barnea-excavations, chs. 1 and 20] [@mazar-archaeology, 444–451]

Inscribed potsherds from the later phases include Hebrew writing and Egyptian hieratic numerals, cursive forms used for recording numbers. One group may represent exercises in scribal training. The finds show how accounting conventions could circulate across languages in frontier administration. Excavations have yielded no secure Late Bronze Age occupation at the mound. Brief mobile camps would be harder to detect than established fortifications. [@ahituv-echoes, 207–212] [@kadesh-barnea-excavations, 245–254]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 14:7 identifies En-mishpat as Kadesh. In Num 13:26, the scouts return to Kadesh in the wilderness of Paran. Num 20:1–21 puts Miriam’s death, the incident at the waters of Meribah and the appeal for passage through Edom there. Deut 1:19, 46 recalls the approach to Kadesh-barnea and Israel’s long stay, and Josh 14:6–7 makes it the place from which Caleb was sent to scout the land. The references treat Kadesh as a recognizable node in travel across a wider region. [@abd, 4:1–4]

Num 34:4 and Josh 15:3 use Kadesh-barnea in defining southern boundaries. Ezek 47:19 likewise includes the waters of Meribath-kadesh in the boundary of its ideal land. The later fortified settlement demonstrates the oasis’s strategic importance to control of travel and access to water. Archaeology is firmer for the world of Iron Age borders than for the scale and route of the remembered [[exodus-historical-questions|wilderness journey]]. The lack of securely identified Late Bronze remains limits historical reconstruction without settling how the tradition arose. [@neaehl, 4:1385–1393] [@abd, 4:1–4]
