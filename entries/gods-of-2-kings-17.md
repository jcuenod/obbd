## Overview

Second Kings 17 lists the gods of people whom the Assyrian empire settled in the towns of Samaria after the fall of the northern kingdom in the late eighth century BCE. The passage connects different communities with particular gods and sets their worship alongside that of YHWH. Nergal of Cuthah is independently identified in Mesopotamian records, while most of the other names remain disputed. The list provides evidence for how the biblical writer understood the religious consequences of population transfers. Assyrian evidence establishes a broader setting for those transfers without identifying all the communities or their practices.

## Sources and Iconography

Assyrian royal inscriptions report the removal of inhabitants from Samaria and the settlement of people from elsewhere. These policies form part of the imperial practice of [[deportation|forced resettlement]]. The inscriptions do not provide a counterpart to the full list of names in Kings. Mesopotamian sources do attest Nergal as the god of Cuthah, a city in Babylonia. He is associated with warfare, disease, and the underworld in different texts. This makes his identity and the geographical association in the biblical passage considerably more secure than the other entries. [@cos2, 2.118D–E] [@ddd, 621–623]

Succoth-benoth, associated with Babylon in Kings, has been connected with Zarpanitu, the consort of Marduk, or explained as a transformed expression. The derivation is uncertain. Ashima is associated with [[hamath|Hamath]]. The name has been compared with the form Ashim-Bethel attested at [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine]], but a connection does not identify the god's role at Hamath. Nibhaz and Tartak, associated with Avva, have no generally accepted identifications. Later descriptions of animal forms for these figures cannot securely be used to reconstruct their Iron Age images. [@ddd, 821–822] [@tad, A2.1, C3.15]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Second Kings 17:24–41 depicts the newcomers being settled in Samaria and receiving instruction in the worship of the local god. The narrator then lists the other deities they worshiped, including Adrammelech and Anammelech of Sepharvaim. The passage associates these two with the burning of children, an accusation considered separately under [[child-sacrifice|child sacrifice]]. There is no independent documentation of the specific practices attributed to the Sepharvites here. The name Adrammelech also appears in 2 Kgs 19:37 as the name of one of Sennacherib's sons. The passages refer to different figures. [@ddd, 8–10, 34–35, 105–106, 621–623, 821–822] [@abd, 5:1089–1091]

## Scholarly Issues

Sepharvaim has been identified with Sippar in Babylonia or with a place in Syria such as Sibraim, named in Ezek 47:16. Neither location is secure. Suggestions for the divine names include forms containing the names of gods such as Hadad or Anu and a word for king. Such reconstructions depend on assumptions about how the names were written and transmitted. The biblical passage gives clearer evidence of the associations the writer wanted to make than of the original forms of the names. [@ddd, 8–10, 34–35]

The account emphasizes that the settlers served their own gods alongside their response to YHWH. That portrayal serves the narrator's criticism of mixed worship. Some scholars read it as part of a later polemic against the people of Samaria, while the exact relationship to the emergence of the later Samaritan community is debated. The history of the region includes both resettlement and continuity. See [[samaritan-origins|Samaritan origins]]. [@abd, 5:940–948] [@cos2, 2.118D–E]
