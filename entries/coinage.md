## Overview

Coins are pieces of metal struck with designs that certify or identify an issue. The earliest coins of the eastern Mediterranean appeared in western Anatolia around 600 BCE. By the Persian period, coins circulated alongside weighed silver in the southern Levant, and local issues appeared in Judah and Samaria. Later biblical texts mention several currencies from the Persian, Greek and Roman worlds. This history helps readers distinguish an ancient unit of weight from a named coin and see how different currencies could circulate in the same place.

## Archaeological Evidence

Some early coins from Lydia and surrounding Greek cities were struck in electrum, an alloy of gold and silver. Persian royal issues included a gold coin known as the daric and a silver coin called the siglos, associated with the reign of [[darius-i|Darius I]] and later kings. The circulation of coins grew alongside older ways of transferring value. [[weights-and-silver-money|Weighed silver]] continued to be important after the invention of coinage. [@abd, 1:1076–1089]

Small silver [[yehud-seals-and-coins|coins of Yehud]] bear the province's name or the names of officials, including a governor named Yehezqiyah on some issues. Samaria also issued silver coins during the late Persian period. In the Hellenistic period, the Hasmoneans issued small bronzes, and John Hyrcanus I is commonly credited with beginning the series, though some attributions are debated. Herodian rulers also issued bronzes, which were useful for small transactions. [@abd, 1:1076–1089]

## In the Bible

In Gen 23:15–16, Abraham weighs silver to buy a burial place. The shekel in that setting is a unit of weight. The list of donations in Ezra 2:69 and Neh 7:70–72 (MT 7:69–71) uses the word *darkĕmônîm*, sometimes connected with Greek drachmas and sometimes with Persian darics. The description of the money as gold has led some scholars to favor darics, while the form of the word invites the comparison with drachmas. The similar but distinct term in Ezra 8:27 is often identified with darics. [@abd, 1:1076–1089]

Matt 17:24–27 refers to a temple payment by two people and a single coin sufficient to cover both. The coin is called a *statēr* in Greek. The Tyrian silver shekel is a plausible identification because coins of that standard are associated with temple payments in later Jewish sources. The precise issue is unstated. Matt 26:15 gives Judas's payment as thirty pieces of silver and is less specific about the coins. Tyrian silver is possible here too, though the narrative gives no denomination. [@abd, 1:1076–1089]

Matt 22:19–21 identifies the Roman coin shown to Jesus as a denarius and makes Caesar's image and inscription central to the exchange. Mark 12:42 counts a widow's small offering in two *lepta*, bronze coins of low value, and explains the amount using a Roman denomination. The different units reflect overlapping currencies and the need for ancient writers to make one monetary scale intelligible in terms of another.
