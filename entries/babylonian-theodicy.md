## Overview

The Babylonian Theodicy is an Akkadian poem from ancient Mesopotamia in which a man in distress debates a friend about divine justice. It probably took shape around the end of the second millennium BCE and survives in later copies. The speakers ask why people who honor the gods can suffer while others prosper. Their exchange is one of the closest Mesopotamian comparisons to the dialogue of Job. The poem also preserves an unusual literary device in which the first signs of its stanzas identify a scholar and describe his allegiance to god and king.

## Manuscripts and Date

Several clay tablets preserve parts of the poem. The surviving copies are from the first millennium BCE, and an ancient commentary attests study of its language. A later list of kings and scholars associates the scholar Saggil-kīnam-ubbib with rulers of the late second millennium BCE, including Nebuchadnezzar I and Adad-apla-iddina. This tradition supports an approximate date around the eleventh century BCE, though it does not establish a year of composition [@lambert-bwl, 63–91].

The reconstructed poem has twenty-seven stanzas of eleven lines each. Within a stanza the same written sign begins each line. Reading these signs in sequence from stanza to stanza produces an acrostic, a message formed from opening signs or letters. The message identifies Saggil-kīnam-ubbib as a specialist in ritual incantations and presents him as devoted to god and king.

## Contents

The sufferer and his friend speak in alternate stanzas. The sufferer begins by describing his isolation and later points to people who disregard the gods yet enjoy prosperity. He challenges the expectation that honoring the gods reliably brings well-being. His friend urges patience and defends piety, insisting that humans have difficulty understanding the ways of the gods [@lambert-bwl, 63–91].

Near the end the friend agrees that the gods have given human beings a tendency toward false speech. The sufferer ends by appealing for help and mercy from the god and goddess who have abandoned him. The poem ends at the appeal, before any response or restoration is narrated. The ending gives room to both the distress voiced in the dialogue and the expectation that a god might answer.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Job and the Theodicy both sustain a debate between a sufferer and those who respond to him. Job's claim that the wicked live, grow old, and prosper (Job 21:7–16) is particularly close to the Babylonian speaker's questioning of a simple connection between right conduct and reward. The same issue appears in Ps 73:2–14 and Jer 12:1–2, where the flourishing of wrongdoers troubles the speaker. The books differ in how they develop the problem.

In Job, God eventually speaks from a whirlwind (Job 38:1–3). The Babylonian dialogue closes with the sufferer's appeal. Its form and themes offer evidence for a wider tradition of [[ane-wisdom-literature|wisdom literature]] in which speakers dispute easy answers to suffering. Ps 119:1–16 provides a biblical example of how an acrostic can structure stanzas. Its signs follow the alphabet, whereas the Babylonian poet uses them to write a message.

## Scholarly Issues

The ending has been read both as an affirmation of continued piety and as a decision to appeal despite the failure of ordinary explanations. The poem's comparison with Job rests on the structure of the debate and the arguments given to its speakers. Those features support a comparison of literary forms and intellectual traditions. The surviving manuscripts supply no evidence that the author of Job used this particular poem.
