## Overview

Anat was a goddess of the eastern Mediterranean, best known for her fierce defense of Baal and her readiness to kill in the Late Bronze Age poems of Ugarit, a city on the Syrian coast. She was also honored in New Kingdom Egypt. Names containing Anat appear in Aramaic documents from Persian-period Egypt and probably survive in personal and place names in the Hebrew Bible. Those biblical traces and the later Egyptian records make it possible to ask how the name and worship of a goddess persisted in different settings.

## Sources and Iconography

In the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]], Anat slaughters warriors in a scene that dwells on the scale of the bloodshed. In a different episode she mourns and buries [[baal|Baal]] and later seizes and destroys [[mot|Mot]], the god Death. The poems often call her *btlt* (“maiden”). The force of the title is debated, and it can describe her status as a young woman without settling questions about sexual experience. [@ktu3] [@parker-unp]

In the [[aqhat-epic|story of Aqhat]], Anat wants the hero's bow and offers him a form of immortality in return. After he refuses, she arranges an attack in which he dies. The Egyptian evidence also associates Anat with warfare. Her standing in the court of Ramesses II is reflected in the name of his daughter Bint-Anat, meaning “daughter of Anat.” [@parker-unp] [@ddd]

## In the Hebrew Bible

The designation “son of Anath” (ESV) accompanies Shamgar's name in both the narrative (Judg 3:31) and the Song of Deborah (Judg 5:6). Scholars have proposed that the phrase is a warrior's title or an inherited personal designation. Beth-anath in Naphtali (Judg 1:33) transparently contains the name, while Anathoth, the town associated with Jeremiah in Jer 1:1, has also been linked with it. Names can preserve a deity's memory without showing how the people who used them worshipped. [@ddd]

Anat's destruction of Mot includes grinding and scattering, actions that have prompted comparison with Moses' treatment of the golden calf in Exod 32:20. The contexts and the objects of destruction differ, and the claim of a direct relationship remains disputed. Anat has also been proposed as the [[queen-of-heaven|Queen of Heaven]] in Jer 7:18, where the goddess has a title but no personal name. A proposed change to the received Hebrew wording of Hos 14:8 (MT 14:9) would introduce Anat and Asherah into the verse. The conjecture is disputed and supplies no secure evidence for Anat in Hosea. [@ddd] [@parker-unp]

## Scholarly Issues

The fifth-century BCE [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine documents]] name Anat-Bethel in a collection account and Anat-Yahu in an oath record. Some scholars understand the names as designating a goddess associated with a male deity as his consort. Others see a manifestation or aspect of the named deity, sometimes called a hypostasis. The documents preserve the names and their immediate uses, while the precise relationships they imply remain debated. [@tad] [@ddd]

Anat's violence and loyalty to Baal are prominent in the Ugaritic poems. Scholars disagree about how much those poems support a description of her as a goddess of love or fertility or as Baal's sexual partner. The surviving episodes make her role as a warrior and ally easier to establish. [@parker-unp] [@ddd]
