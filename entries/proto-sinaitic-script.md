## Overview

Proto-Sinaitic is a name for early alphabetic writing represented at Serabit el-Khadim in Sinai. Related inscriptions at Wadi el-Hol in Egypt also belong to the evidence for the alphabet’s beginnings. Semitic speakers appear to have assigned sounds to pictures familiar from Egyptian writing, using the first sounds of their own words. The date, location, and social setting of the innovation remain debated. These inscriptions help explain the ancestry of the later Hebrew alphabet that shapes biblical poems arranged in alphabetical order.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Serabit el-Khadim was a mining and religious site with Egyptian inscriptions and short texts in a different script. Wadi el-Hol preserves two early alphabetic inscriptions cut into rock along a desert route. The signs are often compared with forms in Egyptian hieroglyphic or cursive writing. Many of the short texts are difficult to read, making their archaeological surroundings especially important to an estimate of date.

Acrophony assigns a sound by using the beginning of the name of the pictured object. A picture interpreted as a house can thus represent *b*, the first sound of a Semitic word for house. A form associated with an ox can represent the sound that began the word *ʾalp*, “ox.” Alan Gardiner’s 1916 reading of *lbʿlt* as a dedication “to the Lady” was an important step in recognizing the Semitic system. Individual sign identifications or readings remain debated. The strength of the proposal lies in its ability to account for a pattern across several signs.

Many scholars place the Wadi el-Hol examples in the first half of the second millennium BCE. Dates proposed for inscriptions at Serabit vary, and a single date need not apply to the entire group. Orly Goldwasser has proposed that miners in contact with Egyptian pictures devised the script. Other scholars have emphasized different settings, such as military or expedition personnel. The surviving evidence does not identify the first writers or show that the whole system appeared in one act.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ps 119 arranges groups of lines by successive letters of the Hebrew alphabet, and Lam 1 begins successive verses with its letters. This technique is called an alphabetic acrostic. It assumes an established sequence recognizable to readers or listeners. The poems belong to a much later stage of writing than Proto-Sinaitic. They illustrate what a stable alphabet made possible for poets, even though they do not describe its invention.

## Interpretive Significance

Early linear inscriptions from Canaan and the later Phoenician alphabet document developments in the writing tradition. By around the beginning of the first millennium BCE, visibly more standardized linear scripts occur, before the later differentiation of [[paleo-hebrew-script|Hebrew]] and other regional scripts. The term Proto-Canaanite is often applied to early alphabetic evidence in Canaan, though terminology and period boundaries vary.

The short texts from Sinai provide no secure evidence of Hebrew composition, Moses, or the Exodus. Their importance for biblical studies lies in the ancestry of alphabetic writing and in demonstrating that Semitic-language signs could be used within Egyptian-connected settings long before the biblical manuscripts. Establishing that writing existed is a different question from identifying the writer or date of a particular narrative.
