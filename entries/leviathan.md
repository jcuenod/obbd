## Overview

Leviathan is a sea creature in the Hebrew Bible, sometimes pictured as a serpent with many heads. A closely related figure appears in Late Bronze Age texts from Ugarit, a city on the coast of what is now Syria, where a seven-headed serpent is an enemy of the gods. Biblical writers give Leviathan different roles: an enemy God defeats, a creature humans cannot tame, and a creature at play in the sea. These differences help explain why later visions can use the same imagery without telling the same story.

## Sources and Iconography

In the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]], a speech addressed to Baal recalls his defeat of a serpent that flees and twists and has seven heads (tablet 1.5, column I, lines 1–3). Its name is written *ltn* and is commonly rendered Litan, though Lotan is also found. Elsewhere in the cycle, Anat claims victory over a seven-headed adversary described in similar terms. The serpent belongs to the wider [[chaoskampf|divine combat tradition]], in which a god battles Sea or a sea monster; [[yam|Yam]], the god whose name means Sea, is also Baal's opponent in the cycle [@cos1, 1.86] [@day-dragon, 21–25].

Images of combat with many-headed creatures are older than the surviving Ugaritic tablets. A third-millennium BCE cylinder seal from Tell Asmar in Mesopotamia is commonly cited as an example of a seven-headed dragon [@day-dragon, 44–48, 62–72]. <!-- VERIFY: Tell Asmar seal identification and dating against the object catalogue. --> A cylinder seal is a small carved roller that leaves an image when rolled across clay. The scene shows that a many-headed opponent could be pictured as well as described; it does not identify the creature by name.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Isa 27:1, Yahweh will strike Leviathan, called both “the gliding serpent” and “the coiling serpent” (NIV), and kill the sea creature. The name and the pairing of descriptions make this a particularly close verbal parallel to the Ugaritic poem, although they do not show that Isaiah used the surviving tablet. In Ps 74:12–17, the defeat lies in the past: God breaks Leviathan's heads in a series of acts that also includes setting the world's boundaries and seasons. The psalm gives no number of heads, and Isaiah's Hebrew term [[tannin]] for the sea creature is not another proper name for Leviathan. Another named sea adversary in Hebrew poetry is [[rahab-sea-monster|Rahab]] [@day-dragon, 72–75] [@ddd, 511–515].

Job invokes people ready to rouse Leviathan as he curses the day of his birth (Job 3:8). In God's speech, Leviathan cannot be hooked, traded, or made into a pet (Job 41:1–34; MT 40:25–41:26). Weapons are no match for it, fire issues from its mouth, and the speech ends by calling it king over the proud. The contrast is between what Job can control and what belongs to God. Ps 104:25–26 offers a quieter picture: ships go across the sea, and Leviathan has a place there among the creatures God made.

## Scholarly Issues

Some readers identify Job's creature as a crocodile described in exaggerated language, pointing to its teeth and scales. Others read it as a mythic creature whose description draws on recognizable animals and on ancient imagery of a king mastering dangerous creatures. Egyptian royal hunting scenes have been proposed as background for that reading. <!-- VERIFY: identify the Egyptian scenes and the study linking them specifically to Leviathan in Job. --> The accounts are not mutually exclusive: crocodile features can help give shape to a serpent that breathes fire.

In Psalm 104, the Hebrew can be read as Leviathan playing in the sea or as God playing with Leviathan. On the second reading, God does what Job cannot: treat the formidable creature as a plaything. Whether the psalm deliberately counters the older combat story or simply uses the creature differently remains debated.

In later apocalyptic literature, which uses visions to disclose hidden realities or coming judgment, Leviathan takes on further roles. In [[first-enoch|1 Enoch]] (1 En. 60:7–9), the female Leviathan lives in the sea's depths, apart from the male [[behemoth|Behemoth]] on dry land. In 2 Esd 6:49–52, part of the Jewish work known as 4 Ezra, the two are assigned separate habitats and kept to be eaten when God chooses. Revelation gives seven heads to both the red dragon and the beast that rises from the sea (Rev 12:3; 13:1). These figures recall the older imagery of many-headed opposition, but neither is named Leviathan.
