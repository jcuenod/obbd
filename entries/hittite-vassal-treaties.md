## Overview

In the Late Bronze Age, Hittite great kings used written agreements to define obligations for subordinate rulers in Anatolia and Syria. The treaties often recount the previous relationship, demand loyalty, name divine witnesses, and give blessings or curses. Their texts survive chiefly on clay tablets from Ḫattuša, with one important treaty on bronze. They have been widely compared with biblical covenants, especially the historical review and sanctions in Deuteronomy and the ceremony in Joshua 24. The comparison helps explain these forms while leaving the date and development of the biblical texts open.

## Manuscripts and Date

The capital at Ḫattuša yielded much of the treaty evidence from the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE. Examples include Šuppiluliuma I's treaty with Aziru of Amurru and Muršili II's treaty with Aziru's descendant Tuppi-Teššub. A bronze tablet found at Ḫattuša in 1986 records Tudḫaliya IV's agreement with Kurunta of Tarḫuntašša. It is a rare surviving treaty on metal and illustrates the use of materials beyond clay for documents of state [@beckman-hdt].

## Contents

A treaty may identify the great king, review earlier contacts and benefactions, specify duties, call on gods as witnesses, and invoke consequences for obedience or disloyalty. Some texts also require deposit in a sanctuary and periodic reading aloud. Tuppi-Teššub's treaty, for example, relates his obligations to the history of the royal houses. Its terms give exclusive loyalty a political meaning. The contents and order vary across documents. The [[egyptian-hittite-peace-treaty|agreement with Egypt]] chiefly expresses reciprocal commitments between equal powers and provides a different diplomatic example.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 20:2 combines the speaker's self-identification with a reminder of rescue from Egypt. It has been compared with treaty introductions and their accounts of past benefits. Exod 20:1–17 has also been read as a set of loyalty stipulations. The connection is debated because the Decalogue has its own form and lacks several elements found in extended treaties. A broader comparison takes in Deut 1:6–3:29, with a long historical review before the laws, and Deut 28, with [[blessings-and-curses|blessings and curses]] as sanctions.

Deut 31:9–13, 24–26 calls for the public reading of the law and its deposit beside the ark. Exod 25:16 places the testimony inside the ark. Such passages make sense alongside ancient provisions for protection and repeated reading of the treaty text. Deut 30:19 calls heaven and earth to witness. Hittite witness lists can name parts of the natural world alongside deities, according them a different place in the treaty's religious setting. Josh 24:1–28 brings historical recital, loyalty, witnesses, and a written record into a single covenant ceremony.

## Scholarly Issues

George Mendenhall and others used the resemblances to explain the shape of biblical [[covenant-and-treaty-form|covenants]], and some took them as support for early composition. Detailed parallels with first-millennium documents, especially [[esarhaddon-succession-treaty|Esarhaddon's succession oaths]], are now central to discussions of Deuteronomy's curses. Joshua Berman and others have pointed to features such as the way history is retold in Hittite treaty prologues. These arguments can illuminate different aspects of the biblical text. The place of an individual biblical passage in literary history cannot be settled from the general treaty form alone.
