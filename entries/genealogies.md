## Overview

Genealogies express relationships through descent. A linear genealogy follows a single line from one generation to the next, while a segmented genealogy sets out several lines branching from a common ancestor. Both forms occur in the Bible and serve purposes that include tracing an inheritance, defining a community, or linking a ruler with a founding family. Ancient Near Eastern king lists and dynastic genealogies offer comparisons for the way scribes arranged the past, especially where a list uses repeated numbers, exceptional lifespans, or carefully selected lines of succession.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]] arranges the past as a succession of cities and kings. Some versions give spectacularly long reigns to early kings before a flood. Since a king list follows officeholders, it can connect rulers from different families and even different cities. The length of the reigns before the flood nevertheless provides a point of comparison with the exceptional lifespans before the flood in Genesis.

The Assyrian King List opens with seventeen “kings who lived in tents” and preserves a section listing ancestors in reverse order. Some names in the opening section also occur in the Genealogy of the Hammurabi Dynasty, an Old Babylonian text that commemorates royal predecessors. Abraham Malamat compared these sources with biblical genealogies. Ancient dynastic lists could create a sense of continuity by organizing traditions about descent and succession, even when the precise connections between names are difficult to recover.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 4:17–22 traces Cain's descendants and branches at Lamech's family to associate particular figures with pastoral life, music, and metalworking. Gen 5:1–32 follows a line from Adam to Noah across ten generations and records ages at fatherhood and at death. Gen 11:10–26 follows the line from Shem to Abram over another ten generations when the endpoints are counted. The [[table-of-nations|Table of Nations]] in Gen 10 uses a branching form to portray relations among peoples and territories. The Edomite list in Gen 36:31–39 follows kings, with several different home towns and fathers given, illustrating the distinction between a sequence of rulers and a line of descent.

The list at the end of Ruth traces ten generations from Perez to David, placing Ruth's son Obed in the royal line (Ruth 4:18–22). First Chronicles begins with Adam and devotes nine chapters to ancestry, territories, and community offices (1 Chr 1–9). In Ezra 2:59–63, some returnees cannot prove their ancestry. Would-be priests whose names are missing from the register are barred from priestly duties and the holy food. The list has practical consequences for membership and entitlement in this setting.

## Interpretive Significance

Genealogies can omit intermediate generations and arrange names to make a point. Matthew announces three groups of fourteen in Jesus's genealogy and organizes them around Abraham, David, the exile, and the Messiah (Matt 1:1–17). Luke traces Jesus's ancestry back to Adam and finally to God (Luke 3:23–38). Their arrangements give the reader different ways to place Jesus's ancestry within the larger biblical story.

The Masoretic Text, Samaritan Pentateuch, and Septuagint transmit different sets of ages in Gen 5 and 11. Adding ages can yield a chronology for the sequence as presented in a particular version. Any historical calculation that treats such totals as elapsed time, such as the chronology of James Ussher, depends on whether the dates correspond to continuous historical generations and on the textual readings chosen. These questions are distinct from what a genealogy communicates about family, office, or God's dealings with a people.
