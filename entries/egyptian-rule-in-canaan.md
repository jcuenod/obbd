## Overview

New Kingdom Egypt controlled much of Canaan for several centuries, beginning with the expansion of the Eighteenth Dynasty and ending during the twelfth century BCE. Pharaohs governed through local rulers, royal officials, and garrisons. Letters and excavations reveal both imperial institutions and the adoption of Egyptian styles by local communities. This system was still in place when an Egyptian monument first named Israel. It provides a historical setting for Israel's emergence and raises a question about why biblical accounts of Canaan have so little to say about Egyptian rule there.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the fifteenth century BCE, Thutmose III defeated a coalition of rulers at Megiddo. His annals present the campaign as a royal victory and document Egypt's growing influence over the Levant. In the fourteenth century BCE, the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] show that local rulers still governed cities, appealed to pharaoh, and communicated with Egyptian commissioners. The Akkadian title *rābiṣu* designates such an official in this correspondence. The letters show how courts in Canaan pursued local interests within Egypt's imperial system [@ael, 2:29–35] [@moran-amarna, EA 287–290].

The Ramesside centuries brought a visible Egyptian presence at sites such as [[gaza|Gaza]], Jaffa, [[beth-shean|Beth-shean]], and Aphek. Seti I's stelae and a lintel naming Ramesses III at Beth-shean attest royal and official activity there. At Karnak, reliefs of Seti I show the chain of forts on the route across northern Sinai known as the Ways of Horus. Egyptian-style buildings and objects have also been documented at Tel Mor, Deir el-Balah, and Tell el-Farʿah South. The evidence differs by site. Some contexts point to resident Egyptian officials or military personnel, while others show local use of Egyptian practices and objects [@anet, 253–255] [@neaehl, 1:216–223, 5:1617–1622].

The [[merneptah-stele|Merneptah Stele]], around 1208 BCE, names Ashkelon, Gezer, Yanoam, and a group called Israel in its account of victory. Its spelling treats Israel as a people. Egyptian power diminished during the twelfth century BCE. A royal statue base naming Ramesses VI found at Megiddo and the dating of the last Egyptian fortress at Jaffa show that the process extended well into that century. Whether these sites lost their Egyptian presence at the same time remains uncertain [@ael, 2:73–78] [@neaehl, 5:1577–1581].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Judg 1:27–29 says that Beth-shean, Megiddo, and Gezer remained in Canaanite hands. Josh 17:11–16 similarly presents Beth-shean and the Jezreel Valley as centers of Canaanite strength. Earlier Egyptian records independently establish the strategic importance of these places. The biblical narratives attribute their strength to local populations and refer to their chariots without naming pharaoh's administration. Exod 13:17 describes a coastal route from Egypt as the way of the land of the Philistines. Its geographical setting can be compared with the fortified route across northern Sinai, although the biblical name reflects a different political description.

## Interpretive Significance

Scholars have proposed that the boundaries in Num 34:1–12 preserve memories of an Egyptian province of Canaan. The provenance and date of this border list remain debated. Another proposal connects the waters of Nephtoah in Josh 15:9 and 18:15 with a place called the wells of Merneptah in an Egyptian papyrus. The phonetic and geographical identification remains uncertain. Gen 10:6 groups Canaan with Egypt and other lands as children of Ham. Some connect that grouping with older political ties, and the genealogy gives no explicit historical explanation.

Egyptian objects can reach a site by several routes. Carolyn Higginbotham emphasized the adoption of Egyptian styles by Canaanite elites. A fortress or an inscription naming an Egyptian official gives stronger evidence of direct administration than isolated objects in Egyptian styles. The material evidence for Egypt in Canaan is substantial despite the biblical silence. Scholars variously explain that silence in terms of selective memory, the purposes of the narratives, or the distance in time between Egyptian rule and the composition of the biblical accounts. The overlap between Egyptian rule and the [[emergence-of-israel|first attestation of Israel]] is firmer than reconstructions of what the biblical narratives remember about it.
