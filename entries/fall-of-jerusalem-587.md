## Overview

The Babylonian army captured Jerusalem twice in the early sixth century BCE. In 597 BCE, King Jehoiachin surrendered and was taken to Babylonia with members of the court and other residents. After the rebellion of his successor Zedekiah, the Babylonians returned, destroyed the city and temple, and ended Judah's monarchy in 587 or 586 BCE. A Babylonian chronicle dates the first capture. Biblical accounts and archaeological remains are the principal evidence for the second. The destruction is the setting for texts about siege, displacement, and communal grief, including portions of Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Lamentations.

## Sources

The [[nebuchadnezzar-chronicle|Nebuchadnezzar Chronicle]] dates the capture of the city of Judah to the second of Adar in the king's seventh year, in March 597 BCE. It reports the removal of one king and appointment of another. Later palace documents independently name the deported king [[jehoiachin|Jehoiachin]] as a recipient of rations. No surviving Babylonian chronicle records the second capture [@abc, 102] [@anet, 308].

Excavations in the [[city-of-david|City of David]] have uncovered evidence of fire and destruction in buildings dated to the end of the monarchy. Burned deposits from Area G preserve pottery and hardened clay seal impressions. Arrowheads of types associated with the period have also been found in Jerusalem. At Lachish, the destruction of Level II gives evidence for severe damage in Judah's western foothills. The deposits help establish the scale of damage, while their exact year depends on historical reconstruction [@neaehl, 2:708–709].

## Reconstruction

After the surrender of 597 BCE, [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] installed Zedekiah as king. Zedekiah's subsequent revolt led to a new siege. Scholars differ between 587 and 586 BCE for the city's destruction because the dates in Kings, Jeremiah, and Babylonian evidence can be correlated using different conventions for counting regnal years. The difference concerns the placement of the event in a modern calendar. Both reconstructions date the destruction about a decade after the first capture.

Judah lost its royal government and many people were deported, but substantial numbers remained in the region. Mizpah became a center of administration under Gedaliah. The collapse of the capital and the continued occupation of other parts of Judah are both important for understanding the [[babylonian-exile|Babylonian exile]].

## Biblical Accounts

2 Kgs 24:10–17 describes the first siege, Jehoiachin's surrender, and the installation of Zedekiah. 2 Kgs 25:1–26 and Jer 39:1–10 describe the siege under Zedekiah, the breach of the city, the king's capture, and the removal or death of many inhabitants. Ezek 24:1–2 dates the beginning of the siege, while Ezek 33:21 reports that a fugitive brought news of the city's fall to the exiles.

Jer 52:28–30 gives figures for three removals totaling 4,600, while 2 Kgs 24:14–16 gives a larger figure for the first capture alone. The figures may count different categories of people or derive from different sources. Their exact relationship remains uncertain. Jer 34:7 names Lachish and Azekah as the surviving fortified towns during the Babylonian campaign. The [[lachish-letters|Lachish letters]] suggest the strain on local communications, although the report that Azekah could not be seen need not mean that it had already fallen.

Lam 2:1–9 gives poetic expression to the loss of walls, rulers, and sanctuary. Ps 137:7 remembers Edom taking a hostile stance on the day of Jerusalem. These texts express consequences of the destruction through grief and accusation. They belong alongside records of the siege while serving different purposes.
