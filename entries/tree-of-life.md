## Overview

The tree of life stands in the garden in Genesis, where access to it promises lasting life. Proverbs uses the same expression for wisdom, fulfilled desire, and healing speech. In Ezekiel and Revelation, trees beside life-giving rivers are part of a vision of restoration and healing. Ancient Near Eastern stories about life-giving foods and images of sacred trees provide background for these descriptions. Their varied meanings help explain how the image could express life as a gift, a mark of flourishing, or a hope for the future.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In Tablet XI of the [[gilgamesh-epic|Epic of Gilgamesh]], the hero obtains a plant that can restore youth. A snake takes it before he can make use of it. In the story of [[adapa|Adapa]], a sage refuses bread and water offered in heaven, missing an opportunity to gain life. These narratives use different substances and settings to explore the limits of human access to life. They are thematic parallels to Genesis.

Levantine objects show stylized trees flanked by goats or other animals. Examples occur on pottery and the [[taanach-cult-stand|Taanach cult stand]]. Some scholars associate these trees with [[asherah|Asherah]], while others stress that an image without a text can carry several meanings. The [[assyrian-sacred-tree|Assyrian sacred tree]] belongs to a related but distinct body of imagery whose meaning remains debated. In Egypt, funerary art sometimes shows a goddess in a tree giving water or nourishment to the deceased, a separate way of expressing the life-giving capacity of a tree.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 2:9 places the tree of life in the [[garden-of-god|garden of Eden]]. Gen 3:22–24 associates eating from it with living forever and ends with cherubim guarding access after the humans leave. The tree is a distinguishing feature of the garden and gives urgency to the loss of access. The Genesis narrative leaves much about the tree's appearance and manner of giving life unstated.

Prov 3:18 calls wisdom a tree of life for those who grasp her. Prov 11:30 uses the image for the fruit of the righteous, Prov 13:12 for a desire fulfilled, and Prov 15:4 for gentle or healing speech. These passages use the tree as an image of flourishing within ordinary life. Exod 25:31–36 gives the [[menorah|lampstand]] branches and almond-shaped decorations, a form that has invited comparison with stylized trees.

## Interpretive Significance

Ezek 47:12 describes trees growing beside a river from the temple, bearing frequent fruit and leaves for healing. Rev 22:2, 14 brings the tree of life into the vision of the renewed city, where its leaves heal the nations and access is granted to the blessed. The vision can be read with both Genesis and Ezekiel in view. The sequence moves from guarded access in Eden to healing and restored access at the conclusion of Revelation.
