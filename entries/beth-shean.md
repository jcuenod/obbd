## Overview

Beth-shean stood near the junction of the Jezreel and Jordan valleys, below Mount Gilboa. In the Late Bronze Age and early Iron Age it served as an Egyptian administrative center. Its mound preserves Egyptian inscriptions and buildings as well as remains from the subsequent Iron Age town. In the Bible, Beth-shean is remembered as a Canaanite city and as the place where the Philistines displayed Saul's body. The later city of Scythopolis developed around the mound and became one of the cities associated with the Decapolis in the Roman period.

## Location and Identification

The ancient mound lies at modern Beit She'an, where routes through the Jezreel Valley meet the Jordan Valley. Its location gave it access both to the coast by way of the inland valleys and to routes along or across the Jordan. The name Beth-shean appears in Egyptian as well as biblical sources. The later name Scythopolis refers to an urban center that included extensive areas around the foot of the mound. [@neaehl, 1:214–235]

## Archaeological History

University of Pennsylvania excavations in the 1920s and early 1930s uncovered temples, houses, and Egyptian royal inscriptions. Excavations directed by Amihai Mazar from 1989 to 1996 refined the sequence of occupation. Egyptian-style administrative and residential buildings belong to the period of New Kingdom control. Royal monuments found at the site include stelae of Seti I and Ramesses II and a statue of Ramesses III. An inscription of Seti I refers to people called [[habiru|ʿApiru]], a designation found in other texts of the period. [@neaehl, 1:214–235] [@anet, 255]

The northern cemetery yielded clay coffins with modeled human features. Some scholars attributed them to mercenaries associated with the Sea Peoples. Egyptian-style burial among a population with varied origins is also possible and fits the site's role as a garrison. The shape of the coffins by itself cannot identify the people laid in them. The site continued into the Iron Age after Egyptian control ended, and later occupation eventually included a substantial Roman and Byzantine lower city. [@neaehl, 1:214–235] [@mazar-archaeology, 279–287, 296–300]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 17:11–16 and Judg 1:27 include Beth-shean among the towns the Israelites did not initially take from the Canaanites. Joshua places the difficulty within a wider description of the valley cities and their chariots. In 1 Sam 31:8–13, the [[philistines|Philistines]] fasten Saul's body to the wall of Beth-shean and place his armor in the temple of the Ashtaroth, the goddesses associated with [[astarte|Astarte]]. The different account in 1 Chr 10:10 mentions a temple of [[dagon|Dagon]] without naming the town. The early excavators associated two sanctuary buildings at Beth-shean with these passages. The buildings lack secure dedications connecting them with either deity, and these identifications remain speculative.

Beth-shean appears in the administrative list in 1 Kgs 4:12. It is usually identified in the Karnak list connected with [[shoshenq-i|Shoshenq I]], the ruler commonly identified with Shishak in 1 Kgs 14:25–26. The Karnak text and Kings give different kinds of evidence for the campaign, and the biblical text does not list Beth-shean among its targets. Scythopolis appears by name in 2 Macc 12:29–31, which praises the city's treatment of its Jewish residents. Mark 5:20 refers more broadly to the Decapolis region. [@neaehl, 1:214–235] [@anet, 263–264]
