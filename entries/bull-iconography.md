## Overview

Images of bulls and calves were widespread in the ancient Near East, especially in the Bronze and Iron Ages. In Syria and the Levant, bulls could express strength, identify a god's sphere of power, or provide a platform on which a deity stood. These uses give context to the golden calves in Exodus and Kings. Biblical writers condemn the calves, but scholars continue to debate whether the images in Israel's sanctuaries represented Israel's God, served as his supports, or had another role.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Ugaritic texts call the god [[el|El]] “Bull El,” linking a divine title with the power of the animal. Elsewhere in Syrian and Anatolian art the storm god can stand on a bull. Such images distinguish the god's human-shaped figure from the animal beneath him. This gives a clear example in which a bull serves as a divine attribute or support [@parker-unp, 13, 91, 100–101, 128] [@anep, figs. 500–501].

Other objects show an animal by itself. A Middle Bronze Age sanctuary at Ashkelon yielded a bronze calf once covered with silver. The [[bull-site-figurine|bronze bull from the Samarian hills]] offers another southern Levantine comparison, although it was found on the surface of a site with early Iron Age material. A free-standing bull could have several meanings, and the surviving examples do not always carry inscriptions or retain their original context [@king-stager-life, 172–173, fig. 84] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 189–195].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 32:1–6 describes Aaron's making a calf at Sinai and announcing a feast for YHWH. The people's declaration identifies the image with the power that brought Israel from Egypt. A similar declaration occurs when Jeroboam sets up calves at [[bethel|Bethel]] and [[dan|Dan]] in 1 Kgs 12:26–33. Many interpreters understand Exodus in light of criticism of those northern sanctuaries, although the direction and literary history of the relationship are debated.

Hos 8:5–6 stresses that the calf of Samaria was made by a craftsman and denies that it is a god. Hos 13:2 depicts people kissing calves, an action of reverence. Num 23:22 and 24:8 use the strength of a wild ox as an image of divine power, illustrating a different use of cattle imagery. The proposed association of the title “Mighty One of Jacob” (Gen 49:24, ESV) with a word for a bull remains disputed.

## Interpretive Significance

Some scholars take the biblical calves to have been images that represented YHWH. Others compare them with the bulls that support storm gods in Near Eastern art and suggest that they served as pedestals beneath a deity conceived as invisible. The surviving biblical criticism shows how antagonists represented the practice. The language of Exodus connects the feast with YHWH but does not set out the original worshipers' understanding of the object. Comparative images make both interpretations intelligible and leave the form and meaning of the northern cult equipment open to debate.
