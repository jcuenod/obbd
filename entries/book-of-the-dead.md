## Overview

The Book of the Dead is the modern name for a collection of Egyptian funerary texts used from the New Kingdom into the Roman period. Scribes copied selected spells onto papyri and other burial objects to guide and protect the deceased. One famous spell describes judgment before Osiris, accompanying a scene in which the deceased's heart is weighed. Biblical passages about divine weighing or declarations of innocence make useful comparisons for particular images and forms. They need to be read in their own settings, especially where they deal with justice in this life or entry to a sanctuary.

## Manuscripts and Date

The Egyptian title is commonly translated as “Coming Forth by Day.” The collection took shape by the beginning of the New Kingdom, in the middle of the second millennium BCE, drawing on older funerary traditions. Surviving papyri often contain pictures alongside their texts, and some spells also appear on coffins, wrappings, or burial equipment. Papyri were prepared with different selections of texts. A more regular order developed in the first millennium BCE, in the tradition usually called the Saite recension, named for the period of the dynasty based at Sais [@ael, 2:119–123] [@anet, 31–36].

## Contents

The spells give the deceased words and knowledge for overcoming dangers, entering protected places, and taking part in the life of the gods. In Spell 125, the deceased addresses divine judges and declares freedom from wrongdoing. The statements, often called the negative confession, deny a range of offenses such as theft and unjust conduct. They are declarations of innocence made as part of obtaining a favorable judgment.

The associated pictures often show a balance with the deceased's heart on one side and an emblem of [[maat|Maat, or right order]], on the other. In some copies Anubis attends the scales, Thoth records the outcome, and the deity Ammit waits by the balance. A favorable result allows the deceased to approach Osiris. A related spell, often written on a beetle-shaped amulet placed with the body, asks the heart to give no hostile testimony [@ael, 2:124–132].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Job's defense lists offenses that he denies having committed, and Job 31:6 asks God to weigh him in a fair balance. Prov 16:2, 21:2, and 24:12 also speak of divine weighing as a way of seeing through human judgment and evaluating motives or hearts. Egyptian judgment scenes give a concrete comparison for these images. Job's speech concerns his present ordeal, while Proverbs concerns moral evaluation without describing a proceeding after death.

Ps 15:1–5 and Ps 24:3–6 describe acceptable conduct in connection with approaching a holy place. Deut 26:13–14 requires a declaration of compliance when presenting the tithe, including denials of certain actions. Scholars have compared these forms with the Egyptian declarations, but the purposes differ and a direct relationship remains uncertain. Dan 5:27 likewise uses weighing as a metaphor in pronouncing judgment on a king, without identifying the source of the image.

## Scholarly Issues

Egyptian texts and visual forms circulated beyond Egypt, and Egyptian-style objects found in the Levant make such contacts plausible. Wisdom texts in Egypt also used images of scales and divine assessment. These are possible settings for the spread of a motif, which can travel independently of a particular funerary papyrus. The Book of the Dead itself was a set of resources for burial and the afterlife, with significant variation from copy to copy.
