## Overview

Cosmology concerns how people picture the structure of the world. Ancient Near Eastern texts and images describe heavens above, land amid waters, and regions beneath the ground. Sources from Mesopotamia and Egypt give several ways of arranging these features, and biblical writers use some of the same images. They can portray the sky as a tent, the earth as a building, and the sea as water held behind doors. Comparing these pictures helps readers recognize the physical world presupposed by biblical poetry and narrative and see how each text uses that world to make a different point.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

A first-millennium BCE Mesopotamian text describes three heavens of different stones and several regions of the earth. Its lowest heaven carries the stars, and the middle region below the surface includes the *apsû*, a subterranean body of fresh water. Other Mesopotamian texts arrange the cosmic regions differently. The [[babylonian-map-of-the-world|Babylonian Map of the World]] depicts the inhabited region horizontally, surrounded by water labeled the bitter river. Its principal concern is the world around Babylonia and what may lie beyond familiar places [@horowitz-cosmic-geography, 3–19, 243–251].

The Babylonian creation poem [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]] connects the world's arrangement with the rise of Marduk to divine kingship. Marduk makes part of the defeated sea deity Tiamat into a covering and restrains her waters. The poem also portrays him setting up the patterns by which the heavenly bodies mark time. These scenes imagine the regularities of the world through a story of divine rule [@lambert-bcm, 84–107, 169–200].

In [[egyptian-cosmology|Egyptian sources]], the sky can appear in human form as the goddess Nut arched over Geb, the earth god. In images such as one preserved in the Greenfield Papyrus, Shu, the god of air, holds Nut apart from Geb. In accounts of the world's beginning, Nun represents the waters from which ordered life emerges. The combination of image and story shows how the world's parts could also be conceived as divine persons [@cos1, 1.14–1.16] [@keel-symbolism, 26–47].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 1:1–2:3 describes God organizing the world through a sequence of separations and assignments. God sets a *rāqîaʿ* (“expanse” or “dome”) between the waters and gathers the lower waters so that dry land appears (Gen 1:6–8). The heavens and waters remain distinguishable features of the ordered world. In the flood narrative, the fountains of the deep and openings of the heavens release water (Gen 7:11). The [[firmament|expanse]] and the [[waters-above-and-below|waters above and below]] belong to a shared stock of physical images, while the two narratives use them for different purposes.

Other passages draw on the language of building and habitation. Ps 104:2–9 portrays God spreading the heavens like a tent, securing the earth on its foundations, and setting a boundary for the waters. Job 38:4–11 pictures God laying the earth's foundations and closing the sea behind doors. In Prov 8:22–31, Wisdom is present as God fixes the heavens, assigns the sea a limit, and sets the earth's foundations. These passages give the limits of sea and land a place in accounts of divine power and wisdom.

Still other pictures appear alongside them. Job 26:7–14 describes God hanging the earth over nothing, binding up water in clouds, and making the pillars of heaven tremble. Isa 40:22 portrays God above the circle of the earth, stretching out the sky like a tent. The circle may describe the visible horizon or the bounded earth. A single poem can move between images of suspension, containment, and architecture.

A vertical arrangement also appears in lists that refer to the whole created order. Exod 20:4 speaks of heaven above, earth below, and water under the earth when describing the scope of the prohibition on images. Phil 2:10 names beings in heaven, on earth, and under the earth to express the full extent of homage to Jesus. Biblical writers sometimes associate the region under the earth with [[sheol-and-the-netherworld|the abode of the dead]]. The passages use the language of spatial arrangement to make statements about worship and authority.

## Interpretive Significance

“Three-tiered universe” is a convenient way of referring to the recurring distinction between heaven, earth, and what lies below. The texts also use several spatial pictures, including the layered waters, the earth on foundations, and the earth hanging over nothing. Their purpose is often local to a poem or narrative. The reader can ask what a particular text communicates by giving water a boundary or making the heavens a dwelling.

Most historians of biblical literature understand these descriptions in relation to the ancient physical world imagined by Israel's neighbors. The broad resemblances establish a context in which the biblical texts were written and heard. Literary dependence on a particular Mesopotamian or Egyptian work requires more specific evidence. The biblical passages concern the world as seen and understood in antiquity and are best read within that historical setting.
