## Overview

Flavius Josephus was a Judean priest and historian who wrote in Greek in Rome during the late first century CE. His four surviving works describe Jewish history, customs and the revolt against Roman rule in which he took part. His accounts are a principal source for the Herodian rulers, the Jerusalem temple and the officials and religious groups who appear in the New Testament. Comparison with other evidence requires attention to his sources, his literary aims and his position under the Flavian emperors.

## Manuscripts and Date

Josephus was born in 37 CE and commanded forces in Galilee during the [[first-jewish-revolt|revolt against Rome]]. After surrender he came under Flavian patronage and took the name Flavius. His Greek *Jewish War* was completed in the 70s CE. He refers to an earlier account in his ancestral language, which does not survive. The *Jewish Antiquities* is dated to 93 or 94 CE, and the *Life* and *Against Apion* belong to the later part of his career. The Greek works survive through later manuscript copying, with portions also transmitted in translations and quotations. [@abd]

## Contents

*War* traces the conflict and its background, including the destruction of [[herods-temple|Herod’s temple]] and the siege of [[masada|Masada]]. *Antiquities* begins with creation, retells biblical history and carries the account to the eve of the revolt. The *Life* defends Josephus’s record, with much attention to rival accounts of his conduct in Galilee. *Against Apion* defends the antiquity and customs of the Jews in argument with Greek authors. Each work selects evidence to serve its own audience and purpose. [@abd]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josephus and Mark 6:17–29 both describe the execution of John the Baptist by Herod Antipas. Josephus emphasizes the tetrarch’s fear of popular mobilization, while Mark gives the marriage dispute with Herodias and her role in the execution. Josephus’s identification of Herodias’s first husband differs from Mark’s. Acts 12:20–23 and Josephus describe Agrippa I’s final illness after acclamation at Caesarea, though they frame the episode differently. Josephus also supplies context for Pilate in Mark 15:1–15, for Felix and Drusilla in Acts 24:24 and for the religious disagreements reflected in Acts 23:6–8. [@abd]

The order of Theudas and Judas the Galilean in Acts 5:36–37 differs from Josephus’s dates. His description of an Egyptian prophet also gives a comparison for Acts 21:38, which has a different figure for the followers. His account places the [[census-of-quirinius|census under Quirinius]] in 6 CE, creating a chronological difficulty for Luke 2:1–2 in the setting of its infancy narrative. Proposed literary use of Josephus in Luke–Acts remains a minority position. [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

The notice on Jesus commonly called the Testimonium Flavianum occurs in *Antiquities* 18.63–64. Most specialists understand the surviving text as a Josephus passage with later Christian alterations. Some defend more of the transmitted wording, while others regard the entire notice as secondary. The notice of the death of James in *Antiquities* 20.200, relevant to Gal 1:19, and the notice of John the Baptist are generally accepted as authentic. Josephus’s numbers for armies, crowds and casualties also need comparison with other evidence. His position under Roman patronage and his own role in the revolt affected the account, but their implications differ from passage to passage. [@abd]
