## Overview

The emergence of Israel concerns how the people first named as Israel in an Egyptian inscription developed in Canaan near the end of the Late Bronze Age. Archaeological surveys document a rise in the number of small highland settlements during the early Iron Age. The Bible preserves accounts of conquest, settlement and continuing life alongside other peoples. Most scholars understand early Israel as emerging largely from populations already in Canaan, while the contributions of mobile groups, incomers and particular conflicts remain debated.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[merneptah-stele|Merneptah Stele]] of c. 1208 BCE is the earliest generally accepted inscription naming Israel. An Egyptian sign classifies Israel as a people, while other names in the same passage are marked as places. The inscription places Israel among groups and places in Canaan in an account of Egyptian victories. It leaves Israel's exact location and political organization unspecified. [@ael] [@anet]

Surveys show many small sites appearing in the central highlands in the early Iron Age. Settlements and objects preserve continuities with older practices in Canaan, and some features change in distribution. Some scholars explain the growth partly through mobile herders establishing permanent villages. Others emphasize movement from the lowlands or a mixture of different local and immigrant populations. Food remains, house designs and pottery help track ways of living, while their use to locate [[ethnicity-and-material-culture|ethnic boundaries]] remains disputed. [@mazar-archaeology] [@king-stager-life]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Josh 1–12 presents campaigns against cities and coalitions and describes the burning of Hazor (Josh 11:10–13). Judg 1:19, 21, 27–35, by contrast, portrays groups remaining side by side in many places. These accounts give different perspectives on Israel's occupation of the land. Both are literary presentations, and comparison with the archaeology requires attention to the region and site involved.

The remains of [[jericho|Jericho]] offer no secure Late Bronze fortified city matching the conquest narrative, and the main site proposed for [[ai|Ai]] has a large gap between its Early Bronze city and a small Iron Age settlement. Alternative datings or site identifications have been proposed, but their ability to explain Josh 8:28 and the account of Jericho is disputed. At [[hazor|Hazor]], a major Late Bronze city really was destroyed. The date and perpetrators of that destruction remain debated. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

## Interpretive Significance

Older models include William F. Albright's unified conquest, Albrecht Alt and Martin Noth's gradual settlement by pastoral groups, and the social revolt models associated with George Mendenhall and Norman Gottwald. A unified military explanation has been weakened by the different histories of the sites. Gradual settlement remains part of some newer explanations, although the identity and origins of the settlers cannot be assumed. Evidence for a coordinated peasant revolt is limited. [@abd] [@mazar-archaeology]

Recent reconstructions more commonly describe a process of *ethnogenesis*, or the formation of a new group identity. Israel Finkelstein, William Dever, Ann Killebrew and other scholars assign different weight to pastoralism, lowland communities and links with populations beyond Canaan. The widespread continuity with local material culture supports a largely indigenous origin. That development allows room for varied groups and traditions, including ones associated with the [[exodus-historical-questions|memory of departure from Egypt]].
