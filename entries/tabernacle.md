## Overview

The tabernacle is the transportable sanctuary described in Exodus and Numbers. In the Priestly account, the *miškān*, or dwelling, houses the ark, provides for sacrificial worship, and gives God's presence a place among Israel's camps. Another account describes Moses meeting God at a tent outside the camp. Texts and images from Egypt and Mesopotamia show that portable structures could serve royal or ritual purposes. They offer comparisons for the biblical descriptions, though the construction and use of the Israelite tabernacle are known from literary sources.

## Archaeological Evidence

Portable structures seldom survive in a form that permits their plan and use to be determined. Administrative tablets from second-millennium BCE Mari record tents and equipment interpreted by some researchers as belonging to movable sanctuaries. Reliefs of Ramesses II's camp at the battle of Kadesh depict a rectangular enclosure and a prominent royal tent. Its axial arrangement and the partitioning of the royal space have been compared with the enclosure and inner rooms in Exodus. The Egyptian relief depicts a royal camp and its own traditions of royal imagery. It offers evidence for the broader architectural and social possibilities of tents [@homan-tents, 111–118].

A later sanctuary can also be described in terms familiar from permanent temple worship. The similarities between the tabernacle and temple descriptions have prompted proposals ranging from preservation of a traditional tent shrine to literary projection of temple arrangements into the wilderness. Comparanda contribute to the plausibility of particular features. Their forms and dates leave the question of the biblical sanctuary's historical development open [@hurowitz-exalted-house, 168–170].

## In the Bible

In Exod 25:8–9, God asks Israel to make a sanctuary so that he may dwell among them and supplies its pattern. Exodus 25–31 specifies the curtains, frames, altar, priestly vestments, and ritual furnishings. A veil screens the inner room and its ark. Exodus 35–40 recounts the collection of materials and construction. Moses inspects the completed work (Exod 39:32, 43), and the cloud and God's glory then fill the dwelling (Exod 40:34–38). The rising cloud guides departure. Numbers 2 puts the tent within the ordered camp, with the Levites and the other tribes assigned their places around it.

The tent in Exod 33:7–11 stands outside the camp. Moses goes there to consult God, and Joshua remains at the tent. Many interpreters treat the location, access, and limited equipment as signs of a distinct tradition, although the expression “tent of meeting” is also used for the Priestly sanctuary. In 2 Sam 7:6 the divine speech contrasts God's previous journeys in a tent with David's proposal for a permanent house.

Heb 9:1–10 interprets the arrangement of the compartments and restrictions on access as figures for a larger argument about priesthood and worship. Its use of the tabernacle shows how the wilderness sanctuary could become a model for reflection on approach to God long after the narrative setting.
