## Overview

Ancient Mesopotamian texts describe gods, kings, and other powerful beings as possessing *melammu*, a brilliance that inspires fear. Assyrian royal inscriptions use this language to explain why opponents flee or submit. The Hebrew Bible speaks of YHWH's *kābôd* (“glory”) in scenes of fire, cloud, and radiant light, and uses related language for royal splendor. These images help readers distinguish visible signs of divine presence from descriptions of honor and understand how biblical writers could use the language of kingship to present God's majesty.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Akkadian *melammu* names a fearsome radiance or splendor. Gods, their shrines, kings, and formidable creatures can possess it. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, the guardian Humbaba's several auras give a narrative form to his supernatural power. A being can wear such splendor like an adornment, and the imagery can describe losing it as well as putting it on [@george-gilgamesh, 1:145–46].

In the [[sennacherib-annals|annals of Sennacherib]], the Assyrian king claims that fear of his lordly brilliance overwhelmed Lulî of Sidon, who fled across the sea. The inscriptions also portray Hezekiah as overwhelmed by Sennacherib's brilliance. The pairing of splendor and fear (*puluḫtu*, “awe” or “dread”) belongs to the language of [[royal-ideology|royal authority]]. The account presents the opponent's response through Assyrian court rhetoric [@rinap3-1, no. 22 ii 37–49].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Hebrew *kābôd* belongs to a word family associated with weight and can denote honor or a perceptible sign of divine presence. In Exod 24:15–18, the glory settles on Sinai, cloud covers the mountain, and the people see what appears like consuming fire. In Exod 33:18–23, Moses asks to see the glory. God proclaims his name and permits Moses only a limited sight as he passes. In Exod 34:29–35 the skin of Moses' face shines, or takes on a hornlike appearance according to another interpretation, and the people are afraid to approach him.

Exod 40:34–35 describes cloud covering the [[tabernacle]] and glory filling it so that Moses cannot enter. In 1 Kgs 8:10–11 the priests are likewise unable to minister when cloud and glory fill the temple. Distinct traditions about God's presence have been described as glory theology, especially in Priestly writing and Ezekiel, and [[name-theology|name theology]], especially in Deuteronomy and Kings. The appearance of glory in Kings illustrates why this distinction is debated. In Ezek 1:26–28 the brilliance around the figure in the [[ezekiel-throne-chariot|throne vision]] is part of the likeness of YHWH's glory. Its later departure and return belong to the theme of [[divine-abandonment|divine abandonment and restoration]].

The combination *hôd wĕhādār* (“splendor and majesty”) gives divine kingship a visible form in Ps 104:1–2, where God wears light like clothing. In Hab 3:3–4, divine brightness covers the sky and rays flash from God's hand. Ps 21:5 (MT 21:6) grants splendor and majesty to the human king, and Dan 4:36 (MT 4:33) links Nebuchadnezzar's recovered splendor to the return of his kingdom. The image of benevolent attention expressed through a shining face has its own associations with [[light-of-the-face|favor]].

## Interpretive Significance

Isa 2:10, 19, 21 pictures people hiding from YHWH's terror and majestic splendor. Shawn Zelig Aster argues that this pairing adapts Assyrian royal language like that in Sennacherib's inscriptions and puts it to work against human pride. The comparison explains the striking conjunction of fear and splendor. A specific polemic against imperial language remains an interpretation of the literary evidence.

Most modern translations render *qāran*, the verb applied to Moses' face, as “shone.” Its relationship to *qeren* (“horn,” with the sense “ray” proposed in some contexts) also underlies the Latin Vulgate's description of a horned face. Some scholars argue for a hornlike transformation or for a play on both images. The scene emphasizes an observable change and the fear it causes.
