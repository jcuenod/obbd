## Overview

The First Temple in Jerusalem is described in Kings as a structure with a porch, a main hall, and an inner sanctuary, built beside the royal complex. The Bible attributes it to Solomon and records later changes before the Babylonians burned it in the sixth century BCE. No excavated remains have been securely identified as part of the building. Iron Age temples elsewhere in the Levant offer useful comparisons for its layout and decoration, while the biblical descriptions remain the main evidence for the Jerusalem structure.

## Archaeological Evidence

The First Temple's site lies on the [[temple-mount|Temple Mount]], where direct archaeological investigation of the temple footprint has not been possible. Material recovered from displaced soil lacks its original building context. The disputed Jehoash inscription and inscribed ivory pomegranate do not provide a secure basis for reconstructing the structure.

In northern Syria, the [[ain-dara-temple|ʿAin Dara temple]] used a sequence of front and interior spaces and included a surrounding gallery. The temple known as Building II at [[tell-tayinat|Tell Tayinat]] likewise had a tripartite plan and stood near royal buildings. The excavated temple at [[tel-motza-temple|Tel Moẓa]] provides a related Judahite comparison. These plans establish regional architectural possibilities. They do not date the construction or biblical description of the Jerusalem temple [@neaehl] [@hurowitz-exalted-house].

## In the Bible

1 Kgs 6:2–3 gives a long building sixty cubits by twenty and a porch ten cubits deep. A cubit was approximately the length of a forearm, with ancient standards varying around 45–52 centimeters. The main hall led to a twenty-cubit inner sanctuary, and chambers rose on the sides of the building (1 Kgs 6:5–10, 16–20). 1 Kgs 6:23–35 describes olivewood cherubim and carvings of flowers and palms. Levantine carved ivories give a comparison for the decorative language [@anep].

1 Kgs 6:36 and 7:12 link temple and palace construction through courses of stone and timber. The description of the palace in 1 Kgs 7:1–12 places the sanctuary near the king's residence. 1 Kgs 5:6–12 (MT 5:20–26) reports cooperation with [[hiram-of-tyre|Hiram]] for timber, and 1 Kgs 8:6–8 places the ark beneath the cherubim. The association with the court helps explain the temple's royal function alongside its role as a sanctuary.

2 Chr 3:1–14 places the building on Mount Moriah, describes a veil, and gives the porch an exceptional height. The height has prompted textual and architectural debate. Ezek 40:48–41:26 describes a temple in a vision using several related features. The plans are literary descriptions with different aims. The precise form of a tenth-century building and the extent to which Kings reflects later rebuilding remain debated.

2 Kgs 16:10–18 describes Ahaz changing the altar and furnishings, and 2 Kgs 22:3–7 reports repairs under Josiah. In 2 Kgs 25:9, 13–17 the Babylonians burn the building and remove its bronze objects. These notices describe a sanctuary that changed during the monarchy and help explain why the form known to a later writer need not represent its first stage in every detail.
