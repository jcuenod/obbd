## Overview

Shalmaneser V succeeded Tiglath-pileser III and ruled Assyria for five years in the late eighth century BCE. Kings names him in the events that led to the end of the northern kingdom of Israel. A Babylonian chronicle reports that he ravaged a place widely identified as Samaria. The few surviving objects bearing his own inscriptions do not narrate a campaign there. These sources support his role in the assault on Samaria, while leaving the division of responsibility between him and his successor Sargon II open.

## Extrabiblical Sources

A Babylonian chronicle records the reign and reports an attack on a place written approximately *Šamaraʾin*. Most scholars identify it with [[samaria|Samaria]], though the spelling has prompted suggestions that another town is meant. The notice is brief. It gives no account of the besieging army, the exact sequence of capture and deportation, or a change of ruler during the campaign.

The surviving royal objects include a brick inscription and lion-shaped weights from Kalhu bearing the royal name. They attest building and administrative activity rather than telling the story of Israel’s defeat. The Tyrian history attributed to Menander and preserved by Josephus (*Ant.* 9.283–287) describes a siege of Tyre under a ruler called Shalmaneser. He is usually identified here as Shalmaneser V, although the passage reaches us through a later quotation and its chronology needs separate assessment.

## Biblical Portrayal

Second Kings 17:3–6 names Shalmaneser as the Assyrian ruler to whom [[hoshea|Hoshea]] was tributary. Hoshea is accused of seeking Egyptian support and withholding payment. The account proceeds from his arrest to a three-year siege and the taking of Samaria. The actor at the end is identified by title rather than personal name. Second Kings 18:9–11 explicitly makes Shalmaneser the initiator of the siege and again describes siege, capture, and deportation.

Hos 10:14 mentions a violent event involving a figure called Shalman. Some readers take the name as an abbreviated form of Shalmaneser. Others compare Salamanu, a Moabite ruler known from Assyrian records. The name of the place in Hosea and the lack of a secure external record of the event leave the identification uncertain.

## Historical Assessment

The biblical notices and the conventional identification in the Babylonian chronicle put Shalmaneser at the center of the attack on Samaria. [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] nevertheless claimed a conquest and deportations there in his own inscriptions. One reconstruction places the fall late in Shalmaneser’s reign and associates Sargon with consolidation or a renewed campaign. Another divides initial siege and final capture between the two kings. Royal credit for a victory does not always match the sequence a later historian would reconstruct. The [[fall-of-samaria|fall of Samaria]] is secure, while the exact responsibility of each ruler remains debated.
