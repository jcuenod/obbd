## Overview

Timna is a valley in the southern Arabah, north of the Gulf of Aqaba, where people mined and smelted copper in several periods. Egyptian inscriptions and a sanctuary of Hathor attest mining activity under the New Kingdom. Later camps and waste from metal production show that work continued and expanded after Egyptian involvement ended. A copper serpent from the shrine has been compared with the serpent in Numbers. Timna provides evidence for metallurgy and worship in the region without identifying the places or people of the biblical episodes.

## Location and Identification

The valley lies on the west side of the Arabah, north of Eilat. Copper deposits were worked in surrounding cliffs and hills, and miners left shafts, galleries, and heaps of waste. Timna also preserves the remains of smelting camps and a shrine. Across the Arabah and farther north, [[faynan|Faynan]] provides comparison for a major copper-producing district east of the valley.

## Archaeological History

Nelson Glueck's early work popularized the association of the mines with Solomon. Beno Rothenberg's excavations identified Egyptian-period mining and an Egyptian shrine dedicated to Hathor. Inscriptions found there name rulers from the time of Seti I to Ramesses V. These finds established Egyptian activity during the Late Bronze Age and early Iron Age. They also showed why the valley's remains could not all belong to a single king or period.

New dating of wood and other organic material at smelting sites has placed major phases of production in the eleventh to ninth centuries BCE. Erez Ben-Yosef and colleagues have argued that a local society could manage a substantial industry while leaving relatively few permanent buildings. Its relation to early [[edomites|Edom]] is a historical proposal. The mines and slag deposits supply firmer evidence for the scale and organization of [[copper-production|copper production]].

At the shrine, material assigned to a phase after Egyptian use includes Qurayyah Painted Ware and a copper snake with traces of gilding. Rothenberg associated the ceramics with groups from northwestern Arabia and proposed that the later shrine included a tent or fabric enclosure. The precise character of the building and the identity of its users remain debated.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Num 21:4–9 describes a bronze serpent made by Moses and set on a pole. The Timna serpent gives evidence for a related kind of metal image in a regional sanctuary. Its presence does not identify the object called [[nehushtan|Nehushtan]] in Kings. The proposed tent-like arrangement at Timna has also invited comparison with the coverings described in Exod 26:7–14. The biblical instructions and the archaeological reconstruction refer to different structures.

The name Timna occurs in accounts of Edomite ancestry (Gen 36:12, 22, 40). The name provides a geographical and literary association with Edom, but does not by itself establish that these persons or clans were connected with this mining district. 1 Kgs 7:45–47 describes bronze furnishings made for Solomon's temple and names where the metal was cast. It gives no source for the copper. Iron Age production makes Timna a relevant comparison for the availability of the material.
