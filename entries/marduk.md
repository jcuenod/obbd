## Overview

Marduk was the patron god of [[babylon|Babylon]], a city on the Euphrates in what is now Iraq. By the late second millennium BCE he had risen to the head of the Babylonian gods. A Babylonian creation epic tells how he defeated Tiamat, the sea goddess, and became king of the gods. In the Bible he appears as Merodach and as Bel, a Babylonian title meaning "lord." His worship helps explain why biblical prophets portray Babylon's fall as a defeat for its god as well as for its rulers.

## Sources and Iconography

The introduction to Hammurabi's law collection already presents the senior gods as granting Marduk authority in the eighteenth century BCE [@roth-law, 76–77]. This predates Marduk's later supremacy. In the Babylonian epic [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]] ("When on high"), the gods give him supreme authority before he goes into battle. He defeats Tiamat, shapes the heavens and earth from her body, and receives fifty names from the assembled gods [@lambert-bcm, 251–255].

Marduk's principal sanctuary in Babylon was Esagil, near [[etemenanki|Etemenanki]], a stepped temple tower. His statue was carried in procession during the Babylonian New Year celebration, the [[akitu-festival|Akitu festival]]. Beyond such [[cult-images|statues used in worship]], artists also represented Marduk through emblems. A pointed spade appears as his symbol on stone monuments, and a creature with a serpent's head and scales appears on the glazed bricks of Babylon's Ishtar Gate [@ddd, 543–549].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 46:1–2 pictures Bel and Nebo, the biblical name of the Babylonian god [[nabu|Nabu]], stooping as their statues weigh down the animals carrying them. The New Year processions give the image its force: statues carried in honor are pictured as burdens hauled away into captivity. The poem does not name the festival or identify a particular procession. Jeremiah likewise treats the city's defeat as Bel's humiliation, using Bel and Merodach side by side (Jer 50:2) and depicting Israel's God making Bel disgorge what he swallowed (Jer 51:44).

The [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]], a Babylonian royal inscription issued after the Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE, credits Marduk with choosing Cyrus [@cos2, 2.124]. Isa 44:24–45:7 instead presents YHWH, Israel's God, as calling Cyrus and empowering him for Israel's sake. Both texts explain Cyrus's success in terms of divine choice, but they name different gods and serve different audiences. The contrast does not establish that Isaiah knew the inscription itself.

Babylonian names also preserve traces of Marduk's prominence. Isa 39:1 names Merodach-baladan, the Babylonian king Marduk-apla-iddina, while 2 Kgs 25:27 names Evil-merodach, the king [[amel-marduk|Amel-Marduk]] who released Jehoiachin. In Dan 4:8 (Aramaic text 4:5), Nebuchadnezzar associates Daniel's court name Belteshazzar with his own god. The narrative association is clearer than the exact Babylonian form behind the name.

In the Greek addition to Daniel known as Bel and the Dragon, Daniel exposes the claim that Bel consumes the food left before his statue (Bel 1–22). Footprints reveal that the priests and their families have entered the sealed temple through a hidden opening and eaten the offerings. The story uses the setting of temple worship to question the statue's ability to eat. It is not a report of how Babylonian priests ordinarily handled offerings.

## Scholarly Issues

Scholars often connect Marduk's rise to supremacy with Nebuchadnezzar I, whose late twelfth-century BCE reign saw the return of Marduk's statue from Elam in what is now southwestern Iran. Lambert associates the epic's composition with this period, but its date is debated. The statue's return provides a plausible setting for either the composition or the promotion of the epic, not a firm date [@lambert-bcm, 271–274; @ddd, 543–549].

Mordecai (Esth 2:5) is generally understood as a name derived from Marduk. A corresponding derivation of Esther (Esth 2:7) from Ishtar, a Babylonian goddess, has been proposed but remains debated. Neither resemblance on its own establishes anything about the worship of the person who bears the name.
