## Overview

Stoicism and Epicureanism were influential Greek schools whose ideas continued to circulate in the Roman period. Their accounts of divine activity, human desires, and a well-ordered life differed sharply. Some concerns, such as self-control and the proper limits of desire, were also widespread beyond formal schools. The schools supply context for Paul's encounter with philosophers in Acts and for comparing New Testament exhortation with ancient moral teaching.

## Greco-Roman Evidence

Stoic writers understood the cosmos as ordered by divine reason. They emphasized virtue and trained judgment as the basis for living well amid changing circumstances. Epictetus makes a potential athlete consider the demands of training, a comparison that turns a familiar practice into a lesson in disciplined choice. The poet Aratus likewise describes the world in terms of its relation to Zeus, language that could circulate outside formal philosophical instruction [@epictetus-handbook, 29] [@aratus-phaenomena, 1–5].

Epicurus speaks of the gods as blessed and undisturbed. His ethical instruction distinguishes necessary desires from others and values freedom from disturbance. Pleasure in this account is compatible with restraint, simplicity, and freedom from fear. The *Letter to Menoeceus* also argues against fearing death, since death ends sensation [@epicurus-menoeceus, 123–131].

## In the Bible

Acts 17:18–34 names Stoics and Epicureans among Paul's Athenian interlocutors. His speech uses a line that also appears in Aratus about human beings being the deity's offspring. It argues against representing the deity in worked materials and calls for repentance in light of coming judgment and resurrection. The appeal to shared language is one part of an argument whose claims about God's activity would invite different responses from each school.

Phil 4:8–13 speaks of conduct worthy of thought and of learning contentment in different circumstances. First Timothy 6:6–8 likewise commends contentment with basic necessities. Such concerns have parallels across schools and popular exhortation. The biblical passages ground them in their own accounts of piety and divine support.

## Interpretive Significance

Vocabulary about self-control or contentment can indicate participation in a common moral conversation. Establishing a writer's affiliation with a school would require more specific evidence. The comparison can instead direct attention to what each text presents as the source or purpose of a good life.
