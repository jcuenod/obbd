## Overview

In the late first century BCE Herod the Great began rebuilding the Jerusalem temple and enlarging the platform on which it stood. The sanctuary and courts became a monumental center for worship and pilgrimage. Work on parts of the complex continued after Herod’s death, and Roman forces destroyed the temple in 70 CE. Ancient descriptions and surviving retaining walls, stairs, and arches give different kinds of evidence for the layout. The distinction between the sanctuary and wider precinct clarifies many of the Gospel and Acts scenes set at the temple.

## Location and Identification

The complex stood on the elevated platform in Jerusalem now known as the Temple Mount or Haram al-Sharif. Sections of the retaining walls preserve Herodian masonry. These supported the expanded precinct and were distinct from the walls of the sanctuary. Ancient literary accounts identify a series of courts on the platform approaching the sanctuary at its center. The exact position of the inner building and the details of its footprint cannot be established from a surviving foundation plan.

## Archaeological History

Josephus describes the ensemble in *Jewish War* 5.184–247 and the rebuilding in *Jewish Antiquities* 15.380–425. The latter places its beginning in Herod’s eighteenth year, often reckoned as 20/19 BCE. *War* 1.401 refers to his fifteenth year. The *Mishnah*, especially *Middot*, preserves a later account of gates, courts, and dimensions. Reconstructions compare these written witnesses with the remains. The accounts differ in purpose and detail.

The southern steps and the gates traditionally called the Huldah Gates belonged to a main route of entry. Robinson’s Arch preserves part of a monumental stairway from the street near the southwest corner. Wilson’s Arch, farther north, belongs to a bridge system joining the platform to the western side of the city. The southern edge of the platform carried the Royal Stoa described by Josephus. Some architectural fragments have been attributed to the building, though the surviving evidence for the stoa is less direct than for the retaining walls. Coins deposited before the completion of a section of the western retaining wall date that phase after 17/18 CE. The find supports an extended building program. It does not date every part of the platform.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

John 2:20 has Jesus’ interlocutors refer to forty-six years of building. A start in 20/19 BCE places that span around 27–28 CE, with variation depending on the starting year and method of counting. Josephus distinguishes the relatively rapid rebuilding of the sanctuary from work on its surroundings. The statement in John is intelligible in terms of the building campaign’s longer history.

Mark 11:15–17 locates Jesus’ action against buying and selling within the temple precinct. Mark 13:1–2 refers to the imposing stones and buildings and foretells their destruction. The monumental walls allow readers to appreciate the scale of the setting. They are surviving retaining structures, while the sanctuary and its buildings were destroyed. John 10:23 places Jesus in Solomon’s Portico, a named colonnade in the precinct. Matt 4:5 evokes an elevated part of the temple complex, but the precise feature called its pinnacle is uncertain.

Luke 1:8–23 depicts Zechariah entering to offer incense while the people wait outside. That scene depends on the division between priestly ritual space and the areas accessible to worshipers. Acts 3:1–11 locates a healing at a gate called Beautiful and a gathering in Solomon’s Portico. The gate’s identification among the names preserved in other sources remains open. Acts 21:26–30 depicts a disturbance over supposed admission of an outsider to an area from which gentiles were excluded. Surviving Greek warning inscriptions give independent evidence of a boundary around the inner precinct. Mark 15:38 describes the tearing of the temple curtain, an image rooted in the sacred divisions of the sanctuary. The wording leaves open which curtain the narrative envisages.
