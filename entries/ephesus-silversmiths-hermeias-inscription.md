## Overview

A Greek funerary inscription from Ephesus identifies Antonius Hermeias as a silversmith and temple official and names his wife, Claudia Erotis. It gives the city's silversmiths' association responsibility for a grave and assigns it fines for violations. The inscription confirms that Ephesian silversmiths acted as an organized body. Its evidence complements Acts 19, which portrays metalworkers assembling to defend the trade associated with the sanctuary of Artemis.

## Description and Provenance

The inscription is published as IEph 2212. The modern edition relies on a copy made by J. T. Wood. The precise findspot is unknown, and the proposed date is the reign of Claudius (41–54 CE) or later [@agrw-161].

## Contents

The text restricts the use of the grave to the people named in it. Its provisions direct a fine of one thousand denarii to the silversmiths if anyone deposits an unauthorized body or erases the inscription. The Greek *synedrion* identifies the organized body assigned to care for the grave. The text also records a sum given by Erotis and a distribution. The arrangements show an association exercising continuing responsibilities outside the immediate production and sale of goods.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Acts 19:24–27 Demetrius calls together silversmiths and others in related trades and argues that Paul's teaching threatens their livelihood and the honor of Artemis. Hermeias's designation as both silversmith and temple official shows that work and sanctuary connections could overlap. The association's role in burial gives an independent example of corporate organization in the same city. Its members and duties need not coincide with those of the assembly in Acts. The inscription names neither Demetrius nor the disturbance. For the sanctuary setting see [[temple-of-artemis-ephesus]].
