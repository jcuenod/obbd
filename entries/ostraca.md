## Overview

Ostraca are fragments of pottery used as surfaces for writing. In the Iron Age and Persian-period Levant, people wrote letters, accounts, lists, and exercises on sherds, often in ink. Broken pottery was readily available and survives after most papyrus and wood have decayed. Groups from Samaria, Arad, Lachish, and other sites preserve records of administration and ordinary correspondence. They give material context to biblical descriptions of sherds and show how much writing could circulate below the level of the royal archives.

## Archaeological Evidence

An ostracon is a single inscribed sherd. Scribes used a writing tool and ink on the surface or scratched characters into the pottery. The object may therefore be older than its inscription. Its date is assessed from the deposit, the form of writing, and any internal information such as a regnal year. The size and curved surface made sherds convenient for short texts. Many other documents would have been written on materials that rarely survive. The recovered mix of genres reflects ancient choices and modern conditions of preservation.

The eighth-century BCE [[samaria-ostraca|Samaria ostraca]] list deliveries of oil and wine. At [[arad-ostraca|Arad]], letters to an official named Eliashib include instructions about provisions, giving evidence for the routine supply of a Judahite fortress. The [[lachish-letters|Lachish letters]] come from the closing years of the kingdom of Judah and preserve communication among military personnel. Other Hebrew groups include sherds from Horvat ʿUza and the late seventh-century petition from [[mesad-hashavyahu-ostracon|Meṣad Ḥashavyahu]]. Aramaic ostraca from Elephantine and Idumea illustrate the medium’s continued use in different Persian-period settings.

Ink that is faint or invisible under normal light can sometimes be distinguished using images made at several wavelengths. Multispectral work on Arad 16 recovered writing on the reverse and improved the reading of its front. The case shows why earlier editions can be revised even when the artifact itself has been known for decades. Damage and variations in handwriting still leave some readings uncertain.

## In the Bible

Job 2:8 uses a sherd as a tool for scraping the skin, while Isa 30:14 refers to fragments large enough to carry a coal or scoop water. Isa 45:9 evokes broken earthenware in an argument about the relation of maker and made. In Ps 22:15 (MT 22:16) the dryness of a sherd is part of an image of exhaustion. These passages take the commonplace presence of pottery fragments for granted. They describe uses or qualities of sherds other than writing.

The contents of some ostraca offer another kind of comparison. Jer 34:7 names Lachish and Azekah as remaining fortified towns during the Babylonian assault. The correspondence from Lachish transmits information about signals and places in the same region. It is commonly read against the same crisis, although the exact sequence and meaning of individual reports are debated. Jer 38:4 shows officials accusing Jeremiah of undermining morale, a point of comparison with complaints about discouraging words in the letters. The Meṣad Ḥashavyahu petitioner seeks the return of a garment. Exod 22:26–27 (MT 22:25–26) regulates the taking of a garment as a pledge. The sherd does not specify the legal rule or establish that the dispute concerned a pledge, but it illustrates how a confiscated garment could lead to an appeal.
