## Overview

The Verse Account of Nabonidus is a Babylonian poem that interprets the fall of Babylon as the removal of a ruler who had offended the gods. It criticizes Nabonidus's devotion to the moon god, his departure for Tayma, and the disruption of public worship. Cyrus appears as the ruler who restores order. The poem also describes Nabonidus handing authority to his eldest son before leaving. It gives valuable evidence for the politics of religious memory after the Persian conquest and for traditions comparable to those in Daniel.

## Manuscripts and Date

The composition survives on a damaged cuneiform tablet in the British Museum (BM 38299). It was written after Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BCE, probably in the early Persian period. The surviving tablet preserves only parts of the poem. Its interest in Babylonian sanctuaries and in the god Marduk has led scholars to associate its authorship with Babylonian priestly or scribal circles. The individual author is unknown.

## Contents

The poem accuses [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] of giving unusual honors to Sîn, of misunderstanding sacred traditions, and of allowing proper worship to lapse. It says that, before departing on a long journey to Tayma, he entrusted kingship to his eldest son. Other contemporary texts identify the son as [[belshazzar|Belshazzar]]. The wording expresses the extent of the authority delegated to him, while surviving Babylonian documents continue to recognize Nabonidus as king.

The poem contrasts the preceding reign with the rule of Cyrus. It describes the removal or reversal of works associated with Nabonidus and praises Cyrus as a restorer. The [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]] shares the favorable presentation of the conqueror. The [[nabonidus-chronicle|Nabonidus Chronicle]] independently attests the king's residence at Tayma and the interruption of particular festival observances.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Dan 5:1–2 gives Belshazzar the title king and connects him with Nebuchadnezzar. The delegation described in the poem helps account for the substantial authority associated with Belshazzar in later traditions. Dan 5:7, 16, 29 also offers a rank often translated “third ruler.” It has been compared with the historical positions of Nabonidus and Belshazzar, although the phrase and story do not establish a particular regency arrangement.

The king who loses his place in Dan 4:28–33 (MT 4:25–30) is Nebuchadnezzar. The [[prayer-of-nabonidus|Prayer of Nabonidus]] from Qumran associates a prolonged affliction with Nabonidus at Tayma. That memory provides a closer link between the kinds of tradition about Nabonidus and the literary account in Daniel. Isa 45:1–7 also honors Cyrus as an agent of divine purpose, there in the service of Israel's God.

## Scholarly Issues

The Verse Account gives the viewpoint of writers hostile to Nabonidus and favorable to Cyrus. Where it ascribes motives or depicts the former king as incompetent, comparison with his building inscriptions and other records is especially important. Distinguishing those judgments from shared events allows the poem to illuminate both the reign and the political uses of its memory.
