## Overview

Sîn was the Akkadian name of the Mesopotamian moon god known in Sumerian as Nanna. He had major sanctuaries at Ur and Harran, and the Babylonian king Nabonidus promoted his worship in the sixth century BCE. The Levant had its own lunar traditions, including the god Yarikh at Ugarit. Lunar emblems on Iron Age objects and biblical criticism of worship directed to the moon give different kinds of evidence for these traditions. They help place biblical references to crescents, astral worship, and the ancestral connections with Ur and Harran.

## Sources and Iconography

The cults of Nanna at [[ur|Ur]] and Sîn at [[harran|Harran]] are attested in temple records, hymns, and royal inscriptions. At Harran his sanctuary was called Eḫulḫul. [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] restored the temple and also supported the cult at Ur. His inscriptions give unusual prominence to the moon god. The [[verse-account-of-nabonidus|Verse Account]] presents a hostile view of the king's religious policies, which needs to be read alongside his own claims [@ddd] [@cos2].

The crescent is a common lunar emblem, and Mesopotamian imagery connects the moon with the horns of a bull. A carved stone from et-Tell, often identified with Bethsaida, shows a bull's head on a standard. The horns and comparisons with Harran have led scholars to propose a lunar interpretation. Some think the imagery may also evoke a storm god. In Late Bronze Age Ugaritic literature, Yarikh is a moon god with a distinct local identity [@keel-uehlinger-ggig] [@parker-unp].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 11:28, 31 places Terah's family in relation to Ur and Harran, the two major cult centers. The geographical overlap is of interest, while the text does not describe the family's lunar worship. Deut 4:19 includes the moon among created heavenly bodies to which Israel must not bow. 2 Kgs 23:5 describes Josiah removing personnel associated with worship of the [[host-of-heaven|host of heaven]], including the moon. Jer 8:2 likewise names the moon among the heavenly bodies that some had served.

Job 31:26–28 describes the temptation to salute the sun or moon and includes it among acts treated as infidelity to God. Judg 8:21, 26 associates crescent-shaped ornaments with Midianite rulers and their camels. Isa 3:18 includes comparable ornaments among the signs of Jerusalem's wealth. Crescents can be used as jewelry or symbols. Their presence in an inventory cannot by itself establish the worship of a named god.

## Scholarly Issues

Attempts to derive Sinai directly from Sîn or use the name Laban as evidence for a family moon cult remain speculative. Jericho's name has also been compared with West Semitic lunar vocabulary, but an etymology alone would not establish the cult or beliefs of the city's inhabitants. The evidence is strongest where a named god, a monument, and a ritual setting can be connected.
