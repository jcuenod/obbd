## Overview

Several biblical prayers ask for shelter under God's wings. Ancient Near Eastern art offers ways to picture this form of protection. In Egypt, goddesses extend their wings over kings and the dead, and winged emblems appear above temple doorways. The Hebrew Bible also describes winged figures overshadowing the ark and compares God's care with that of a bird tending its young. These different settings explain why the same image can express safety at a sanctuary, rescue from danger, or an intimate relationship with God.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The innermost coffin of Tutankhamun depicts Isis and Nephthys crossing their wings over the body of the king. The same coffin includes Nekhbet, the vulture goddess associated with Upper Egypt, in winged form. Such images give protection a bodily shape by bringing the receiver within the span of a divine figure's feathers. They draw on the language of [[egyptian-kingship|Egyptian kingship]], and related images also occur on nonroyal coffins [@anep] [@keel-symbolism].

A [[winged-sun-disk|winged sun disk]] above an Egyptian temple doorway embodies divine presence and protection in a different form. Winged disks and winged protective figures also appear in Iron Age Levantine art, including carved ivories and seals. Local uses of the motifs vary. The winged emblems on some Judahite [[lmlk-seal-impressions|royal jar stamps]], for instance, appear in an administrative setting, and their precise symbolism is debated [@anep] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Exod 19:4 YHWH describes carrying Israel from Egypt to himself on eagles' wings. Deut 32:11 gives the image more detail by describing a large bird hovering over its offspring, spreading its wings, and carrying them. In Matt 23:37 Jesus expresses a desire to gather Jerusalem as a hen gathers her brood. The choice of bird and situation can change while the sheltering action remains recognizable.

Ruth 2:12 applies the image to an individual. Boaz blesses Ruth for seeking refuge under YHWH's wings, linking divine shelter with the security she seeks as a foreigner. Ps 17:8, 57:1 (MT 57:2), and 91:4 likewise present the wings as a place of refuge from danger. The language of shade and covering makes the metaphor effective even in settings where the text names no sanctuary.

The sanctuary does matter in some passages. Ps 36:7 (MT 36:8) sets the shadow of the wings alongside abundance from God's house in the next verse. Ps 61:4 (MT 61:5) pairs refuge under the wings with residence in God's tent. In Exod 25:20 the [[cherubim]] spread their wings over the cover of the [[ark-of-the-covenant|ark]]. In the imagery of the [[cherub-throne|cherub throne]], wings surround the earthly site of YHWH's enthronement.

## Interpretive Significance

Some scholars understand the psalms chiefly in light of animal behavior. Others emphasize sanctuary imagery, and Othmar Keel compared biblical expressions of protection with Egyptian winged goddesses. Temple language gives the sanctuary reading particular force in Psalms 36 and 61. The image can evoke both a bird's actions and the protective figures familiar from worship. Mal 4:2 (MT 3:20) associates healing with the wings of the sun of righteousness. The winged disk offers a visual parallel for this combination of light and wings, although the verse leaves its visual associations open.
