## Overview

Biblical expressions translated “sons of God” or “sons of the gods” often designate heavenly beings. Comparable language appears at Late Bronze Age Ugarit for members of the divine family. In the Bible such beings praise God, attend his court, and in one brief account take human wives. Second Temple Jewish texts developed that story into an account of fallen angels called Watchers. The ancient comparison and later readings help distinguish what individual biblical passages say from the more detailed stories that grew from them.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Texts from Ugarit refer to sons of [[el|El]] or to divine sons. The Baal Cycle mentions the seventy sons of Athirat, a divine figure associated with El. Such language places the gods in a household or assembly, with roles and loyalties expressed through kinship. The figure seventy can convey the extent of the group. It provides a comparison for biblical divine assemblies without fixing the number or relationships in every biblical scene [@ktu3] [@parker-unp].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Job 1:6 and 2:1 show the sons of God presenting themselves before YHWH in a [[divine-council|divine court]]. Job 38:7 pairs them with the morning stars in a scene of joy at creation. Ps 29:1 summons divine beings to ascribe glory to YHWH, and Ps 89:6 (MT 89:7) declares that YHWH has no equal among them. In Ps 82:6 those judged are called sons of [[elyon|the Most High]], and the psalm pronounces their mortality. Dan 3:25 uses similar language when the king describes the appearance of a fourth figure in the furnace.

Deut 32:8 assigns peoples to their territories. The Masoretic Text relates the allocation to the children of Israel. A [[4qdeut-j|Qumran copy]] supports the reading sons of God and the ancient Greek tradition also points to a reading about heavenly beings. Many scholars regard this as earlier than the Masoretic wording. It offers a context for comparing divine beings with the nations of the world.

Gen 6:1–4 briefly describes sons of God taking wives from among human women and mentions the [[nephilim|Nephilim]]. The prevailing historical reading understands the sons as supernatural beings, in light of their designation elsewhere and the contrast with the daughters of humankind. Later readings take them as human rulers or nobles, or identify them with the line of Seth. The short account leaves many details about their actions and the Nephilim unresolved.

## Interpretive Significance

In [[first-enoch|1 Enoch]] 6:1–7:6 the Watchers descend, bind themselves by an oath, take wives, and father giants. Jubilees also develops the story. Jude 6 and 2 Pet 2:4 refer to angels who sinned and were confined, language often read in light of the Watcher tradition. These sources establish how some ancient readers interpreted Genesis. Their elaborations should be distinguished from the few details Genesis itself supplies. The language of a king as God's son belongs to another setting, with its own traditions of [[king-as-son-of-god|royal sonship]].
