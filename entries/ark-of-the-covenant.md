## Overview

The ark of the covenant is a portable wooden chest described in several biblical traditions about Israel's worship. It appears in narratives set from the wilderness journey through the early monarchy in the southern Levant. Deuteronomy presents it as a repository for the covenant tablets. Exodus describes an elaborate chest with a gold cover above which Israel's God meets Moses, and Samuel presents it as a sign of divine presence in war. Egyptian portable shrines and ancient Near Eastern treaty deposits provide comparisons for different features of the biblical accounts.

## Archaeological Evidence

Portable Egyptian shrines could be moved on poles. The [[tutankhamun-anubis-shrine|Anubis shrine from Tutankhamun's tomb]] consists of a gilded wooden chest topped by the figure of a jackal and mounted on a platform with carrying poles. Egyptian barque shrines used a similar method to transport a god's ceremonial boat. These objects show how sacred equipment could be carried and displayed, while the Egyptian examples had their own uses and meanings.

Some [[covenant-and-treaty-form|ancient treaties]] specified that copies be placed before the gods. A Hittite treaty with the king of Mittani directs that one copy be deposited before the Sun Goddess of Arinna and another before the Storm God in Mittani. In correspondence with the Hittite court, the Egyptian king Ramesses II also speaks of placing a written oath before the gods. This practice is a useful comparison for the tablets placed in the ark in Deuteronomy [@beckman-hdt].

## In the Bible

In Deut 10:1–5 Moses makes a wooden chest for the replacement tablets. In the account of the [[tabernacle|portable sanctuary]] in Exod 25:10–22, a gold-covered acacia chest has poles, a cover, and two cherubim, or winged guardian figures. The cover, called *kappōret* and often translated “mercy seat,” is the place above which God addresses Moses. In 1 Chr 28:2 David associates the ark with God's [[footstool|footstool]], an image of royal authority. The chest and its surrounding furniture have often been interpreted in light of the [[cherub-throne|thrones flanked by winged creatures]] known from the Levant [@keel-symbolism].

Num 10:35–36 links the ark's movement with God's advance against enemies. In 1 Sam 4–6, the Israelites carry it into battle expecting deliverance, but the Philistines capture it. The image of Dagon falls before it in Ashdod, and after a series of afflictions the Philistines send it back. Mesopotamian accounts of the seizure and return of [[cult-images|statues representing gods]] provide a comparative setting for the capture of sacred objects in war. The narrative in Samuel represents the god's power as active even after the object has fallen into enemy hands.

The ark passes to Kiriath-jearim in 1 Sam 7:1. In 2 Sam 6 David brings it to Jerusalem, and in 1 Kgs 8:1–9 Solomon installs it in the inner sanctuary under cherubim. Leonhard Rost proposed that an account of the ark's movements once circulated independently. Scholars still debate its boundaries, date, and purpose. Excavations at Kiriath-jearim have prompted proposals tying the story to a sanctuary there under northern Israelite influence, but the archaeological remains cannot identify the ark itself. Its fate is unknown, and Jer 3:16 imagines a future in which it will no longer be remembered or remade.
