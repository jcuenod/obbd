## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern descriptions of conquest use recurring phrases and images to portray a victory as complete. Royal inscriptions from Egypt, Assyria, and neighboring kingdoms claim that enemies vanished, divine powers fought for the king, and resistance ended across whole regions. Biblical narratives in Joshua use some of the same conventions. The comparison helps readers see how ancient authors selected and shaped events to tell a story about victory and divine support. Questions about the historical events behind the accounts and the violence depicted in them require attention to the sources and aims of each text.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[merneptah-stele|Merneptah Stele]] of the late thirteenth century BCE says that Israel was laid waste and its seed was gone. The ninth-century BCE [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]] presents Israel as having perished permanently after Moab threw off Israelite control. The continued existence of Israel shows what such language could do in a victory text. It expressed the gravity and completeness of the loss from the winner's perspective [@cos2, 2.6, 2.23].

Assyrian annals repeat formulas about taking cities, destroying defenses, deporting inhabitants, and collecting tribute. Egypt's [[kadesh-battle-reliefs|accounts of the battle of Kadesh]] present the god Amun as answering Ramesses II in his hour of danger. Hittite campaigns likewise portray gods assisting their favored ruler. K. Lawson Younger compared such accounts with Joshua and identified repeated patterns of conquest claims, descriptions of divine aid, and summaries of captured territory.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Josh 10:28–43 a sequence of cities is taken in turn. The narration repeats expressions for taking each city, striking its inhabitants, and leaving no survivor. Josh 11:16–23 and 21:43–45 summarize Israel's possession of the land and victory over its enemies in broad terms. Yet Josh 13:1–7 names territory still to be possessed, and Judg 1:1–36 reports a series of places where local populations remained. These passages present the extent of conquest in different ways.

Divine support also shapes the story. Josh 10:11–14 describes YHWH delivering Israel's enemies and sending hail, then depicts the sun and moon stopping. Some scholars have proposed that the sun and moon language reflects ancient readings of celestial signs. That interpretation remains disputed, and the narrative presents the episode as exceptional divine intervention. Exod 14:13–14 similarly presents YHWH as the decisive combatant.

## Interpretive Significance

Formulaic language makes it possible for a text to present a victory as total while using expressions familiar to ancient readers of military reports. Individual claims still need to be considered in their local setting. The conventions give no independent count of people killed or places captured. The ways different biblical passages prescribe and narrate violence, including the rules in Deut 20:1–20 and the practice of [[herem|devotion to destruction]], remain important in their own right.

Archaeology gives a separate body of evidence. The occupation and destruction histories of sites such as Jericho and Ai have led most archaeologists to reject a single large-scale conquest of Canaan in the thirteenth century BCE as an account of the area's settlement. The archaeological case and the range of historical models are discussed under [[emergence-of-israel|the emergence of Israel]]. Comparison of the rhetoric helps explain how the conquest is told.
