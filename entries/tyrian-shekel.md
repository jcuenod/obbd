## Overview

Tyrian shekels and half-shekels were silver coins used in the eastern Mediterranean in the late Second Temple period. They show the head of the god Melqart in the guise of Herakles and an eagle. Their relatively consistent weight and silver content made them suitable for payments at the Jerusalem temple. The denominations help explain the tax collected in Matt 17 and the roles of money changers in the Gospel accounts. The precise mint of some late issues and the identity of particular biblical coins remain debated.

## Archaeological Evidence

The coins bear a laureate head of [[melqart|Melqart-Herakles]] on one side and an eagle with a Greek legend describing [[tyre|Tyre]] as holy and inviolable on the other. Dates are reckoned from the Tyrian era that began in 126/125 BCE. The full shekel was a tetradrachm, a coin of four drachmas. Its half was equivalent to two. Issues continued into the 60s CE, and hoards show that the coins circulated in Judaea.

Some later examples have a different style and bear a monogram often read as KP. Yaʿakov Meshorer proposed that such coins from around 19/18 BCE onward were produced in Jerusalem. Other numismatists have challenged the mint attribution. The design and weight standard still identify the securely attested series, while the location of later production remains a separate question.

## In the Bible

Exod 30:11–16 prescribes a half-shekel according to the sanctuary weight as ransom in connection with a census. This passage concerns a weight of silver and belongs to the background of the later tax. 2 Chr 24:6–9 recalls a levy associated with Moses, and Neh 10:32 (MT 10:33) records agreement to contribute one third of a shekel annually. The Qumran ordinance 4Q159 is often understood as limiting a half-shekel obligation to one payment in a person's lifetime. These references show that practice and interpretation varied.

The Mishnah prescribes Tyrian currency for sanctuary payments and describes money changers setting up tables in the provinces and later at the temple (m. Bekhorot 8:7 and m. Sheqalim 1:3). It also discusses an exchange surcharge. A widely accepted explanation for the preference is the coins' weight and purity, which made them reliable even though their imagery depicted a foreign god.

In Matt 17:24–27 the tax collectors ask about the didrachma, a coin worth half a shekel on this reckoning. The stater in the narrative pays for two people. The passage fits the value of a full shekel without specifying a mint. Matt 21:12, Mark 11:15–17, and John 2:13–16 depict money changers in the temple precinct. The Romans diverted a Jewish tax to Jupiter Capitolinus after the temple's destruction, a later context in which readers could hear Matthew's story.

Tyrian shekels are sometimes proposed as the thirty silver pieces paid to Judas (Matt 26:15 and 27:3–10). Matthew identifies the amount and links the money with the temple treasury. The denomination remains uncertain.
