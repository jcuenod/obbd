## Overview

The exodus narrative recounts Israel's forced labor in Egypt, departure under Moses and passage through the wilderness. Egyptian records and archaeology establish that people from the Levant lived and worked in Egypt and that a people called Israel was present in Canaan by about 1208 BCE. Connecting these facts to a specific departure remains difficult. Scholars have proposed a remembered experience of Egyptian domination, the journey of a smaller group, and dates in either the fifteenth or thirteenth century BCE. The available evidence bears differently on each proposal.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian texts and excavations document people of West Semitic origin in the Nile Delta in different periods. Individuals and groups entered as migrants, laborers, captives and traders. The [[hyksos|Hyksos]], who ruled from Avaris in the eastern Delta, were expelled by the early New Kingdom pharaohs. Later Egyptian rulers exercised influence and control in Canaan. The [[papyrus-anastasi|Anastasi papyri]] include reports about escaped servants and the admission of pastoralists into the eastern Delta, illustrating how officials monitored movement across the frontier. None of these sources identifies Israelites as slaves in Egypt or reports their departure. [@anet] [@mazar-archaeology]

The closing lines of the [[merneptah-stele|Merneptah Stele]] place a people called Israel among the Egyptian king's opponents in Canaan around 1208 BCE. The designation establishes a latest date by which such a people was known in the region. Surveys also show a growth in highland settlements near the transition to the Iron Age. Material continuity at many sites suggests a substantial local component in the [[emergence-of-israel|emergence of Israel]]. Settlement patterns alone cannot determine the histories of all groups that joined those communities. [@ael] [@anet] [@neaehl]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 1:8–11 links forced labor to Pharaoh's fear of a growing population and names [[pithom|Pithom]] and [[pi-ramesses|Raamses]] as store cities. Raamses is usually associated with Pi-Ramesses, the Delta residence developed under the Ramesside kings. Competing identifications of Pithom and the possibility that place names were updated in transmission complicate dating. Egyptian personal names and plausible details of frontier administration also come from or fit more than one period. Exod 12:37–41 gives about six hundred thousand men on foot, besides children, and Num 33:1–49 lists encampments between Egypt and the plains of Moab. Late Bronze Age evidence in Sinai and around Kadesh-barnea gives no support for a population on this scale. Small mobile groups could leave limited traces. The size specified by a literal reading would present severe demographic and logistical difficulties. [@abd] [@neaehl]

Exod 15:1–18 celebrates divine victory at the sea and the people's passage toward their inheritance. Hos 11:1 recalls the departure in the language of God's love for a child. Amos 9:7 compares it with the migrations of Philistines and Arameans. These passages show that deliverance from Egypt could be remembered and put to different uses apart from a continuous account of the journey. The age of the Song of the Sea in its present form remains disputed. [@abd]

## Interpretive Significance

Donald Redford associated the tradition with memories surrounding the Hyksos. Other accounts of cultural memory, including those associated with Jan Assmann and Ronald Hendel, have examined how experiences of Egyptian power and the formation of Israel could have shaped the story. Richard Elliott Friedman proposes a smaller departure involving ancestors of the Levites. The Levite identification remains inferential. Kenneth Kitchen and James Hoffmeier favor a Ramesside setting, drawing especially on Delta geography and Egyptian details. An earlier date in the fifteenth century BCE follows a literal calculation from the 480 years in 1 Kgs 6:1. Judg 11:26 also assigns three hundred years to Israelite occupancy of Transjordan. Those figures may serve literary or schematic purposes, and the early date faces difficulties in the archaeological sequence of sites associated with the conquest. [@abd] [@mazar-archaeology]

The [[admonitions-of-ipuwer|Admonitions of Ipuwer]] are an Egyptian composition about calamity and social disorder. The poem supplies no dated record of the plagues. Identifications with the eruption of Thera or with alleged chariot wheels beneath the sea have no secure connection to the narrative. The mummy of [[ramesses-ii|Ramesses II]] supplies no evidence that he drowned at the crossing. The ancient evidence leaves the scale, date and route of any remembered departure open. [@ael] [@abd]
