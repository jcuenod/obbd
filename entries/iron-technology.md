## Overview

Iron use spread gradually across the Levant near the end of the Late Bronze Age and in the first centuries of the Iron Age. Early iron objects were uncommon, and bronze stayed in use for tools and weapons as ironworking expanded. Excavations show that communities in several parts of the region worked iron. In the Bible, iron appears in accounts of farmers’ tools, chariots and royal power, as well as in images of hardship and strength. These passages reflect different uses of the metal and different interests in telling its history.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Iron objects existed long before iron became common. A small number used naturally occurring meteoritic iron. The increasingly common alternative was smelting, extracting metallic iron from ore with heat and fuel. Early furnaces yielded an irregular mass of metal that smiths hammered to remove impurities and shape it. Contact with carbon could harden the metal, although the adoption and control of this process varied over time. Bronze manufacture remained important throughout the transition. [@mazar-archaeology] [@neaehl]

The expansion of ironworking in the southern Levant is particularly visible in Iron IIA, broadly the tenth and ninth centuries BCE. Tell Hammeh in the Jordan Valley preserves evidence for early smelting, and Tel Beth-Shemesh preserves remains of smithing. Finds from Tell es-Safi, generally identified with Philistine Gath, document iron and bronze production there. The distributions provide no archaeological basis for a general [[philistines|Philistine]] monopoly. Earlier claims of a Hittite monopoly likewise have little support in the evidence for production and circulation across the region. [@mazar-archaeology, 264–266, 509–510] [@neaehl, 5:2080–2083]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 1 Sam 13:19–22 the Philistines control access to smiths, so Israelites take their implements to them for sharpening. The account makes weapons scarce in Israel before Saul’s battle. Its claim about specific political and military circumstances has often been expanded into a broader claim about the early history of metallurgy. Weights inscribed with the word [[pim-weights|*pim*]] clarify the price for smithing in 1 Sam 13:21. The weights correspond to about two-thirds of a shekel, a unit of mass. The unusually phrased list of implements and services poses additional questions for translators. [@ahituv-echoes] [@abd, 6:905–907]

The [[chariots-and-horses|chariots]] in Josh 17:16–18 and Judg 1:19 are described as iron and are associated with the advantage of forces on lowland terrain. The description need not imply that a whole vehicle was made from metal. Deut 8:9 describes a land rich in resources, including iron-bearing stone. Deut 4:20 uses the image of a furnace to describe Israel’s experience in Egypt. Gen 4:22 assigns the working of both bronze and iron to Tubal-cain among the ancestors of early crafts. [@king-stager-life, 164–175] [@abd]

## Interpretive Significance

The hard surface and durability of iron account for some of its figurative uses. Deut 3:11 gives Og an iron bed or couch, whose size and associations signal exceptional status. The suggestion that this was a basalt sarcophagus remains debated. Translations also differ over whether the iron implements in 2 Sam 12:31 represent forced labor or punishment of the conquered Ammonites. Archaeological knowledge can clarify the objects and the range of possible uses, while the wording and context remain important to the interpretation of each passage. [@abd] [@king-stager-life, 164–175]
