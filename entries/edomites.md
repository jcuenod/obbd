## Overview

Edom was an Iron Age kingdom in southern Transjordan, where its highland settlements overlooked the Arabah and the copper-producing region of Faynan. Egyptian texts record earlier groups associated with Edom, and Assyrian inscriptions name its kings. In later centuries the name Idumea came to designate a region west of the Arabah. Biblical texts portray Edom as both Israel's relative and Judah's rival. Archaeology and inscriptions help distinguish the growth of Edomite political and economic power from the biblical authors' judgments about the relationship.

## Sources

Papyrus Anastasi VI reports that [[shasu|Shasu]] groups from Edom were allowed into the eastern Nile Delta to reach water for themselves and their livestock in the time of Merneptah. Other Egyptian texts associate Shasu groups with Seir. These references name mobile groups and landscapes long before the Neo-Assyrian records identify kings of Edom. Tiglath-pileser III names Qaus-malaka among rulers who paid tribute, and Esarhaddon names Qaus-gabri. An impression from Umm el-Biyara gives the latter's name and royal title in the local script. [[edomite-inscriptions|Local inscriptions]] also attest [[qos|Qos]], the principal deity known from Edomite sources [@anet, 259] [@rinap1, 122] [@rinap4, no. 1] [@ahituv-echoes, 351–356].

## History

The date at which an Edomite state formed is debated. Excavations at [[faynan|Faynan]] document substantial copper production and organization from the eleventh to ninth centuries BCE. Thomas Levy and Erez Ben-Yosef have argued that this industry reflects early political complexity connected with Edom. Piotr Bienkowski distinguishes the lowland producers from the later kingdom centered in the highlands around [[bozrah|Bozrah]], pointing to gaps and differences in settlement and production. The Assyrian records establish a named monarchy by the late eighth century BCE. How far the earlier economy belongs to the same political development remains open.

Evidence associated with Edom appears west of the Arabah, including at Ḥorvat Qitmit and ʿEn Ḥaṣeva. An Arad letter warns of a military threat from Edom, while some archaeological contexts indicate religious activity and exchange across the frontier. The territory later called Idumea developed in the southern hills west of the Arabah. It was incorporated into the Hasmonean realm under John Hyrcanus, and Herod the Great came from a family of Idumean background [@neaehl, 4:1230–1233] [@abd, 2:287–301].

## In the Bible

Gen 25:30 connects Edom to Esau through a play on the Hebrew word for red. Gen 36:31–39 places Edomite kings before kingship in Israel and lists rulers associated with several towns. The list's relationship to the independently attested chronology of Edom remains debated. Num 20:14–21 presents Edom refusing passage even though Israel appeals to kinship. Deut 23:7–8 (MT 23:8–9) applies that kinship to the terms of admission to the assembly.

In 2 Sam 8:13–14 David puts garrisons in Edom. In 2 Kgs 8:20–22, Edom breaks with Judah under Joram, and in 2 Kgs 14:7 Amaziah defeats Edom and takes [[petra-and-sela|Sela]], whose precise identification is debated. These accounts trace changing relations between the kingdoms. Assyrian recognition of Edomite monarchs anchors part of their historical setting, while the earlier relations are known primarily from biblical tradition.

Ps 137:7 and Obad 10–14 blame Edom for its response to Jerusalem's fall. Lam 4:21–22 anticipates judgment on Edom, and Mal 1:2–5 interprets Edom's devastation in light of the contrast between Jacob and Esau. These texts preserve Judahite accusations and theological interpretations. The archaeological evidence for an Edomite presence to the west helps explain their setting, while Edom's precise role in the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem remains debated.
