## Overview

Papyrus Amherst 63 is a religious anthology in Aramaic written in Egyptian Demotic script. The surviving copy comes from Egypt, and the writings collected in it reflect traditions that may be older than the papyrus itself. One prayer shares a distinctive sequence of petitions and images with Psalm 20. The resemblance sheds light on the circulation and adaptation of prayers in the ancient Levant and Egypt, even though their exact relationship cannot be recovered.

## Description and Provenance

The papyrus is preserved in the Morgan Library & Museum in New York. Its use of Demotic, a cursive Egyptian script, to write [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]] complicates the reading because the script and language have different conventions. The manuscript is generally placed in the later first millennium BCE. The date of this copy does not date the individual prayers or the traditions they preserve [@van-der-toorn-amherst].

## Contents

The papyrus brings together prayers, hymns, and other religious compositions. The prayer usually compared with Psalm 20 is in column XII, lines 11–19, in van der Toorn’s edition. It asks for help, invokes a divine name, contrasts human military strength with divine support, and petitions for an answer. The anthology preserves traditions of more than one community or cultic setting. The [[elephantine-papyri]] supply distinct evidence for Aramaic-speaking communities in Egypt.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ps 20:1–9 (MT 20:2–10) asks that the king receive help in distress. It contrasts reliance on chariots and horses with reliance on the name of Israel's God. The Aramaic prayer shares several elements in a comparable order, while the settings and religious language differ. Differences in the divine names and places invoked are part of the evidence for adaptation. The relationship is closer than a single common image and still allows several histories of transmission [@kister-amherst-ps20].

## Scholarly Issues

Suggestions include descent from a shared prayer tradition and adaptation of one form of the prayer within another community. The two surviving versions are separated by language, transmission, and setting. Neither preserves a securely identifiable original. Their parallel is strongest as evidence for the mobility of ancient poetic traditions.
