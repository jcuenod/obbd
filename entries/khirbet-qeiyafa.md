## Overview

Khirbet Qeiyafa is a fortified site overlooking the Elah Valley in the southern Levant. Its principal Iron Age occupation is usually dated near 1000 BCE. Excavations revealed a wall with chambers, two gates, buildings with objects interpreted as ritual equipment, and an early alphabetic inscription on pottery. The site has become important in debates about when fortified towns appeared in Judah and who built them. Proposed identifications with a place named in Joshua and Samuel remain open.

## Location and Identification

The hilltop stands above the Elah Valley near the border between the uplands of Judah and the coastal lowlands. Its position would have given inhabitants a view of movement toward the hill country and toward the area of Philistine [[gath|Gath]]. The excavators proposed identifying it with Shaaraim, a name conventionally understood to suggest two gates. The presence of two gates supports that proposal, although no inscription gives the ancient name of the site. Other identifications have been proposed. [@levin-qeiyafa, 73–76, 78–80]

## Archaeological History

Excavations directed by Yosef Garfinkel and Saar Ganor from 2007 to 2013 uncovered a short-lived fortified Iron Age town alongside remains from other periods. A [[city-gates|casemate wall]], built with a series of compartments between two parallel walls, enclosed the settlement. Houses incorporated parts of the wall into their plans. Radiocarbon measurements on charred olive pits place the occupation broadly around the turn of the millennium. The excavators have argued for a date around 1020–980 BCE, and other analyses allow a longer or later chronology.

Excavators interpret several rooms as settings for worship, based on objects such as stone and clay models of shrines. An [[khirbet-qeiyafa-ostracon|ink inscription on a sherd]] testifies to early alphabetic writing, though its language and content remain contested. The excavators understand the town as evidence for early Judahite organization, possibly during David’s reign. Other proposals associate the site with a northern or Saulide polity or with a local Canaanite group, and Philistine affiliation has also been proposed. The reported scarcity or absence of pig bones, pottery and the layout are components of these arguments. None of these features independently gives the town’s political affiliation. [@king-stager-life] [@mazar-archaeology]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

1 Sam 17:1–3 sets the encounter of Israelite and Philistine armies near the Elah Valley. The area and its routes give the story a recognizable setting. 1 Sam 17:52 mentions Shaaraim in the description of the Philistines’ flight, and Josh 15:36 lists it among Judah’s lowland towns. The proposal that these references point to Qeiyafa remains disputed. Neither the settlement nor its finds can independently attest the battle. [@levin-qeiyafa, 76–79]

Recessed frames on a stone shrine model from the site have been compared with architectural features in 1 Kgs 6:31, 33. The resemblance offers a proposal for understanding difficult language in the account of Solomon’s temple. The model’s scale and use differ from the building described in Kings. Qeiyafa establishes that a defended center existed in the region early in the Iron Age. The extent and nature of the authority behind it remain part of the [[low-chronology-debate|debate over Iron Age chronology and political organization]]. [@garfinkel-mumcuoglu-doorframes, 144–149, 153–155] [@levin-qeiyafa, 73–76]
