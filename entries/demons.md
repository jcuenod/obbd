## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern texts describe harmful beings and forces that could bring disease, frighten people, or threaten households. Mesopotamian rituals from the second and first millennia BCE give particularly detailed examples of how people understood and countered these dangers. The Hebrew Bible names several harmful beings and sometimes depicts an afflicting spirit sent by God. Its references vary by book and setting. Comparing these texts with their neighbors helps explain the language of danger and protection while distinguishing earlier portrayals from the more developed accounts of demons in some later Jewish and Christian writings.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian incantations, or words spoken as part of a ritual, describe several kinds of beings blamed for affliction. The series now commonly called Evil Demons gives examples of formulas addressed against harmful spirits. Such texts could accompany ritual actions used to remove a threat from a person or a house. The [[maqlu|Burning ritual]] dealt with harm attributed to witchcraft, a related but distinct concern. These texts preserve the explanations used by ritual experts, which may differ from how other people explained illness. [@cos1] [@ddd]

The images and texts associated with [[lamashtu-pazuzu-amulets|Lamashtu and Pazuzu]] show how dangerous powers could be opposed by other supernatural powers. Lamashtu threatened mothers and infants. Pazuzu's image could be used to ward off her attack, although he also had dangerous associations. Assyrian and Babylonian sources also speak of protective beings called *šēdu* and *lamassu*. Such cases make it important to ask what role a being plays in a particular text before assigning it to a fixed category. [@ddd] [@anep]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 32:17 calls the recipients of prohibited sacrifices *šēdîm*, a term often compared with Akkadian *šēdu*. Ps 106:37 uses the same Hebrew term in condemning the sacrifice of children. Lev 17:7 prohibits sacrifices to beings called *śəʿîrîm*, a word associated with goats or shaggy creatures. In Isa 13:21, related beings inhabit the devastated city of Babylon. These texts associate the beings with condemned worship or desolation without offering a single account of their origin. [@ddd]

In 1 Sam 16:14–23, a harmful spirit from YHWH afflicts Saul, and David's playing brings relief. Ps 91:5–6 speaks of threats associated with night and day, including pestilence and destruction. Some scholars read names or personified powers behind the Hebrew expressions for these dangers. The evidence differs for each term. [[resheph|Resheph]] is known as a deity elsewhere in the region, while the term rendered [[lilith|Lilith]] in some translations belongs to a separate debate about the inhabitants of ruined places. [@ddd]

## Interpretive Significance

The [[day-of-atonement-elimination|elimination rite]] in Lev 16:8–10 sends a goat away to Azazel. Some scholars understand Azazel as a wilderness being, while others take the name to refer to a place or to removal. The rite illustrates the diversity of biblical ways of approaching danger, which also include appeals to divine protection. In Tob 3:8, Asmodeus is named as a demon responsible for deadly harm. Mark 5:1–20 gives an extended account of an exorcism, meaning the expulsion of harmful spirits. These later texts make demonic activity more prominent than it is in most of the Hebrew Bible.
