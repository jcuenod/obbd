## Overview

The dromedary, or one-humped camel, became important in the first millennium BCE as a pack animal and mount in Arabia and the southern Levant. Finds from copper-working sites in the Arabah, together with Assyrian texts and images, help establish when camels were in regular use. Camels appear in Genesis as transport and signs of wealth and in Judges as mounts used by raiders. Archaeologists and biblical scholars compare those references with the material evidence when asking what period the stories' details reflect.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Bones alone do not always show whether a camel was wild or domesticated. Archaeologists use the location, date, and distribution of the bones, as well as comparisons with images and written records. Lidar Sapir-Hen and Erez Ben-Yosef studied remains from the Arabah, including copper-producing sites near [[timna|Timna]]. They dated the first substantial appearance of domestic camels in those deposits to the latter part of the tenth century BCE. Earlier isolated finds and pictures have been reported elsewhere, but their dates or evidence for domestication are often uncertain.

The [[kurkh-monoliths|Kurkh Monolith]] says that Gindibu the Arab brought a thousand camels to the battle of Qarqar in 853 BCE. Later Assyrian kings report receiving camels as tribute, and reliefs of Ashurbanipal show Arab camel riders. The two-humped camels pictured on the Black Obelisk belong to a different species, the Bactrian camel. By the later Iron Age, one-humped camels carried goods on long routes linking Arabia and the Levant [@rima3, A.0.102.2 ii 78–102] [@rinap1, no. 35 iii 1–5, 19] [@anep, figs. 63, 351–355].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 12:16 camels are among Abram's possessions. Gen 24:10–64 depicts Abraham's servant taking a group of camels on his journey and returning with Rebekah. Gen 37:25 describes an Ishmaelite caravan carrying goods to Egypt. These are scenes of regular transport, including travel and trade. The spices and gold borne by the queen of Sheba's camels in 1 Kgs 10:2 fit the setting of Arabian [[incense-trade|long-distance trade]].

Judg 6:5 and 7:12 portray the camels of [[midianites-and-kenites|Midian]] and its allies as too numerous to count. The scenes use the animals to convey the scale of the incursion. Camel-mounted groups fit securely documented conditions in the later Iron Age. This comparison can inform discussion of the stories' composition, though it supplies no exact date for the events they narrate.

## Interpretive Significance

Scholars have long argued that the patriarchal stories' use of camels is easier to explain in a first-millennium setting than in the earlier period in which those stories are often placed. The Arabah study strengthens the evidence for regular local use by the late tenth century BCE. A few earlier animals, if established, would show that the species was present without establishing a developed traffic in pack camels. The archaeological evidence concerns a feature of the narratives and cannot by itself date their entire composition.
