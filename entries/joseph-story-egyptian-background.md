## Overview

The Joseph story in Genesis 37–50 places much of its action in Egypt. It depicts a foreign captive who interprets dreams, enters royal service and develops a policy for famine. Egyptian names, court customs, river agriculture and embalming make the setting recognizable. Comparisons range across several periods of Egyptian history. Scholars debate whether the story preserves older traditions, reflects the experience of Judeans in first-millennium Egypt, or combines both kinds of material. Its setting also serves the plot about migration, family reconciliation and survival abroad.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Evidence from several periods attests people of Levantine origin in Egypt, from herders and laborers to high officials. Bay, a chancellor in the late Nineteenth Dynasty commonly assigned a Syrian origin, illustrates the possibility of a foreigner achieving high office. The [[papyrus-anastasi|Papyrus Anastasi VI]] reports that Egyptian officials admitted pastoral groups from Edom to water and pasture in the eastern Delta. It provides a comparison for a migration by herders during hardship, without identifying the group described in Genesis. Egyptian records also attest grain storage, and prescriptive texts and stories show a long interest in [[dreams-and-dream-interpretation|interpreting dreams]]. [@anet, 259, 553] [@ael]

The [[tale-of-two-brothers|Tale of Two Brothers]] compares well with Gen 39:7–20 in its opening episode, in which a woman tries to seduce a man and then accuses him falsely. The stories have different relationships among the characters and different outcomes. Egyptian iconography and texts also attest gifts associated with high rank, including gold ornaments. Such practices took several forms over time. Their resemblance to a biblical detail draws attention to the social meaning of that detail, while remaining less precise as a dating criterion. [@ael, 2:203–211] [@anet, 23–25] [@anep]

## In the Hebrew Bible

The dreams of Pharaoh’s officials in Genesis 40 lead to Joseph’s appearance at court, where Pharaoh’s specialists fail to explain his dreams in Gen 41:1–36. The action makes Joseph’s claim to receive interpretation from God a means of gaining authority. Pharaoh confers a signet ring, fine linen and a gold necklace and gives Joseph a wife from the priestly family of On, the Egyptian city known as Heliopolis (Gen 41:42–45). The narrative’s names are discussed in [[egyptian-loanwords|Egyptian loanwords]]. [@anet, 495–496] [@abd, 2:953–956, 3:976–981]

Gen 43:32 describes separate dining for the Egyptians and Hebrews. Gen 46:34 portrays Egyptians as averse to shepherds when Joseph’s household seeks settlement in Goshen. An Egyptian rule against all shepherds is unconfirmed. Gen 47:13–26 gives a famine policy that transfers land to Pharaoh, with a continuing levy of one-fifth and an exception for priests’ lands. A precise Egyptian counterpart for these arrangements has not been established. Gen 50:2–3, 22–26 mentions [[mummification|embalming]], mourning and Joseph’s death at 110, an age also valued as an ideal lifespan in Egyptian texts. [@ael] [@abd, 2:490–494]

## Interpretive Significance

Donald Redford stressed details that fit the Saite and early Persian periods, and Bernd Schipper has proposed a story about maintaining Judean identity in Persian-period Egypt. Kenneth Kitchen and James Hoffmeier emphasize second-millennium parallels. Many details have long histories. The land of Rameses in Gen 47:11 has a Ramesside reference point and may reflect an updated name in a story set earlier. The camel caravan of Gen 37:25–28 fits documented large-scale first-millennium caravan trade more readily than the earlier setting sometimes proposed. Philistines appear elsewhere in the ancestral narratives, for example in Gen 26:1, also raising the question of later geographical vocabulary. [@anet] [@abd]

The work is often read as a court tale or diaspora novella, a story about life away from an ancestral homeland, comparable in some respects to Esther and Daniel. Gerhard von Rad highlighted the sage’s character and the narrative’s wisdom themes. The Egyptian sources supply no identifiable record of Joseph. Proposed identifications with Imhotep, Amenhotep son of Hapu or a vizier of the Hyksos period remain unsubstantiated. The pyramids were royal funerary monuments, and their identification as Joseph’s granaries is a tradition without archaeological support. [@ael] [@abd, 2:953–956, 3:976–981]
