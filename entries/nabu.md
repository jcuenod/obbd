## Overview

Nabû was a Mesopotamian god associated with writing, wisdom, and scribal work. His chief temple at Borsippa was called Ezida, a name also borne by a major temple of Nabû in Assyria. First-millennium texts depict him as Marduk's son and connect him with the writing of destinies. Biblical writers knew his name in forms such as Nebo and in the names of Babylonian kings and officers. Isaiah uses the image of Nabû being carried as a burden in a poem about the powerlessness of Babylon's gods.

## Sources and Iconography

The temple Ezida at Borsippa was Nabû's principal cult center. Royal inscriptions describe its construction and restoration. The god also held a prominent place at Kalhu, modern [[nimrud|Nimrud]], where the temple of Nabû preserves evidence of the work of scholars and copyists. In Babylonian accounts he is associated with [[marduk|Marduk]] as son and scribe, roles that connect him with the writing and establishment of fates.

A stylus, often shown with a tablet or on a support, could serve as a symbol of Nabû in Mesopotamian art. The use of his name in personal names shows that his worship had a broad reach. Aramaic sources from Egypt also attest Nabû beyond Mesopotamia. A letter from the Hermopolis collection refers to his temple at Syene, illustrating the diversity of worship among Aramaic speakers in Egypt.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 46:1 pictures Nebo stooping alongside Bel while their images are loaded onto animals. The poem treats these gods as burdens that must be carried. It draws on the recognizable transport of cult statues and gives that movement a polemical meaning. The god honored in Babylon and Borsippa cannot protect the image or those carrying it.

The Akkadian name of [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar]] is *Nabû-kudurrī-uṣur*, conventionally understood as an appeal to Nabû to protect the heir. The god's name also appears in Nebuzaradan (2 Kgs 25:8), comparable to the Akkadian name *Nabû-zēru-iddina*, and Nebushazban (Jer 39:13), comparable to *Nabû-šēzibanni*. Dan 1:7 gives the name Abednego to Azariah. It is commonly understood as an altered form of a name meaning servant of Nabû, though the reason for its transmitted form remains uncertain.

## Scholarly Issues

Num 32:38 names the town of Nebo in Transjordan. Some interpreters connect the name of the town and the associated mountain with the god. The spelling allows the comparison, while the place name supplies little independent information about worship there. Names and symbols are most informative when their settings and wording can also be recovered.

Ezek 9:2–4 describes a man in linen carrying a scribal case and marking certain inhabitants of Jerusalem. Nabû provides background for the idea of a divine or heavenly scribe and for the association of writing with human fate. Ezekiel gives the figure a particular role in Jerusalem and leaves him unnamed. Comparison with Babylonian scribal imagery can illuminate the scene without identifying the figure.
