## Overview

Aramaic is a Semitic language closely related to Hebrew, with written evidence from Syria and upper Mesopotamia beginning in the early first millennium BCE. It spread through the Assyrian and Persian empires as a shared language of communication and remained in use in many regional forms. Parts of Ezra and Daniel are in Aramaic, and the New Testament preserves Aramaic expressions in Greek letters. The script used for most later Hebrew manuscripts also grew out of a variety of Aramaic writing, a development distinct from the spread of the language itself.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Scholars commonly divide written Aramaic into Old, Official or Imperial, Middle, and Late phases. They differ about where to place some of the boundaries. Regional varieties coexisted at every stage, and older spelling and literary conventions survived changes in speech. The labels primarily help readers compare bodies of texts.

Old Aramaic is represented by royal inscriptions such as the [[tell-fekheriye-statue|Tell Fekheriye statue]], which has versions in both Akkadian and Aramaic. The [[sefire-treaties|Sefire treaties]] near Aleppo show the language used in formal agreements. The [[tel-dan-stele|Tel Dan Stele]] uses it to commemorate a military victory in the southern Levant. These texts preserve differences in spelling and language as well as shared conventions [@kai, nos. 222–224, 309] [@cos2, 2.82].

Aramaic circulated alongside Akkadian under Assyria. The administration of [[persia|Achaemenid Persia]] used a relatively standard written Aramaic across great distances. The [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine documents]] from Egypt illustrate its use in letters, legal transactions, and public affairs [@tad, A4.7–A4.8, A6.1–A6.16]. In the centuries after Persian rule, authors wrote varieties often classified as Middle Aramaic. The Aramaic literary manuscripts found at Qumran are one example. Late Aramaic includes regional forms such as Classical Syriac and the Jewish Babylonian Aramaic of the Babylonian Talmud.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Aramaic can carry information about a scene even when the surrounding narrative is Hebrew. In Gen 31:47 Laban and Jacob give their cairn names in Aramaic and Hebrew respectively. In 2 Kgs 18:26 the Judahite officials ask the Assyrian envoy to speak Aramaic because they understand it and wish to keep the exchange from the people on the wall. Jer 10:11 is an isolated Aramaic verse within a Hebrew passage.

Ezra uses Aramaic in 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26. The first block combines correspondence with narrative, while the second presents Artaxerxes' directives to Ezra. Ezra 4:7 introduces an Aramaic letter in a way often understood to distinguish its writing from its language. Daniel switches to Aramaic in the middle of 2:4 when the court advisers address the king and continues through the vision in 7:28.

## Interpretive Significance

The language of Ezra and Daniel has been compared with Persian-period and later sources. A feature shared with an early document can persist in a later text or reflect conventional literary usage. A feature characteristic of later sources can also reflect scribal transmission. Arguments about the age of a document or the final form of a book therefore require other evidence as well as language.

Communities that wrote Hebrew gradually adopted an Aramaic-derived script during the Persian and Hellenistic periods. This led to the Jewish square script, named for the shape of its letters. Some scribes continued to use [[paleo-hebrew-script|the older Hebrew script]], which survives in some Qumran manuscripts. The same new script could represent Hebrew or Aramaic. Much later, the Greek text of Mark preserves and explains such Aramaic expressions as Jesus' address to the girl in Mark 5:41 and his cry from the cross in Mark 15:34.
