## Overview

The Sabbath is the seventh day of the week, set apart in biblical law for cessation from work. The Hebrew Bible connects it with creation, liberation from slavery and the covenant with YHWH. It also describes the day in connection with worship, trade and public life. Mesopotamian texts attest a full-moon observance and restrictions on selected days of the lunar month. The similarities have prompted proposals about the Sabbath's origins, although the evidence for Israel's continuous weekly cycle and the evidence for earlier lunar observances differ.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Akkadian *šapattu*, also written *šabattu*, ordinarily denotes the fifteenth day of the lunar month, near full moon. A lexical explanation describes it as a day of quieting the heart, commonly understood as appeasing a deity. The possible relationship of the word to Hebrew *šabbāt* remains debated. Mesopotamian [[hemerologies|hemerologies]] also assign restrictions to days such as the 7th, 14th, 19th, 21st and 28th in some months. The prohibitions can apply to specified people such as kings or ritual specialists. The days are reckoned within the month and have a separate terminology. Surviving Near Eastern sources offer no clear counterpart to the biblical [[seven-day-patterns|continuous week]] independent of the month. ## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 2:2–3 uses the verb *šābat*, “cease,” for God's stopping on the seventh day. The same verb underlies seventh-day cessation for a working household in Exod 23:12 and at plowing and harvest in Exod 34:21. The noun *šabbāt* first occurs in the biblical sequence at Exod 16:23. Exod 16:22–30 prescribes advance preparation of the manna and remaining in place on the seventh day. Exod 20:8–11 grounds rest in creation and uses *nwḥ*, “rest,” of God. Deut 5:12–15 grounds rest for dependents in Israel's liberation from slavery. Exod 31:12–17 calls the day a covenant sign, makes profanation a capital offense, and adds *npš*, “be refreshed,” to the language of divine cessation. Ezek 20:12–24 likewise treats Sabbaths as signs vulnerable to profanation. [@abd, 5:849–856]

The general legal term for work is *mĕlāʾkâ*. Exod 35:2–3 forbids kindling a fire, and Num 15:32–36 tells of the execution of a man who gathered wood. Lev 23:3 heads the festival calendar with a convocation and uses *šabbātôn*, a related noun for solemn rest. Lev 23:11, 15–16 reckons the offering and the count toward Weeks from the day after the Sabbath, an expression interpreted differently in antiquity. 2 Kgs 11:5–9 describes shifts of Temple guards changing on the Sabbath. [@abd, 5:849–856]

Jer 17:19–27 condemns carrying burdens through the city's gates or from houses. Neh 10:31 (MT 10:32) pledges abstention from buying, and Neh 13:15–22 describes action against treading, loading and trade. Isa 56:2–7 includes foreigners and eunuchs among Sabbath observers, and Isa 58:13–14 calls for setting aside one's own affairs. Jub 50:6–13 and the Damascus Document specify further restrictions. The later rabbinic classification gives thirty-nine principal categories of labor (m. Shabb. 7:2). [@abd, 5:849–856]

## Interpretive Significance

The pairing of Sabbath and [[new-moon|new moon]] in 2 Kgs 4:23, Amos 8:5, Hos 2:11 (MT 2:13) and Isa 1:13 has prompted a proposal that *šabbāt* earlier marked a full moon, and that the lunar feast merged with a separate practice of seventh-day cessation. The passages could also group distinct recurring observances. The late seventh-century [[mesad-hashavyahu-ostracon|Meṣad Ḥashavyahu ostracon]] may say “before the Sabbath” or “before stopping.” Fifth-century BCE ostraca from [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine]] mention *šbh* more securely. Personal names such as Shabbethai in Ezra 10:15, Neh 8:7 and 11:16 and Babylonian Šabbatāya give linguistic evidence. Neither the names nor the isolated references settle the earlier history of the weekly observance.
