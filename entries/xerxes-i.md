## Overview

Xerxes I was an Achaemenid king who succeeded Darius I in 486 BCE and ruled until 465 BCE. Royal inscriptions and the remains at Persepolis document his building activity and claims to rule. Greek sources emphasize the invasion of Greece. The Hebrew name Ahasuerus in Esther and Ezra is usually identified with Xerxes on linguistic grounds. That identification supplies a historical setting for the court narrative in Esther, while the events and several persons in the story cannot be independently identified.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Xerxes continued building at [[persepolis|Persepolis]], where his name appears on palace and gateway inscriptions. Babylonian documents and the archives at Persepolis supply different kinds of evidence for administration under [[persia|Achaemenid rule]]. In the inscription known as XPh, Xerxes credits Ahuramazda with helping him suppress rebellion and describes eliminating a place of worship of beings he calls *daivā*. The identity of these beings, the place concerned and the scope of any policy remain debated. [@anep, fig. 766] [@abd, 5:239–243]

Herodotus, particularly in books 7–9 of the *Histories*, recounts Xerxes's campaign in Greece, including Thermopylae and Salamis in 480 BCE. The account gives a Greek perspective on the campaign and on the Persian court. Herodotus names Amestris as Xerxes's wife. The surviving royal inscriptions focus on divine favor, imperial rule and construction. The military reverses in Greece fell early in a reign that continued for another fifteen years. Building records and texts from the empire's administrative centers preserve aspects of rule that receive less attention in Greek accounts. [@abd, 5:239–243] [@anep, fig. 766]

## Biblical Portrayal

Esth 1:1–2 presents Ahasuerus as the ruler of an extensive empire from his seat at [[susa|Susa]]. Esth 1:3–8 sets a royal banquet in his third year, and Esth 2:16 places Esther's arrival at court in his seventh. When read against Xerxes's regnal years, the latter falls after the invasion of Greece. The story makes no connection to that expedition. Its messengers, banquets and court vocabulary are compatible with a Persian setting. [@abd, 1:105–106, 2:637–640]

Ezra 4:6 places an accusation against the inhabitants of Judah and Jerusalem at the beginning of Ahasuerus's reign. Dan 9:1 names Ahasuerus as the father of Darius the Mede. The relationship in Daniel cannot be matched to the known Achaemenid succession. [@abd, 1:105–106, 2:637–640]

## Historical Assessment

Hebrew *ʾăḥašwērôš* corresponds closely to Old Persian *Xšayāršā* through forms comparable to those in Akkadian and Aramaic. The Greek version of Esther and Josephus use Artaxerxes, a different Persian royal name. The Hebrew name offers the clearest evidence for identifying the ruler intended by the Hebrew narrative. Amestris cannot securely be equated with either Vashti or Esther. Many scholars regard the narrative as a literary work set at a Persian court, and the parallels for court life do not establish the historicity of its plot. [@abd, 1:105–106, 2:637–640]
