## Overview

Paleo-Hebrew is the conventional name for the alphabetic script used in Iron Age Israel and Judah and retained by some later Jewish scribes. It developed within the same Northwest Semitic writing tradition as Phoenician. Inscriptions on stone, pottery, and seals record its use from official monuments to everyday correspondence. Most Jewish manuscripts later adopted an Aramaic-derived script, but older letter forms recur in select Dead Sea Scrolls and on coins. The distinction between script and language helps explain how Hebrew writing could change its appearance without changing the language it recorded.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Early Hebrew letter forms developed from the linear alphabet shared across the region. By the ninth and eighth centuries BCE, patterns in the shapes of letters such as *kaph* and *mem* help distinguish Hebrew writing from related scripts. The evidence also shows variation among places, writing surfaces, and scribes. Ink letters, incised legends, and monumental inscriptions offer different comparisons. Some administrative inscriptions use numerals derived from Egyptian cursive writing, although their alphabetic words belong to the local tradition.

Palaeography compares the forms and use of letters to identify scripts and estimate their dates. Work by Frank Moore Cross, Joseph Naveh, and Christopher Rollston has helped define sequences of change. A proposed date works best when compared with independently dated examples. Individual letters can retain older forms or change at different rates, so a script generally provides a range, rather than the precise year a text was written. The [[siloam-inscription|Siloam Inscription]] and Hebrew [[ostraca|ostraca]] offer examples from different media and settings.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 8:1 and Hab 2:2 describe writing a message on a surface where it can be read. Deut 27:2–8 calls for writing words of the law on plastered stones. Job 19:23–24 and Jer 17:1 use durable engraving to picture words or actions of lasting consequence. These texts illuminate expectations about visibility, material, and permanence. They do not describe enough letter forms to identify a particular alphabet in the passage.

Second Kings 18:26 distinguishes the language of Judah from Aramaic in a diplomatic encounter. Neh 13:24 describes concern over the languages spoken by children in mixed households, and Ezra 4:7 distinguishes aspects of writing and language in the correspondence with Persia. Speech and script are related but distinct categories. A scribe could record Hebrew in a script developed from Aramaic without translating the text into [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]].

## Interpretive Significance

Aramaic-derived Jewish writing eventually became the principal script for copying Hebrew. The old script continued in selected settings. Among the [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]], the manuscript 11QpaleoLeviticus uses old Hebrew letters throughout. Some otherwise square-script manuscripts write the divine name in older letter forms. Letters on Hasmonean coins and on issues of the Jewish revolts show the visual prestige of an old national script, although the motives for choosing it must be inferred from individual contexts. The Samaritan script preserves a later development from the older Hebrew family of scripts.

Writing occurs in archives, workshops, monumental settings, and military posts. Such distribution matters for the history of [[writing-and-literacy|literacy]], but the surviving inscriptions give no census of readers. Training, access to writing materials, and the ability to read would have varied with social position and setting.
