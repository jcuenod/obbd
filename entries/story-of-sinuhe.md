## Overview

The *Story of Sinuhe* is a Middle Kingdom Egyptian literary narrative about a courtier who flees Egypt when Amenemhat I dies. Sinuhe makes a home in the Levant, acquires land and a family, and defeats a local challenger. He returns late in life at the invitation of Senusret I, who provides for his burial. The story gives an Egyptian portrait of life among Levantine chiefs and a sustained account of the pull of home. Biblical readers have compared its scenes of flight, combat, and burial with episodes in Genesis, Exodus, and the narratives about David.

## Manuscripts and Date

The work survives in numerous copies, chiefly on papyrus and ostraca. Papyrus Berlin 3022 preserves much of the story, while Berlin 10499 preserves its opening. A large limestone ostracon now at the Ashmolean Museum (1945.40) attests its continued transmission in the New Kingdom. The setting is the early Twelfth Dynasty. Amenemhat's son Senusret receives news of the death while returning from a campaign, and Sinuhe overhears a message before he flees. The composition is usually placed in the Middle Kingdom, and later copies testify to a much longer history of reading and copying [@ael, 1:222–235; @anet, 18–22; @cos1, 1.38].

## Contents

Sinuhe crosses Egypt's northeastern frontier and eventually enters the household of Ammunenshi, a ruler of Upper Retenu. His host gives him his eldest daughter in marriage and lets him choose land. The resulting portrait of Yaa stresses its vines, figs, honey, oil, grain, and livestock. When a local champion challenges him, Sinuhe wounds him with an arrow and kills him with the man's own axe. Despite his success, Sinuhe asks his god to bring him home. A royal letter offers him a return and an Egyptian funeral. In Egypt, court servants remove the signs of his life abroad, and the king provides him with a tomb.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Moses also flees Egypt and marries into a household that receives him abroad (Exod 2:15–22). Hadad of Edom travels in the other direction, finding protection and marriage into the Pharaoh's family (1 Kgs 11:17–22). These scenes illustrate how hospitality and marriage could connect a displaced person with a host household. Sinuhe's desire for burial in Egypt gives the story a further point of comparison with Jacob's insistence on burial with his ancestors outside Egypt (Gen 47:29–30).

Sinuhe's fight and David's encounter with Goliath both culminate in the use of the defeated fighter's own weapon (1 Sam 17:48–51). David's victory shapes Israel's battle with the Philistines, while Sinuhe's victory secures his standing where he has settled. The account of Yaa's produce also belongs alongside biblical portrayals of the Levant's agricultural abundance (Deut 8:7–9; Num 13:23–27).

## Scholarly Issues

The story borrows the first-person conventions of Egyptian tomb autobiography while developing a courtier's uncertain motives and changing fortunes. Its royal letter and precise setting lend verisimilitude. Their presence leaves open whether any historical courtier lies behind the protagonist. The location of Yaa is uncertain, and the story constructs its picture of Retenu through Sinuhe's Egyptian point of view. The biblical parallels concern recurring plots and social practices. A claim of literary dependence would require evidence of transmission.
