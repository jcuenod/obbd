## Overview

The Galilee Boat is a wooden hull recovered in 1986 from the shore of the Sea of Galilee near Ginosar. The surviving hull measures about 8.2 meters long and belongs to the late Hellenistic or early Roman period, roughly the first century BCE or the first century CE. Its joinery and repairs give unusually detailed evidence for the construction and upkeep of boats on the lake. The hull illustrates the kind of craft available to fishers and passengers in the Gospel narratives.

## Description and Provenance

When the lake receded in 1986, two local brothers noticed parts of the vessel in the exposed shoreline mud. The mud had protected the lower hull for centuries. Archaeologists excavated the boat, placed a protective casing around the waterlogged wood, and floated it to a place where it could undergo conservation. The hull is displayed at the Yigal Allon Centre in Ginosar.

Pottery recovered in and around the hull, radiocarbon tests, and construction features together place the remains within a broad span around the turn of the era. The excavators measured a surviving width of about 2.3 meters, with a depth of about 1.2 meters. The upper hull and equipment were largely missing. Reconstructions of its appearance above the surviving timbers and of its rigging depend on comparisons and naval analysis.

## Contents

Boatbuilders assembled the shell first, joining neighboring planks with wooden tenons set in matching slots. Internal frames subsequently reinforced the shell. Cedar predominates among the hull planks and oak among the frames, with a range of other woods in lesser quantities. Repairs and reused timbers indicate that boatbuilders replaced and adapted parts over the vessel's working life.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Mark introduces the fishers on the lake through their nets, their boat, and the hired workers who stay with Zebedee (Mark 1:16–20). Luke describes partners in a second boat coming to help with a catch (Luke 5:1–11), and John describes fishers working at night and hauling a net toward shore (John 21:1–11). The Galilee hull gives these accounts a material scale and illustrates the labor involved in keeping such a craft in service. Matt 13:47–48 pictures a dragnet being drawn onto the shore for sorting, another task in the fishery with which the vessel is generally associated.

In Mark 4:35–41 the disciples cross the lake and encounter waves that fill their boat, while Jesus sleeps at the stern. A hull of this size gives readers a sense of the space available to a small crew and passengers. The remains give no direct evidence for the names or occupation of the people who used this particular vessel.

## Scholarly Issues

The excavators compared the hull with the depiction of a boat in a mosaic from Magdala and with [[josephus|Josephus's]] accounts of crews and vessels on the lake (War 2.635–641 and 3.522–531). Reconstructions suggest four rowers and a helmsman, with room for additional people. Crew and passenger numbers depend on how the boat was equipped and loaded. The nickname “Jesus Boat” reflects the date and setting of the discovery.
