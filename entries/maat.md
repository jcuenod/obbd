## Overview

*Maat* is the Egyptian idea of right order, encompassing truth, justice, and the proper functioning of society and the world. Egyptian writers and artists also represent Maat as a goddess, commonly marked by an ostrich feather. Maintaining maat was a duty of kings and a standard for judging the dead. This combination of moral, political, and cosmic meanings has been compared with biblical pictures of justice supporting a throne and with the personification of Wisdom in Proverbs. The parallels differ in kind and strength.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In [[egyptian-kingship|Egyptian royal ideology]], the king maintains maat through rule, ritual, and fair judgment. Temple scenes can show him presenting a small figure of Maat to a god. The action expresses the king's provision of the right order through which divine and human life continue. Texts of instruction encourage conduct consistent with maat. The [[eloquent-peasant|Eloquent Peasant]], for example, associates sound judgment with the duties of those in authority [@ael] [@anep].

Maat also matters in accounts of judgment after death. In the [[book-of-the-dead|Book of the Dead]], the deceased's heart is weighed in a divine tribunal. The feather or figure of Maat gives visible form to the standard of judgment. This use connects an individual's life with the broader principle of right order.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ps 89:14 (MT 89:15) and Ps 97:2 describe righteousness and justice as the foundation of God's throne. Prov 16:12 says that righteousness establishes a throne, and Isa 9:7 (MT 9:6) associates justice and righteousness with the security of the Davidic throne. Hellmut Brunner compared this imagery with Egyptian art, where the form under a divine or royal throne can resemble the sign used for maat. The comparison gives the Hebrew figure a possible visual setting. The image of justice sustaining government also makes sense within a broader ancient Near Eastern ideal of kingship [@keel-symbolism].

Prov 8:22–31 presents Wisdom as a woman present before or at the making of the world. Prov 3:16 portrays her with gifts in her hands. Christa Kayatz proposed that Egyptian Maat contributed to this portrayal. Other scholars have compared Isis, Levantine figures, or developments within Israelite wisdom writing. The proposals differ in the features they explain. Neither passage identifies Wisdom as a goddess worshipped in Egypt.

## Interpretive Significance

Hans Heinrich Schmid drew on maat in arguing that biblical righteousness could express a wider order of relations. Critics have questioned the extent to which one concept of world order can explain the varied uses of Hebrew words for righteousness. The comparison is strongest where a text explicitly brings together moral order, the king's role, and the stability of rule. Where Proverbs personifies Wisdom, the Egyptian evidence is one comparison among several for how an abstract quality can be given a voice and visible actions.
