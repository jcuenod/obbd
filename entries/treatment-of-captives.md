## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern accounts of war describe a range of outcomes for people captured in battle or in a defeated town. Some captives were displayed or killed, while others were deported, enslaved, or made to work. Royal texts and reliefs often selected acts of exceptional violence to demonstrate the consequences of resistance. Biblical narratives, laws, and prophecies describe or respond to similar practices. Reading the texts alongside the images helps distinguish what rulers claimed, what ancient writers condemned, and what the sources can show about the lives of captives.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Assyrian inscriptions credit Ashurnasirpal II with severe punishments and displays of defeated enemies. The [[lachish-reliefs|Lachish reliefs]] of Sennacherib include scenes understood as executions, alongside groups of people leaving the city with possessions. The artists composed such scenes for a palace, where the actions display the king's power and make resistance appear futile. The images and texts offer evidence for how punishments were remembered and publicized. They provide much less information about how frequently particular practices occurred.

Egyptian reliefs at [[medinet-habu-sea-peoples|Medinet Habu]] show body parts being counted as a record of enemy dead. The practice gives a point of comparison for trophies used to demonstrate that a demand or military objective had been fulfilled. Texts from Mesopotamia also attest different outcomes for captives, including forced work and relocation. See [[deportation|Deportation]] for the wider imperial organization of population transfers.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Judg 1:6–7 portrays the mutilation of Adoni-bezek in terms of the similar treatment he had imposed on other rulers. Nahash's demand for the loss of his opponents' right eyes in 1 Sam 11:2 makes bodily injury a mark of disgrace. In Judg 16:21 Samson is blinded and made to grind grain. The blinded Zedekiah is bound after his sons are killed and taken to Babylon (2 Kgs 25:7). The examples have different purposes and settings, though both accounts of blinding portray the loss of personal or political power.

In 1 Sam 18:25–27 Saul demands Philistine foreskins as a bride price and David brings them as proof of his exploits. In 2 Sam 8:2 David measures captured Moabites with a cord and distinguishes those killed from those left alive. Josh 10:26 pictures the bodies of five defeated kings displayed until evening. Isa 20:4 imagines captives led away barefoot and exposed, while 2 Kgs 19:28 turns the image of a hook used to control captives against the Assyrian king himself.

The laws of Deut 20:10–14 envisage surrender, enforced labor, killing, and seizure of persons and property as possible outcomes of a campaign. Deut 21:10–14 sets conditions for taking a captive woman as a wife and prohibits selling her if the marriage ends. These laws address the use of coercive power within the practices their writers recognized.

## Interpretive Significance

2 Kgs 8:12 and 15:16 use descriptions of violence against pregnant women, and Amos 1:3, 13 condemns extreme violence as part of war. Assyrian comparanda show that some of this language belonged to a wider repertoire of descriptions of conquest. Ps 137:9 voices the rage of displaced people in the image of violence against children. The texts range from a prophet's accusation to a claim of victory or a plea for retaliation. Genre and audience help determine what each account can establish historically.
