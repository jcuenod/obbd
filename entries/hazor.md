## Overview

Hazor, at Tell el-Qedah in northern Israel, was the largest Bronze Age city in the southern Levant. The upper mound and vast lower city preserve evidence of a major Canaanite center that traded with cities far beyond the region. The Late Bronze Age city ended in a severe fire. A smaller Iron Age city later occupied the upper mound and fell to Assyria. Hazor’s prominence suits the setting of Joshua 11, while the destruction and the date of the Iron Age gate remain central questions in comparing the excavation with Joshua and Kings.

## Location and Identification

The site overlooks the Huleh Basin and the routes that run through upper Galilee toward the Beqaa Valley and Damascus. The ancient city occupied the upper mound and an extensive plateau to its north. The scale of the ruins, the geography of biblical Hazor, and the ancient textual evidence for a prominent city of that name in this region converge on the identification. The Hazor mentioned with Kedar in Jer 49:28–33 belongs to a different geographical setting.

## Archaeological History

Excavations directed by Yigael Yadin established the principal outline of the city’s growth and destruction. Renewed excavations led by Amnon Ben-Tor and colleagues expanded the evidence from both the palace area and the lower city. The [[mari-letters|Mari correspondence]] documents Hazor’s long-distance connections in the Middle Bronze Age. The Late Bronze [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] include messages from Hazor (EA 227–228). EA 228 identifies its sender as ʿAbdi-Tirši, ruler of Hazor, addressing the Egyptian king. The excavated public buildings, including temples and the palace, fit the picture of a regional center with international contacts.

A major conflagration destroyed Late Bronze public buildings and brought the occupation of the lower city to an end. The destruction is usually placed in the thirteenth century BCE, with debate about its precise date. Burned architecture and damaged statuary show violence at the end of the city but cannot identify who caused it. Proposed agents include groups associated with early Israel and a revolt within the city. The Iron Age settlement on the upper mound eventually developed substantial fortifications, including a six-chambered gate and casemate wall.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 11:1–13 portrays Jabin of Hazor leading a coalition and identifies Hazor as the foremost city among those kingdoms. The archaeology supports the historical prominence presupposed by that portrait. It also provides evidence of a major fire, the fate Joshua assigns to Hazor. The agreement leaves the chronology of the narrative and the identity of the attackers open. Josh 19:36 includes Hazor in Naphtali’s allotment. Judg 4:2, 17 again places a king called Jabin at Hazor. Whether these passages reflect independent memories, related literary traditions, or a dynastic naming practice cannot be decided from the excavation.

First Kings 9:15 names Hazor with [[megiddo|Megiddo]] and [[gezer|Gezer]] as sites built up by Solomon. Yadin assigned Hazor’s Stratum X gate and wall to the tenth century on the basis of stratigraphy, pottery, and the biblical comparison. Other archaeologists favor a ninth-century date in the [[low-chronology-debate|Iron Age chronology debate]]. Similar gate plans make comparison useful but cannot establish that the gates shared a builder or date. The final Iron Age destruction in Stratum V is widely connected with the campaign of [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] around 732 BCE, consistent with the capture of Hazor reported in 2 Kgs 15:29.
