## Overview

Magic is a modern label for practices that use spoken or written formulas, objects, or ritual actions to protect, heal, or harm. Texts and objects from Bronze and Iron Age Egypt, Mesopotamia, Anatolia, and the Levant show that such practices could belong to accepted religious life as well as to activities that authorities condemned. The Bible likewise depicts both forbidden sorcery and approved rites that use material objects. The distinction is often less about what the action looks like than about who performs it, whose authority stands behind it, and what it is meant to accomplish.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In Mesopotamia, *āšipūtu* was the learned craft of the exorcist, a specialist whose work included rites against curses, illness, and [[demons|harmful spirits]]. Preserved incantations, or ritual formulas, could accompany actions involving figurines. The [[maqlu|Maqlû]] series treats *kišpū* as harmful sorcery to be countered, while [[shurpu|Šurpu]] addresses curses that need not come from a sorcerer. These texts distinguish feared acts from recognized forms of [[medicine-and-healing|healing]] without separating ritual from religion [@cos1, 1.168].

Egyptian *heka* was a divine power that people could draw on, including lector priests, whose duties included reciting ritual texts. Egyptian [[amulets]] and curved protective wands illustrate the material side of such rites. Hittite ritual texts from Anatolia likewise combine spoken formulas with objects and actions to remove sources of affliction [@cos1, 1.101]. At Ketef Hinnom in Jerusalem, two small rolled silver inscriptions bear blessings and are generally interpreted as protective objects [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 263–275]. The Aramaic incantation bowls of late antiquity illustrate the later history of written protective rites rather than their use in biblical Judah.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 18:10–15 prohibits a range of practices, including sorcery, charming, [[divination]], and [[necromancy]], then directs Israel to listen to a prophet raised up by YHWH. Hebrew *kšp* is conventionally glossed as practicing sorcery, *ḥbr* as casting a charm in this context, and *ʿnn* as a kind of divination; the precise practices are not always recoverable. Exod 22:18 (MT 22:17) prescribes death for a female practitioner of sorcery but gives no description of her activities. The King James Version's choice of a term associated with early modern witch trials belongs to the verse's later reception and does not define the ancient offense.

Pharaoh's ritual experts imitate the early signs but fail to reproduce the plague of insects and recognize divine agency (Exod 7:11, 22; 8:7, 18–19; MT 8:3, 14–15 for the chapter 8 references). The Hebrew title of these specialists is discussed under [[egyptian-loanwords]]; *lahaṭ* and *lāṭ* are conventionally glossed as secret arts or spells, without identifying a specific technique. Isa 47:9–13 mocks Babylon's sorceries and spells as unable to avert disaster. Ps 58:4–5 (MT 58:5–6) uses snake charming as a metaphor: the unresponsive serpent ignores the charmers, whose activity is described with a form of *lḥš*, a root associated with incantation.

Ezek 13:17–23 condemns women who prophesy and accuses them of ensnaring lives. The items called *kĕsātôt* and *mispāḥôt* are often rendered as bands and veils, but their identification, use, and the nature of the rite are disputed. By contrast, Num 5:11–31 prescribes a priestly ordeal for suspected adultery, in which a priest washes a written curse into water that the woman drinks. Both texts involve material objects, but Numbers presents the procedure as an instruction from YHWH, not as a practice he condemns.

## Interpretive Significance

Elisha uses salt at a spring, flour in a pot of stew, and a stick where an iron axe head has sunk (2 Kgs 2:19–22; 4:38–41; 6:1–7). Whether to classify these actions as magic is debated; at the spring Elisha explicitly attributes the healing to YHWH. In Num 21:8–9, the bronze serpent serves as a means of healing at YHWH's command. Material actions invite comparison across traditions, but resemblance does not establish borrowing or explain how each narrative understands the act.

In Acts 8:9–24, Peter rebukes Simon, a practitioner of magic in Samaria, for offering money for the authority to confer the Holy Spirit. At Ephesus, traveling exorcists unsuccessfully invoke Jesus' name, and practitioners publicly burn books valued in the narrative at fifty thousand units of silver (Acts 19:13–20). Both accounts distinguish divine authority from power that can be purchased or wielded by repeating a name.
