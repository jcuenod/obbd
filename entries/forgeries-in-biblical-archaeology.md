## Overview

A forgery in biblical archaeology is an object or inscription made or altered in modern times and presented as ancient evidence. Disputed examples range from manuscripts offered for sale in the nineteenth century to fragments tested in the twenty-first. Establishing authenticity can require examining where an object came from, how its letters were formed, and how an inscription relates to its surface. A genuine ancient object can carry a modern addition. These distinctions matter particularly when an inscription appears to name a biblical person or confirm a biblical event that otherwise has little external evidence.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The first question is provenance, the record of where and under what circumstances an object was found. A documented archaeological context links an inscription to other evidence, such as a building, layer, or associated objects. Many [[seals-and-bullae|seals and seal impressions]] entered collections from the antiquities market and have no recorded excavation context. These can be compared with securely excavated examples, although the comparison cannot restore the information lost when the object was removed [@avigad-sass-wsss, 453–460, 548–551] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions].

Scholars examine writing through palaeography, the comparison of letter forms and writing habits. They may also study the material of an object and its patina, a surface layer that forms with age or exposure. Petrography, the study of a material's mineral and rock content, can help identify the origin of stone or clay. A suspicious reading or an unusual surface feature may warrant further study. The age of the substrate and the age of the writing are separate questions.

In the nineteenth century, Moses Wilhelm Shapira sold inscribed objects described as Moabite antiquities that were later rejected as forgeries. In 1883 he offered leather strips said to preserve an ancient form of Deuteronomy. Most specialists have regarded those strips as modern, although Idan Dershowitz argued in 2021 that they represent an early form of Deuteronomy. The originals have not been located, limiting the current debate to descriptions, images, transcriptions, and related historical evidence.

## In the Hebrew Bible

The unprovenanced Jehoash Inscription appears to describe repairs to the Jerusalem temple, an episode told in 2 Kgs 12:4–16 (MT 12:5–17). Specialists have challenged the language and the surface of the inscription, and it is widely judged to be modern. Another disputed inscription on an ancient ivory object shaped as a pomegranate was proposed as a reference to the temple. Pomegranates appear on priestly clothing in Exod 28:33–34. The biblical motif alone cannot identify the origin or use of the object, and investigators disagreed about the age of its lettering.

The James Ossuary is an ancient burial box with an inscription that includes the words “brother of Jesus.” Gal 1:19 identifies James as the Lord's brother, making the proposed association particularly prominent. The age of all or part of the inscription and the identity of the people named remain disputed. The 2012 acquittal in the forgery case established that the criminal charge had not been proved. It did not establish the inscription's authenticity or its connection to the James named by Paul.

## Interpretive Significance

Two inscribed pottery fragments from the Moussaieff collection illustrate the problem of stories that fit an expected biblical setting unusually well. One appears to refer to a payment for the temple of YHWH. Their inscriptions and surface deposits have been challenged, and they cannot bear the same historical weight as items from documented excavations. In 2020, an independent examination commissioned by the Museum of the Bible concluded that all sixteen fragments in its own collection marketed as [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] had modern writing.

Publishing an unprovenanced object can provide information, but it can also increase interest in objects that entered the market through illicit excavation. An object's evidence can be discussed with its origin, custody, and tests clearly stated. That allows readers to distinguish a reading of the text from judgments about the writing's age and a possible identification with a biblical figure.
