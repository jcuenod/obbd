## Overview

The Caiaphas ossuary is a decorated limestone bone box from a first-century CE tomb found in Jerusalem in 1990. It bears an Aramaic inscription naming Joseph and a name commonly read as Caiaphas. The combination has led many scholars to identify the box with Joseph Caiaphas, the high priest connected with Jesus' arrest and trial in the Gospels. The archaeological context and the names support the identification. The lettering, the absence of an official title, and the use of the box for multiple burials leave it short of certainty.

## Description and Provenance

The box was recovered during a rescue excavation of a rock-cut burial cave in Jerusalem's Peace Forest. The cave contained several [[ossuaries|ossuaries, or boxes for bones collected after a body had decayed]]. The decorated example has carved rosettes, but its inscriptions are scratched into the stone separately from the ornament. The tomb gives the box an archaeological context that can be assessed alongside its lettering and style.

## Contents

The fuller inscription is commonly read *Yehosef bar Qayafa*, meaning “Joseph son of Caiaphas.” Shorter writing on the box gives the name in a form read with a different spelling. If Caiaphas functioned as a family name, the reference could fit the way other sources identify a priest called Joseph Caiaphas. The bones found inside represent several people, which prevents assigning the box or its contents exclusively to one individual.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

[[josephus|Josephus]] records that the Roman governor Valerius Gratus appointed Joseph called Caiaphas and that Vitellius later removed him. Matt 26:3, 57 calls Caiaphas the high priest and places him at the center of proceedings against Jesus. John 11:49–53 depicts him advising that one man should die for the people, and John 18:13–14, 24 associates him with Annas by marriage. The inscription is relevant to the priest's name and family setting. It does not bear on the details of the proceedings described in the Gospels.

## Scholarly Issues

The match between Joseph and a relatively unusual family designation in the right period and city makes the identification plausible. The spelling and interpretation of the name have been debated. There is no priestly title on the box, and the tomb is modest compared with some elite monuments of the period. Neither consideration rules out the identification, but both limit the confidence with which it can be made.

A separate ossuary published in 2011 bears an inscription naming Miriam, daughter of Yeshua, son of Caiaphas, and naming a priestly group. Its publishers argued that it is ancient based in part on physical examination. It was recovered from looters, so its original setting is unknown. It may give additional evidence for the Caiaphas name in a priestly context without establishing the family relationships of the people buried in the Peace Forest tomb.
