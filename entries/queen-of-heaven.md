## Overview

The Queen of Heaven is a goddess whose worship Jeremiah describes among Judahites in Jerusalem and Egypt. Families prepare cakes, and speakers in Egypt defend offerings made with the participation of their households. Related titles and offerings occur elsewhere in the ancient Near East, including Mesopotamian worship of Ishtar and a Persian-period Aramaic letter from Egypt. Several goddesses have been proposed as the figure behind the title. The biblical evidence is clearest about the practices Jeremiah condemned and the value his opponents placed on them.

## Sources and Iconography

Mesopotamian texts give [[ishtar|Ishtar]] titles associating her with celestial rule. The Hebrew name for the cakes offered to the Queen of Heaven, *kawwānîm*, is commonly compared with Akkadian *kamānu*, a type of cake used as an offering. Some scholars have compared molds bearing female imagery. The shape or subject of an individual mold is difficult to connect with a particular goddess or rite, and the comparison does not identify the cakes in Jeremiah. [@ddd] [@anep]

An Aramaic letter found at Hermopolis sends greetings to the temples of Bethel and the Queen of Heaven at Syene in Egypt. It attests the use of the title in a Persian-period setting. The letter is distinct from the documents found at [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine]], although Syene and Elephantine were neighboring communities. Related letters from Hermopolis names several deities worshiped at Syene. The title does not necessarily designate the same goddess in every community. [@tad, A2.1]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Jer 7:18 children gather wood, fathers light the fire and women prepare cakes. The description presents a shared household practice. In Jer 44:15–19, 25 Judahites in Egypt defend their offerings, saying that prosperity accompanied the practice and that hardship followed its abandonment. Women speak prominently in the defense and refer to their husbands’ awareness. The text mentions cakes, drink offerings and the burning of offerings. Versions differ in details of how the cakes represented or honored the goddess. [@abd] [@ddd]

Jeremiah’s dispute is about the explanation of disaster as well as the legitimacy of worship. The speakers regard the goddess as a source of food and security, while Jeremiah assigns their calamities to religious disobedience. The two accounts of responsibility explain why the participants interpret the same events differently. The description preserves an unusually detailed biblical view of how household worship was organized and defended. [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

The Masoretic vocalization of the Hebrew letters for queen is unusual and has been read in other ways, including in relation to the host of heaven. Many editions read the title Queen of Heaven, in agreement with the offerings and with the Aramaic parallel. Ishtar and the West Semitic [[astarte|Astarte]] are common candidates, and some scholars consider a local combination of traditions. [[anat|Anat]] and Asherah have also been proposed. The title and the kind of offering narrow the comparison, while the texts leave the identity unresolved. [@ddd] [@abd]
