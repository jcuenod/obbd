## Overview

Some Iron Age altars from the southern Levant have projections resembling horns at their upper corners. Examples range from a large stone altar reconstructed at Beersheba to smaller objects from Megiddo and Ekron that may have held incense or other offerings. Biblical texts describe horned altars in the tabernacle and place the horns at the center of some rites. The stories of Adonijah and Joab also present grasping the horns as an appeal for protection. These uses show the prominence of the feature even though its origin and symbolism remain uncertain.

## Archaeological Evidence

A large horned altar excavated at [[beersheba|Beersheba]] was reconstructed from dressed stone blocks recovered in later construction. Three of its horns were preserved. The reuse shows that the original installation was dismantled. Yohanan Aharoni linked its removal with the reform attributed to Hezekiah in 2 Kgs 18:4, 22. Both the stratigraphic interpretation and the historical link to the [[cult-reforms-archaeology|reform]] have been debated [@neaehl, 1:170–173] [@mazar-archaeology, 492–501].

Smaller stone altars bearing four projections have been found at [[megiddo|Megiddo]] and [[ekron|Ekron]]. An isolated horn-shaped stone at Dan has been interpreted as part of a larger altar. The smaller examples are often called [[incense-and-incense-altars|incense altars]], although oil or other offerings are also possible. Size, find context, and residues, where available, give more information about use than the horns alone [@neaehl, 1:328–330, 5:1955–1957] [@anep, fig. 575].

## In the Bible

The plan for the tabernacle in Exod 27:2 gives the main altar a horn at each corner. In Exod 29:12 blood is put on its horns during priestly ordination. Lev 4:7, 18, 25, 30, 34 likewise assigns blood to horns, using different altars according to the kind of purification offering. Exod 30:2–3, 10 gives the golden incense altar horns and prescribes an annual rite of atonement upon them. Lev 16:18 also calls for blood to be applied to the horns in a Day of Atonement rite. The projections provide a specific surface for applying blood.

In 1 Kgs 1:50–51 Adonijah asks for his life to be spared while holding the altar's horns. Joab does the same in 1 Kgs 2:28 and is subsequently killed (1 Kgs 2:29–34). The episodes show that the act could function as an appeal for [[asylum-and-cities-of-refuge|asylum]] before a king. Its outcome depended on how the claim was judged.

Amos 3:14 promises that the horns of Bethel's altar will be cut off, making damage to a visible part of the sanctuary stand for its loss of power. Jer 17:1 depicts sin engraved on altar horns. Ps 118:27 connects the horns with a difficult phrase about a festal offering or procession. Rev 9:13 imagines a voice coming from the four horns of a golden altar. Some interpreters see the shape as a sign of strength or an echo of animal horns. The biblical uses establish its ritual importance, while the reason the form was first adopted remains uncertain.
