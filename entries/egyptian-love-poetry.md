## Overview

Several collections of love poetry survive from New Kingdom Egypt, mainly from the Ramesside period. Written on papyrus and on fragments of a ceramic vessel, the poems give voice to lovers who praise one another, seek meetings, and fall sick with longing. They often call the beloved brother or sister. These Egyptian poems offer some of the closest ancient parallels to the language and imagery of the Song of Songs. The similarities help explain particular conventions of love poetry, while the surviving sources are separated by centuries and belong to different literary settings.

## Manuscripts and Date

The main collections appear in Papyrus Chester Beatty I, Papyrus Harris 500, a fragmentary Turin papyrus, and fragments of a vessel from Deir el-Medina now in Cairo. The surviving copies come from the later New Kingdom. Some preserve sequences of stanzas, and others preserve isolated poems or fragmentary groups. The date of a manuscript establishes roughly when the material was copied, while the original composition dates may be earlier [@ael].

## Contents

The speakers include young men and young women, sometimes taking turns within a sequence. They describe the beloved's body, ask for a meeting, or complain about separation. Terms for sister and brother are used as familiar forms of address between lovers. In one Chester Beatty song, a man explains that after several days without seeing his beloved he is stricken, and her visit would restore him more effectively than medical care.

Gardens, fruit trees, shade, and fragrance give the poets ways to describe attraction and the expectation of a meeting. Songs can also give speech to the garden's trees. Other poems place a meeting or a missed encounter at the beloved's house. The variety of settings and voices shows that the motifs could be adapted to different social and emotional situations [@ael].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The speaker's address to his sister and bride in Song 4:9–12 resembles the Egyptian use of *snt* (“sister”) for a lover. The comparison supports reading the address as poetic language of intimacy. In Song 4:1–7, praise moves through parts of the woman's body, transforming them through comparisons. Chester Beatty I contains a comparable description of the beloved's physical qualities. In both collections such praise is a recognizable form of poetry.

The woman calls herself sick with love in Song 2:5 and 5:8. The Egyptian scene in which the lover's sickness responds to the beloved's presence offers a close parallel. The garden in Song 4:12–5:1 combines an enclosed place, fruit, and spices in a scene of desire. Song 5:2–6 portrays a lover at the door and a meeting that fails to occur. The Egyptian poetry helps readers recognize gardens, sickness, and the threshold as motifs that can carry emotional and erotic force.

## Scholarly Issues

Michael V. Fox made a detailed comparison of the Song of Songs with the Egyptian collections. The parallels are strongest in poetic forms and the use of recurring images. The gap in time and the lack of an identified transmission history leave a specific literary dependence unestablished. The Egyptian poems are generally treated as human love poetry, though their original performance settings are uncertain. They offer a parallel for a reading of the Song as love poetry. The [[sumerian-love-songs|Sumerian love lyrics]] include material associated with [[sacred-marriage|sacred marriage]], a separate context to consider when discussing possible settings for the Song.
