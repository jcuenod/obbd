## Overview

Tel Jezreel overlooks the eastern part of the Jezreel Valley in northern Israel. Excavations uncovered a large Iron Age enclosure often associated with the dynasty of Omri, as well as an extensive agricultural landscape. Kings presents Jezreel as a place of royal residence, the site of Naboth’s vineyard and a center of Jehu’s revolt. The enclosure’s date affects regional debates about Iron Age pottery and architecture. The nearby installations illustrate ancient agriculture, although no excavated feature can be assigned to Naboth by name.

## Location and Identification

The mound occupies an elevated position on the southern side of the valley, near a spring and routes leading east toward Beth-shean. The view over the valley and access to water and farmland suited a substantial settlement. Newer investigations have identified remains below the mound as well as on it. The setting helps explain the reference to troops encamped by the spring in 1 Sam 29:1. [@neaehl, 5:1837–1840] [@abd, 3:849–851]

## Archaeological History

David Ussishkin and John Woodhead excavated the site in the 1990s and uncovered a large rectangular enclosure. Its defenses included towers, a rock-cut ditch and exterior walls divided into compartments known as casemates. The excavators associated its main occupation with the ninth-century BCE [[omri|Omride dynasty]]. Others have proposed a somewhat later date for the enclosure. Its brief duration as reconstructed by the excavators gives the associated pottery particular importance. Comparisons with Iron Age levels at other sites enter the [[low-chronology-debate|chronology debate]] over whether some buildings often assigned to the tenth century belong to the ninth century. [@neaehl, 5:1837–1840] [@mazar-archaeology]

The enclosure could have served military or administrative purposes. Its location and plan have led to proposals that it housed chariots or troops. Neither that specialized purpose nor the cause of the destruction is firmly established. The destruction has been attributed to [[jehu|Jehu’s]] revolt or to action by [[hazael|Hazael]] of Damascus, among other possibilities. The excavated layers give evidence of the enclosure’s occupation and end, while the name of the responsible party would require additional evidence. [@neaehl, 5:1837–1840] [@abd, 3:849–851]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 1 Kgs 18:45–46, Ahab rides to Jezreel and Elijah runs before him. In 1 Kings 21, Ahab seeks to acquire Naboth’s vineyard beside the palace. A renewed survey and excavations led by Norma Franklin and Jennie Ebeling investigated a rock-cut winery and other agricultural installations near the mound. The date of the winery’s first use is debated. It illustrates the local capacity for processing grapes, while its connection with the specific owners and events in Kings is unverified. [@neaehl, 5:1837–1840] [@king-stager-life]

2 Kgs 9:14–37 relates that Joram and Jezebel die at Jezreel during Jehu’s takeover. In 2 Kgs 10:1–11, the heads of members of Ahab’s family are brought to the gate from Samaria. The fortified compound provides a plausible physical setting for movement through an entrance, even though the story cannot be tied to particular excavated masonry. Hos 1:4–5 remembers the bloodshed of Jezreel as a reason for judgment on Jehu’s dynasty. The place name thus carries political and literary significance beyond the enclosure’s architectural history. [@neaehl, 5:1837–1840] [@abd, 3:849–851]
