## Overview

Copper was mined, processed, and traded across the ancient Near East for tools, ornaments, and vessels. In the southern Levant, Timna and Faynan preserve the remains of substantial production in the dry Arabah valley, including mines, furnaces, and heaps of waste. Earlier operations at Timna show Egyptian involvement, and production expanded under local groups in the early Iron Age. Biblical passages refer to mining copper, smelting ore, and casting bronze for the Jerusalem temple. The archaeological record helps explain how these activities worked and what they required of the people who organized them.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Smelting separates metal from its ore by heating it in a furnace under controlled conditions. It produces metal and slag, the solid waste left from processing the ore. Slag, furnaces, and ore can reveal the scale and date of production even when much of the usable metal was carried elsewhere. Copper could be worked on its own or combined with tin to make bronze.

At [[timna|Timna]], Egyptian New Kingdom expeditions left inscriptions and a shrine to Hathor, an Egyptian goddess. The shrine also yielded a copper serpent. Evidence from furnaces, slag, and radiocarbon dating shows intensive Iron Age production after the Egyptians withdrew. At [[faynan|Faynan]], across the Arabah in southern Jordan, Khirbat en-Naḥas preserves substantial evidence of production during the tenth century BCE and surrounding centuries.

Late Bronze Age trade moved raw metals across long distances. Letters from the ruler of Alashiya, generally identified with Cyprus, record deliveries of copper to Egypt [@moran-amarna]. The Uluburun shipwreck off southern Türkiye carried large quantities of copper ingots, most associated with Cypriot ores, as well as tin ingots. Tin came from different sources, several of which remain debated. The cargo shows that bronze production depended on networks able to bring the metals together.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Hebrew *nəḥōšet* can refer to copper or to bronze. The surrounding description often suggests which is intended. Deut 8:9 describes a land where copper can be dug from the hills, and Job 28:1–11 depicts people working below ground and smelting copper-bearing rock. Ezek 22:18–22 compares the people to the residue discarded when metals are processed. The image depends on the distinction between useful metal and what remains in the furnace.

The temple account describes Hiram producing metal objects including the [[bronze-sea|Bronze Sea]] and reports casting in the clay ground of the Jordan Valley (1 Kgs 7:13–47). Num 21:4–9 describes a metal serpent used in a different setting, with the account focused on the people's survival. The serpent from Timna supplies a local comparison for the form and material of the [[nehushtan|biblical serpent]]. It does not establish any historical link to the episode.

## Interpretive Significance

The popular label “King Solomon's Mines,” associated with Nelson Glueck, implies a political identification for which the excavated sites provide no direct evidence. Erez Ben-Yosef has argued that early copper production could have been organized by a complex society with a substantial mobile population, possibly an early Edomite polity. He has also argued that an emphasis on stone buildings can obscure such societies in the archaeological record. Other scholars question whether the sites were governed by one political authority or whether that authority can be called [[edomites|Edomite]] so early. The production evidence is firmer than the political identification.
