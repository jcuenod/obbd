## Overview

Ritual purity in the Bible concerns a person’s, object’s, or place’s fitness for particular activities, especially access to sacred space. Conditions such as childbirth or contact with a corpse could produce impurity without implying wrongdoing. Other passages describe grave offenses as polluting a sanctuary or land. Ancient Near Eastern temples likewise regulated the condition of people handling offerings or approaching divine images. The comparisons clarify some practical aims of purity rules while leaving room for different biblical accounts of how impurity arises, spreads, and ends.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Priests and other workers at temples could be required to wash, wear clean clothing, or avoid activities for a period before handling sacred property. Egyptian sources use a term meaning “pure one” for a class of temple personnel. Mesopotamian rules include restrictions described as *ikkibu*, a prohibition or taboo, whose objects vary with ritual and institution. The [[hittite-instructions-temple-officials|Hittite Instructions for Temple Officials]] regulate bodily preparation and guard the food destined for the gods. These are concrete cases of controlling access and preserving ritual order. They are too varied to constitute one regional purity code.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Lev 10:10 names distinctions between holy and ordinary and between unclean and clean. These categories govern different questions. Something ordinary can be clean, while eligibility for a sacred activity can require particular preparation. Lev 11–15 describes conditions related to animals, bodily discharges, childbirth, and skin or surface disorders. Some conditions end after washing and waiting, and some call for a priestly examination or an offering. Num 19 addresses contact with the dead. Such contact can occur through ordinary and necessary activity.

Num 5:1–4 separates certain affected people from the camp because of the divine presence. Lev 16:16, 19 describes purging the sanctuary from accumulated impurity, and Lev 18:24–28 associates prohibited acts with pollution of the land. These passages distinguish forms of contamination that can arise through routine life from the effects of offenses. A rite’s use of purification language does not make every impure person morally culpable.

Hag 2:10–14 uses questions about transfer of holiness and impurity to make a point about the community. Ezek 44:23 assigns priests responsibility for teaching the distinctions. Isa 6:5–7 and Ps 51:7 draw on the language and action of purifying in accounts of unclean lips and the removal of wrongdoing. Mark 7:1–23 starts with a dispute over washing and develops a distinction between what enters a person and conduct proceeding from within. Each passage uses impurity language within a different argument.

## Interpretive Significance

Mary Douglas emphasized classification and symbolic order. Jacob Milgrom examined the effects of impurity on the sanctuary and interpreted several rules in relation to life and death. Jonathan Klawans distinguished ritual impurity from what he calls moral impurity, the defiling effects of serious transgression. These approaches can explain different parts of the evidence. The biblical rules discuss time, access, bodies, and the divine presence. A general explanation based on hygiene cannot account for their different categories, consequences, and remedies.
