## Overview

Three biblical laws forbid preparing a young goat in its mother’s milk. Exodus places the rule beside instructions for festivals and firstfruits, while Deuteronomy places it among food laws. Ancient and modern readers have proposed ethical, symbolic and ritual reasons for the prohibition. An influential claim that the law opposed a Canaanite rite relied on a disputed reading of a tablet from Ugarit. Later rabbinic practice developed a broader rule separating meat and dairy, making the short biblical instruction the basis for a more extensive tradition.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

A Late Bronze Age tablet from Ugarit describing the [[shahar-and-shalim|birth of divine figures]] includes a damaged line involving milk or a dairy product. H. L. Ginsberg restored part of the line and read it as a command to prepare a young goat in milk. Later photographs, examined by Robert Ratner and Bruce Zuckerman, led to a reassessment of the signs used to support that reading. The reading remains insecure and the line gives no clear reference to the animal’s own mother. Neither this text nor other known Ugaritic evidence establishes the practice often cited as the biblical law’s target. [@ktu3, 1.23] [@cos1]

Images of a mother animal suckling her young were widely available in ancient Near Eastern art. Othmar Keel emphasizes this imagery when interpreting the bond between the two animals in the prohibition. Such images give a setting for considering the mother’s milk as a sign of nurture. They do not identify a practice of boiling offspring in it. [@keel-symbolism] [@anep]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 23:19 and 34:26 give the rule immediately after the requirement to bring the first of the harvest to God’s house. Deut 14:21 places it after an instruction about an animal found dead. The settings suggest questions about ritual and food preparation, but no passage states the reason for the prohibition. The phrasing specifies the relationship between the young animal and the milk. [@abd]

Lev 22:28 forbids slaughtering a cow or sheep and its young on the same day. Deut 22:6–7 requires releasing the mother bird when taking young or eggs from a nest. These laws also give special significance to the relation of parent and offspring. They provide comparisons for an ethical reading even though they address different situations. [@abd]

## Interpretive Significance

Philo stresses the impropriety of preparing a young animal in the milk intended to feed it. Jacob Milgrom develops an interpretation in which life-giving milk is kept apart from the death of the offspring. Maimonides had connected the law to a possible idolatrous custom, an explanation for which no secure ancient evidence has been identified. The biblical contexts leave room for more than one kind of explanation. Later rabbinic law generalized the prohibition into rules for separating meat and dairy, a development that extends beyond the wording of any single biblical occurrence. [@abd] [@keel-symbolism]
