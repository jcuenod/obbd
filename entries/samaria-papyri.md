## Overview

The Samaria papyri are Aramaic legal documents from fourth-century BCE Samaria, recovered with clay sealings and human remains from a cave in Wadi ed-Daliyeh. Most identifiable records concern the sale of enslaved people. Several preserve dates and official names, supplying evidence for provincial government and legal practice near the end of Persian rule. The place of discovery has been interpreted as a refuge during the Macedonian conquest. The documents also figure in efforts to reconstruct the family of Sanballat, the governor named in the Elephantine correspondence.

## Manuscripts and Date

The finds became known in 1962, and archaeological work followed at the cave. The papyri preserve fourth-century Aramaic writing, often in fragments. Their dating formulas and letter forms help establish the sequence of the manuscripts. The latest deed with a preserved date is WDSP 1, from 335 BCE. Some fragments could be slightly later. The dates are dates of transactions in Samaria. They give a lower limit for the deposition of the documents at Wadi ed-Daliyeh. [@neaehl] [@abd]

## Contents

The surviving agreements identify parties, witnesses and occasions for transfer. Slave-sale deeds record property claims over persons in the legal language of the period. Their formats can include an inner text protected by a seal and an accessible outer text. The [[seals-and-bullae|clay sealings]] give evidence for authentication and preserve imagery and, in some cases, lettering. One reads the title governor of Samaria in association with a name or patronymic partly restored as Sanballat. The onomastics include a substantial number of Yahwistic names alongside other names used under Persian rule. [@avigad-sass-wsss] [@abd]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Neh 2:10, 19 introduces [[sanballat|Sanballat]] and Neh 13:28 connects his family to Jerusalem's high priestly family by marriage. The papyri and sealings give later evidence for the prominence of family names associated with the governorship, though their precise relationship to the first securely attested governor is open. The Yahwistic names also complicate any attempt to treat the polemic of 2 Kgs 17:24–41 as a complete account of later religion in Samaria. Names indicate participation in a naming tradition and do not establish the identity or worship of each bearer. [@tad, A4.7] [@abd]

Lev 25:44–46 permits ownership and inheritance of foreign slaves. The deeds give a comparison for the legal treatment of enslaved people under Persian rule. Jer 32:9–14 describes the witnessing and preservation of a property deed in sealed and open forms. The papyri illustrate how [[sealed-deeds|sealed records]] could protect and authenticate a transaction. [@abd] [@tad]

## Scholarly Issues

Frank Moore Cross reconstructed three governors named Sanballat by proposing that the name recurred across generations. His third Sanballat is the founder of the Gerizim sanctuary in Josephus (*Antiquities* 11.302–347), placed near the time of Alexander. Jan Dušek has challenged the restorations and identifications underpinning the sequence and finds no need for a third governor of that name. The cave assemblage suggests flight from Samaria. A connection with Alexander's reprisals reported by Curtius Rufus (*History of Alexander* 4.8.9–11) remains an inference. [@avigad-sass-wsss] [@abd]
