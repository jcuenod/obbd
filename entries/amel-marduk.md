## Overview

Amel-Marduk was a king of Babylon who succeeded his father Nebuchadnezzar II in 562 BCE and was killed in 560 BCE. His Akkadian name means “man of Marduk,” referring to the main god of Babylon. The books of Kings and Jeremiah call him Evil-merodach and remember him for releasing the Judahite king Jehoiachin from prison. That act brought a captive from Judah into a privileged position at the Babylonian court and supplies the closing episode of both biblical books.

## Extrabiblical Sources

A Babylonian king list from Uruk assigns Amel-Marduk two years and places Neriglissar after him. [@anet, 566–567] A small body of royal inscriptions, including texts on bricks, gives some evidence of building activity. Business tablets dated by the king's reign supply a firmer basis for chronology than the surviving narrative accounts. In Babylonian official reckoning the partial year before the first New Year could be called the accession year, so the first numbered year can be distinguished from the calendar year when the king took power.

The Babylonian historian Berossus, writing in Greek centuries after the events, is quoted in Josephus's *Against Apion*. In that account Amel-Marduk disregarded the laws and was killed in a plot led by his brother-in-law Neriglissar, who then took the throne. The succession is independently documented, while the portrayal of Amel-Marduk's rule comes from sources that give little opportunity to assess his policies.

## Biblical Portrayal

In 2 Kgs 25:27–30 and Jer 52:31–34, Amel-Marduk lifts [[jehoiachin|Jehoiachin]] out of confinement in the thirty-seventh year of his exile. The two accounts give different days of the same month. Both depict a change of clothes, a place at the king's table, precedence over other kings at Babylon and a regular allowance. The change restores Jehoiachin's personal standing at court, while the books leave Judah under Babylonian rule.

The Hebrew form Evil-merodach has sometimes been explained as a play on the similar Hebrew word *ʾĕwîl* (“fool”). That is a possible explanation for the form of the name, and the text itself gives no reason for the spelling. <!-- VERIFY: compare specialist treatments of the Hebrew name before attributing a deliberate pun -->

## Historical Assessment

Babylonian administrative texts from the reign of [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] list oil allowances for Jehoiachin, called king of Judah, and for his sons. These [[jehoiachin-ration-tablets|ration tablets]] independently confirm his presence and recognition at the court before the release described in Kings and Jeremiah. They give no information about the terms of his confinement or Amel-Marduk's reasons for changing his status. [@anet, 308]

Jer 39:3, 13 mentions a Babylonian official called Nergal-sharezer at Jerusalem. A corresponding Babylonian name was borne by Neriglissar, who held office under Nebuchadnezzar and later became king. The identification is plausible, although a shared name is insufficient by itself to establish that the passages refer to that particular official. The evidence for the succession in [[babylonia|Babylonia]] is much firmer than the proposed identification in Jeremiah.
