## Overview

Egyptian mummification preserved the body through drying and, in many periods, the use of wrappings and other treatments. Its aim was connected with Egyptian beliefs about the continuing life of the deceased. The procedures varied with time and resources. Genesis describes both Jacob and Joseph as embalmed in Egypt, and it gives separate figures for the treatment of Jacob's body and the period of mourning. The episodes give the deaths an Egyptian setting while maintaining the family's connection to burial in its ancestral land.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian mummies and equipment show that embalmers sometimes removed internal organs, dried the body with natron, treated it with oils or resins, and wrapped it in linen. Practices varied among places and periods, including in the treatment and storage of organs. Chemical analysis of labeled vessels from a Late Period embalming workshop at Saqqara shows that specialists used mixtures of substances for different treatments. The Egyptian evidence includes both bodily preparation and spoken or performed rites.

Herodotus describes embalming as a professional trade and distinguishes methods according to cost (Histories 2.86–89). In his account the most elaborate treatment includes seventy days in natron. Diodorus Siculus also describes the work of specialists (Library of History 1.91). Their accounts come from comparatively late periods in Egyptian history. The reported durations are best compared with Egyptian evidence for an ideal interval of roughly seventy days between death and burial.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Joseph directs his servants, called physicians, to embalm Jacob (Gen 50:2–3). The text gives forty days as the time taken for embalming and seventy days as the period of Egyptian mourning. These figures distinguish two concerns of the story, treatment of the body and communal mourning. The two statements need not describe consecutive phases. Their exact relation to the timings given by other sources is uncertain.

Gen 50:26 also says that Joseph was embalmed and placed in a coffin in Egypt. Exod 13:19 says that Moses takes Joseph's bones when Israel leaves Egypt, recording the dead man's wish that his remains accompany his people. The treatment Joseph receives in Egypt and the account of his bones leaving it serve different narrative purposes. The latter makes the return to the homeland important to his burial.

## Interpretive Significance

The explicit embalming of Jacob and Joseph is unusual in the Bible and anchors both deaths in an Egyptian social setting. The common burial practices documented in Judah include laying a body in a tomb and, in some periods and tomb types, collecting bones after decomposition. See [[bench-tombs|Bench Tombs]]. Asa's body is placed on a bier with prepared spices in 2 Chr 16:14. John 19:39–40 describes spices and linen cloths used in the burial of Jesus, which the narrator identifies as a Jewish custom. Such uses of aromatic substances or wrappings have their own cultural contexts.

The treatment of human remains could express respect for the deceased and beliefs about their future, while using different materials and ceremonies. Genesis contains no technical account of the procedure used for either patriarch. The description of physicians, the time allowed, and the coffin suffice to evoke an Egyptian setting.
