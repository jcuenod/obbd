## Overview

Teraphim are objects mentioned in biblical accounts of households, shrines, and divination. Rachel conceals them during Jacob’s departure, Michal uses one in a deception involving David, and other passages associate them with oracles or condemn them. Their form and purpose are not described consistently. Ancient Near Eastern records about household gods and family ritual provide context for some uses. The objects may have included images of ancestors or protective powers, but no excavated type can be securely identified as the one intended in every biblical passage.

## Archaeological Evidence

Small clay, stone, and metal figures survive from many domestic and religious settings. Their form can suggest uses, but appearance alone rarely establishes what their users called them. No securely labeled figurine identifies itself as a teraphim object. Differences among archaeological images therefore cannot be mapped directly onto the varied biblical contexts.

Texts from [[nuzi-texts|Nuzi]] and Emar describe household gods and obligations connected with families and estates. A comparison with Nuzi once encouraged the claim that possession of household images carried a legal title to inheritance. Closer examination showed that possession alone did not establish such a rule. The documents remain relevant to the practice of keeping family cult objects. They cannot establish why Rachel acted as she did.

## In the Bible

Gen 31:19 and 30–35 describes Rachel taking and hiding her father’s teraphim. They are small enough to be concealed in the story’s setting. First Samuel 19:13–16 has Michal arrange an object in David’s bed so that it can pass for him at a glance, with another object used near its head. The account does not allow a precise measurement. Together the stories make a single standard size difficult to infer.

Judg 17:5 and 18:14–20 associate teraphim with a shrine and an ephod. Hos 3:4 places them alongside institutions and objects whose absence marks a period of deprivation. Ezek 21:21 (MT 21:26) associates them with divination in a portrayal of the Babylonian king, and Zech 10:2 criticizes misleading answers. These examples suggest that images could be consulted or associated with the seeking of divine knowledge, although the procedure is seldom described.

First Samuel 15:23 uses teraphim in a negative comparison, and 2 Kgs 23:24 includes them among practices or objects suppressed by Josiah. Some narratives report their presence without the same direct evaluation. The differences reflect the aims of individual passages and do not establish a simple chronology from acceptance to rejection.

One influential interpretation connects teraphim with ancestor images used in [[divination|divination]] and family religion. Some contexts are compatible with that reading, while others give little evidence about whom the image represents. Proposed derivations from Hittite and Hebrew terms remain uncertain. The varied biblical uses make it prudent to describe a range of objects or roles before attempting a precise identification.
