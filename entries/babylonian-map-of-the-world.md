## Overview

The Babylonian Map of the World is a clay tablet from first-millennium BCE Mesopotamia. Its drawing shows Babylonia and other named places inside a ring of water. Beyond the ring lie distant regions that the accompanying text associates with remarkable geography and figures from legend. The drawing combines places known to Babylonian scribes with ideas about the world's limits. It offers a comparison for biblical descriptions of an inhabited world bounded by water and for prophetic language that places Jerusalem at the center of the nations.

## Description and Provenance

The tablet is held in the British Museum as BM 92687. It is generally associated with Sippar, now Abu Habba in Iraq, although its original find context is uncertain. The surviving copy is commonly dated around the sixth century BCE. A statement in the tablet indicates that the scribe worked from an earlier exemplar, a tablet that served as the source for a copy. The date and original form of that source remain uncertain [@horowitz-cosmic-geography, 20–42].

## Contents

A pair of concentric lines forms a ring around the interior of the diagram. The label *marratu* (“bitter river”) identifies the waters in that ring. Within it, some marks represent cities or regions and others indicate physical features such as a river and a marsh. The Euphrates is shown crossing the inhabited area, and [[babylon|Babylon]] is prominently labeled where the river passes through it.

Triangular forms project beyond the water. The Akkadian term used for these outer places is *nagû* (“region”). The damaged text describes distant regions in terms of such features as great distances, darkness, and extraordinary phenomena. Text on the tablet also mentions animals and legendary figures. A drawing of the bounded world and accounts of places beyond it suggest that the scribe's interest was in the shape and limits of the world as Babylonian scholars pictured it [@horowitz-cosmic-geography, 20–42].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 40:22 pictures God above the “circle of the earth” (ESV) and likens the sky to a shelter for those below. The expression can evoke the disk or visible horizon of the world. A circular boundary appears in the language of Job 26:10 and Prov 8:27, where it is drawn on the waters or the deep. Ps 24:2 portrays the earth as founded on seas and rivers. These passages supply points of comparison for ancient pictures of an ordered world set in relation to water, a subject discussed under [[waters-above-and-below|waters above and below]].

The map gives Babylon a prominent place. Jerusalem occupies a comparable position in the language of Ezek 5:5, where God sets the city among surrounding nations. Ezek 38:12 uses the image of a navel to place a people gathered from other nations at the world's center. The Bible thus uses the center as an image of a community's place in the world, while the tablet displays a view centered on the Babylonian region.

## Scholarly Issues

The drawing includes actual places, but it compresses distances and sets them within a pattern that reaches into legendary geography. Its distinctions between the familiar world and distant regions matter more than geographic scale. The function of the accompanying text and the reading of several damaged descriptions remain debated. Biblical poetry and this tablet can be compared as evidence for [[cosmology|ancient conceptions of the world]], though each expresses those conceptions in a different form and setting.
