## Overview

The Ekron Royal Dedicatory Inscription is a limestone block found in 1996 at Tel Miqne, in the sanctuary of a seventh-century BCE temple. It records that Achish son of Padi, ruler of Ekron, built the temple for a goddess. The inscription confirmed the identification of Tel Miqne with biblical Ekron, one of the Philistine cities. Its royal names can be matched with Assyrian records, and its brief prayer can be compared with a biblical blessing. The goddess's name and its origin remain disputed.

## Description and Provenance

Excavators found the block in the sanctuary of Temple Complex 650 at [[ekron|Tel Miqne-Ekron]]. Its archaeological setting places it in the seventh century BCE, when Ekron was a major center of olive oil production within the Assyrian imperial system. The inscribed stone has five lines and records a dedication to a deity. The position of the find in a temple gives the dedication an archaeological context independent of the biblical traditions about the city's worship [@cos2] [@neaehl].

## Contents

Achish gives his ancestry through Padi and three further generations and identifies himself as the ruler of Ekron. He states that he built the temple for his lady, whose name is commonly transcribed *Ptgyh*. The inscription asks the goddess to bless and protect him and grant long life or lasting prosperity to his land. Its language is Northwest Semitic and has features close to Phoenician. The royal title *śr* (“ruler”) resembles Hebrew *śar*, a word for a leader or official.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 13:3 names Ekron among the five principal [[philistines|Philistine]] cities. The inscription gives the city's own name at Tel Miqne. Sennacherib's account of his [[sennacherib-campaign-701|campaign in 701 BCE]] reports that Padi, king of Ekron, was delivered to Hezekiah in Jerusalem and was later restored. 2 Kgs 18:8 places Hezekiah in conflict with Philistine towns but gives no account of Padi. Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal list a ruler of Ekron named Ikausu, generally identified with the inscription's Achish. These correspondences connect the temple inscription with the political history of the region [@cos2] [@rinap3-1] [@rinap4].

Achish is also the name of a king of [[gath|Gath]] in 1 Sam 21:10–15 (MT 21:11–16) and 1 Kgs 2:39–40. The comparison concerns the use of a royal name in Philistia over time. The biblical king belongs to a different city and narrative setting. The title in the inscription can also be compared with the term for Philistine leaders in 1 Sam 29:3–4.

Num 6:24 asks YHWH to bless and keep the recipient. The petition on the block combines corresponding verbs directed to the goddess, attesting a similar blessing formula. In 2 Kgs 1:2–3, the Israelite king seeks an answer from [[baal-zebub|Baal-zebub]], the god associated with Ekron. The dedicatory inscription adds evidence of the city's worship of a goddess. Zeph 2:4 addresses Ekron among other Philistine towns, and the archaeology helps establish the prominence the city could have in the period associated with that oracle.

## Scholarly Issues

The name Achish is often explained as having a non-Semitic origin, with Aegean and Anatolian comparisons proposed. The goddess's name is harder to explain. One proposed reading changes the third consonant and connects the word with Greek *Potnia* (“mistress”). Another retains the consonant as commonly read and proposes a form related to Pytho and Gaia. Both depend on uncertain linguistic comparisons. The dedication gives strong evidence for a royal cult at Ekron and leaves the goddess's wider identity unresolved.
