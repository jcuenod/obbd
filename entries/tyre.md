## Overview

Tyre was a Phoenician city on an island off the coast of what is now Lebanon. Its harbors and trade connected the Levant with Egypt, Cyprus, and communities throughout the Mediterranean. Its rulers appear in biblical accounts involving David and Solomon, and its shipping and wealth shape the prophets' portrayals of the city. The Iron Age remains are difficult to reach beneath later settlement. Archaeology, Assyrian records, and accounts of Tyrian political history supply different kinds of evidence for the city's importance.

## Location and Identification

Ancient Tyre included the offshore urban center and installations and settlements on the mainland. Alexander's troops built a causeway during the siege of 332 BCE. Sediment eventually accumulated around the causeway, helping create the connection to the mainland visible today. Later occupation has obscured much of the Iron Age city. Patricia Bikai's limited sounding and the excavations of the al-Bass cemetery on the mainland provide important evidence for its earlier material culture.

## Archaeological History

The Egyptian and Near Eastern sources place Tyre within a network of coastal cities that became centers of [[phoenician-seafaring|Phoenician trade]]. Wood, dyed textiles, crafts, and imported goods formed part of its economy. Phoenician communities farther west maintained links with the homeland, and the cult of [[melqart|Melqart]], a god of Tyre, traveled with them. The cemetery at al-Bass provides human and material evidence for a city whose public buildings remain largely inaccessible at Iron Age levels.

Assyrian inscriptions record tribute from Tyre, and a treaty of Esarhaddon with Baal of Tyre regulates aspects of his rule and maritime activity. [[josephus|Josephus]] preserves royal traditions attributed to Menander and reports a thirteen-year siege under [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] (Ag. Ap. 1.116–127, 156). The city survived as a major port and later issued the silver coins known as [[tyrian-shekel|Tyrian shekels]], used in connection with the Jerusalem temple tax.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Sam 5:11 describes Hiram of Tyre sending timber and craftspeople to David. 1 Kgs 5:1–12 (MT 5:15–26) recounts [[hiram-of-tyre|Hiram's]] timber agreement with Solomon, and 1 Kgs 9:11–14 describes towns given to Hiram. The accounts associate Tyre's expertise and access to timber with the political relations of neighboring kingdoms.

Isa 23 imagines Tyre's downfall as a disaster for other maritime towns. Amos 1:9–10 connects the delivery of captives to Edom with a broken covenant of kinship, and Joel 3:4–8 (MT 4:4–8) accuses Tyre and Sidon of selling Judeans abroad. Ezek 26–28 combines a siege oracle, the lament for a ship laden with trade goods, and a lament over Tyre's king using Eden and the divine mountain. Ezek 29:17–20 acknowledges the effort of a Babylonian campaign against Tyre and its lack of reward. These passages use different settings and images to evaluate the city's strength and conduct.

Mark 7:24–31 places Jesus in the region of Tyre. In Acts 21:3–7 Paul's ship stops there to unload cargo. The latter scene illustrates the continuing use of the port in the Roman period.
