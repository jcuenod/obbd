## Overview

Ugarit was a prosperous city and small kingdom on the northern Syrian coast in the Late Bronze Age. Excavations at Ras Shamra uncovered palaces, houses, temples, and archives in several languages. A nearby harbor connected the city with other parts of the eastern Mediterranean. The kingdom was destroyed at the end of the Late Bronze Age, around the beginning of the twelfth century BCE. Its archives offer some of the most important evidence for the languages, literature, and religious traditions of the region before the biblical period.

## Location and Identification

Ras Shamra lies near the Mediterranean coast north of modern Latakia. The nearby bay and settlement at Minet el-Beida served as a harbor area. The discovery of a tomb at Minet el-Beida in 1928 led to formal excavations in 1929 under Claude Schaeffer. The archives provided the ancient name of Ugarit and evidence for its networks inland and overseas.

## Archaeological History

The royal palace at Ras Shamra contained courtyards, rooms for administration, and archives. The city also had large temples and private houses, some of which preserved their own collections of tablets. Houses associated with Rapanu and Urtenu preserve records of the work of officials, traders, and scribes. The palace at nearby Ras Ibn Hani also yielded tablets. The finds distinguish several settings where documents were made and kept.

The archives contain [[ugaritic-texts|Ugaritic texts]] in alphabetic cuneiform, a system in which wedge-shaped marks usually represent individual consonants. Akkadian appears prominently in international documents, and other languages include Hurrian and Hittite. This evidence shows that Ugarit's scribes worked across different traditions of writing. The literary and ritual texts also describe particular forms of worship at Ugarit. The kingdom's own records distinguish Ugarit from Canaan, and its evidence reflects a specific region and society.

Late letters report threats and attacks involving ships, and the final occupation ended in destruction around 1200–1185 BCE. The evidence belongs to the larger disruption at the end of the Late Bronze Age. The letters establish that some people faced danger from the sea, while the identity of those responsible for the city's final destruction is uncertain.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ps 29 describes a divine voice in the storm in language often compared with descriptions of [[baal|Baal]] at Ugarit. Isa 27:1 describes Leviathan with epithets comparable to the Ugaritic serpent in the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]]. Ps 82 imagines an assembly of divine beings. Deut 32:8–9 has variants in Qumran and Greek witnesses that refer to divine beings in the division of the nations. The imagery has comparanda in Ugaritic accounts of El and the divine family, although the texts organize divine authority in their own ways.

The righteous or wise figure named Danel in Ezek 14:14, 20 and 28:3 has been compared with the hero of the [[aqhat-epic|Aqhat Epic]]. The comparison remains disputed. Ugarit is unnamed in the Bible. Its importance lies in the ancient evidence it provides for language, literary motifs, and ritual comparisons.
