## Overview

Honey in the Bible can mean bee honey or a sweet syrup made from fruit. Both meanings fit the agricultural world of ancient Israel. At Tel Reḥov in the Jordan Valley, archaeologists found rows of clay hives dating to the late tenth or early ninth century BCE, direct evidence of large-scale beekeeping. Biblical stories also describe gathering wild honey. These uses help explain why honey could be a food, a trade good, or an image of abundance in the promise of a land flowing with milk and honey.

## Archaeological Evidence

At Tel Reḥov, Amihai Mazar and Nava Panitz-Cohen's excavation recovered about thirty unfired clay hives or their remains, arranged in rows. The excavators estimated that the original apiary contained many more. The structure is the earliest excavated installation of its kind known in the ancient Near East. It gives a scale for organized beekeeping that the earlier textual and visual evidence alone could not establish.

Bee fragments trapped in the installation made analysis possible. A research team found that their measurable traits most closely resemble those of honeybees now found in parts of Anatolia. The team proposed that beekeepers may have imported bees, while noting that ancient bee populations could have had a different natural distribution. Egyptian reliefs also depict beekeeping, and the [[hittite-laws|Hittite Laws]] prescribe penalties for the theft of hives or bees [@bloch-et-al-apiculture, 11240–11244] [@roth-law, 228–229, §§ 91–92].

## In the Bible

Hebrew *dĕbaš* can refer to bee honey or to fruit syrup. Judg 14:8–9 mentions bees in the lion's carcass, and 1 Sam 14:25–29 describes Jonathan eating honey in the forest. These narratives depict wild honey as readily recognizable food. Honey from rock in Deut 32:13 also suggests the sweetness available from a rocky landscape. Matt 3:4 explicitly identifies wild honey as part of John the Baptist's diet.

The agricultural list in Deut 8:8 and the [[firstfruits-and-firstborn|firstfruits]] in 2 Chr 31:5 make date or other fruit syrup plausible. The honey traded by Judah and Israel in Ezek 27:17 is another example where the precise product is uncertain. Prov 24:13 commends honey for its sweetness and goodness, and Ps 19:10 (MT 19:11) uses honey and the comb as a measure for the sweetness of God's ordinances. Lev 2:11 excludes honey from what is burned on the altar. The type of honey intended there remains discussed.

The “milk and honey” of Exod 3:8 gives the promised land a language of abundance. In the Ugaritic [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]], a vision of wadis running with honey signals renewed prosperity. The Egyptian *Story of Sinuhe* also includes abundant honey in its description of a fertile Levantine region. These are comparisons for the literary image. Neither comparison fixes one meaning of *dĕbaš* throughout the Bible [@parker-unp, 157–158] [@ael, 1:225–226].
