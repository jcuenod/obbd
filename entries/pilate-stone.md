## Overview

The Pilate Stone is a fragmentary Latin inscription found in 1961 in the theater at Caesarea Maritima, where its limestone block had been reused. It names Pontius Pilate, gives his title as prefect of Judea, and records work on a structure called a Tiberieum. The inscription supplies contemporary evidence for the office held by the governor in the Gospel trial narratives. Damage to the lettering and the block’s secondary setting leave open the original location and function of the building.

## Description and Provenance

An Italian archaeological expedition excavating the theater at [[caesarea-maritima|Caesarea Maritima]] found the block reused in a stairway. The carving had already been damaged. Its reuse accounts for the difference between the spot where archaeologists found it and the unknown site of the building to which the dedication belonged. The stone is kept at the Israel Museum. It is the securely identified contemporary inscription that names Pilate and gives his office.

## Contents

The surviving letters allow the core of the name and title to be read as [Pon]tius Pilatus and [praef]ectus Iudae[a]e. Brackets indicate missing letters supplied by editors. The word *Tiberieum* is preserved and associates the building with Tiberius. The preceding words and the verb in the last line have been restored in different ways. A dedication honoring the emperor is a plausible interpretation. Proposed functions include an [[imperial-cult|imperial cult building]] and a harbor-related structure, such as a lighthouse. The theater findspot does not favor either by itself.

The prefect title is the clearest administrative detail. Tacitus later refers to Pilate as a procurator (*Ann.* 15.44), a title better known from later governors of Judea. The stone provides a contemporary point of comparison for the accuracy of official language in sources of different dates. Pilate’s coinage and the accounts of Philo and Josephus provide other evidence for his administration and its conflicts.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Luke 3:1 places John’s activity in the time of Tiberius and names [[pontius-pilate|Pontius Pilate]] as governing Judea. Its broader Greek description of authority is consistent with the Latin title without reproducing it. Matt 27:2, 11, Mark 15:1–15, and John 18:28–19:16 give Pilate authority in the proceedings against Jesus. Acts 4:27 and 1 Tim 6:13 remember his involvement for different rhetorical purposes. The inscription confirms the title and jurisdiction of the official. It supplies no account of the proceedings or their location.

Acts 23:33–35 presents Caesarea as the place where another governor receives Paul and orders him held. That setting illustrates the city’s administrative importance under Rome. It helps explain why a monument commissioned by a prefect would appear at Caesarea even though the Gospel narratives place Pilate in Jerusalem for Jesus’ trial.

## Scholarly Issues

The structure to which the word *Tiberieum* refers has not been securely identified. The inscription’s name and title require fewer assumptions than the restoration of the whole dedication. A separate ring from Herodium, published in 2018, was initially read as naming Pilate in Greek. The reading has been challenged, and there is no secure link to the prefect’s personal seal or administration.
