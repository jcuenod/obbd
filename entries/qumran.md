## Overview

Khirbet Qumran is an archaeological site on a terrace above the northwestern Dead Sea. Nearby caves yielded most of the Dead Sea Scrolls. The settlement preserves buildings, a water system with stepped pools, pottery and a large cemetery. Many scholars connect its inhabitants with the Essenes described by ancient writers and with the community portrayed in some scrolls. The identification and the exact relationship between the settlement and the manuscripts remain debated. The site is important for interpreting Jewish communal life and scriptural interpretation in the late Second Temple period.

## Location and Identification

The ruins stand between the Dead Sea shore and the cliffs of the Judean wilderness. Several manuscript caves are close to the settlement, and pottery and other material links support a relationship between some caves and the site. Cave 4, where many fragments were recovered, is especially close. The contents and archaeological contexts of the caves differ. Proximity alone cannot establish who collected or deposited every manuscript. [@neaehl] [@abd]

## Archaeological History

Roland de Vaux led excavations in the 1950s. He identified Iron Age remains beneath the later settlement and proposed a sequence of occupations for the Hellenistic and Roman periods. Jodi Magness revised the sequence and placed the beginning of the principal community around the late second or early first century BCE. The principal occupation ended during the revolt against Rome, conventionally around 68 CE. Specific dates and the purpose of the earliest phases remain matters of interpretation. [@neaehl]

The site includes a tower, rooms used for assembly or work, and an extensive system for collecting and storing water. Many scholars identify several stepped pools as [[stone-vessels-and-mikvaot|ritual baths]], though others have stressed water storage or industrial uses for parts of the system. The large cemetery has roughly a thousand graves. Some excavated groups are predominantly male, but only a limited sample has been studied and age, sex and dates are not well established for the whole cemetery. [@neaehl] [@abd]

The prevailing interpretation associates the site with Essenes or an Essene-related group. Ancient descriptions by Pliny, Philo and [[josephus|Josephus]] contribute to this reading. Archaeologists have also proposed a fort, a villa or a pottery production center, sometimes for different phases. Another hypothesis treats the manuscripts as collections brought from Jerusalem or elsewhere. The evidence can support a connection between the site and the caves without requiring a single origin for the entire collection. [@neaehl] [@abd]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The [[community-rule|Community Rule]] interprets Isa 40:3 in connection with preparing a way in the wilderness. It helps explain why withdrawal to an isolated place might have acquired scriptural significance. Lev 15:13–18 gives examples of washing in connection with purity, a disputed comparison for particular pools at the site. The possible identifications of Qumran with one of the wilderness towns in Josh 15:61–62 or with the towers of 2 Chr 26:10 remain uncertain. The wilderness setting of John’s baptism in Matt 3:1–6 is comparable in broad terms. Claims that John or Jesus belonged to the community lack direct evidence. [@neaehl] [@abd]
