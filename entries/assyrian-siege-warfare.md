## Overview

The Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian empires besieged fortified cities in the Levant during the first millennium BCE. Their attacks were recorded in royal inscriptions, palace reliefs, and biblical accounts. At Lachish, stone remains from an Assyrian siege ramp can be compared with images of the assault carved for Sennacherib at Nineveh. Biblical texts also describe the defense of Jerusalem and its later fall to Babylon. Together these sources help distinguish the equipment, negotiations, blockade, and assault evoked in biblical narratives and prophetic images of siege.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Excavations led by David Ussishkin at [[lachish|Lachish]] exposed remnants of a stone siege ramp outside the wall of the city destroyed in 701 BCE. The [[lachish-reliefs|palace reliefs at Nineveh]] identify Lachish in an inscription and show wheeled engines with battering rams, devices that struck a wall or gate. The archaeological ramp and the carved attack scenes are unusually direct evidence for the same siege. Details of the ramp's original shape and the precise appearance of the attack remain subject to reconstruction [@neaehl, 3:897–911] [@anep, figs. 371–373].

Assyrian royal texts use several terms for operations against fortified cities. They mention ramps, rams, and breaching or sapping, an attack that weakens a fortification by removing part of its base. A blockade surrounds a city to restrict movement and supplies, and it could be reported even when a city was never stormed. In the [[sennacherib-annals|annals of Sennacherib]], the king lists captured towns of Judah and describes confining Hezekiah in Jerusalem like a caged bird. He records receiving tribute from Hezekiah without claiming to have taken Jerusalem [@rinap3-1, no. 22 ii 37–iii 49].

## In the Hebrew Bible

2 Kgs 18:13–35 combines a notice about captured towns and tribute with a scene of negotiation outside Jerusalem. The Assyrian officer speaks in Judean within hearing of people on the wall and urges them to surrender. The narrative shows the pressure exerted on defenders through promises and threats. In Isa 37:33 the promise that the Assyrians will neither raise a shield nor cast up a mound represents a reprieve from an assault by familiar means.

The defenses were also part of siege warfare. In 2 Chr 32:2–5 Hezekiah blocks springs outside Jerusalem and strengthens its walls. Isa 22:8–11 mentions gathering water, repairing breaches, and creating a reservoir. For the later Babylonian siege, 2 Kgs 25:1–4 describes siegeworks around Jerusalem, severe famine, and an eventual breach. These accounts concern different campaigns and ways of describing a city's exposure to attack.

## Interpretive Significance

Ezekiel portrays siege with a model of Jerusalem on a clay tile surrounded by camps and implements of attack (Ezek 4:1–3). The prophetic action uses equipment familiar to its audience to convey the threat to the city. In a different genre, Deut 20:19–20 limits which trees can be cut for a siege, protecting those that produce food. Assyrian sources show that armies sometimes destroyed trees, but interpreters differ on whether the law responds to specifically Assyrian practices or addresses a broader problem of ancient siege warfare.
