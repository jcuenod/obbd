## Overview

Rezin was the last king of Aram-Damascus. Assyrian inscriptions name him among the rulers who paid tribute to Tiglath-pileser III in 738 BCE and describe the later campaign against him. Kings and Isaiah associate him with Pekah of Israel in the war against Judah in the 730s BCE. The biblical accounts focus on the threat to Jerusalem and Ahaz's response, while the Assyrian records situate the defeat of Damascus within a wider program of conquest and annexation in the Levant.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[tiglath-pileser-iii-inscriptions|royal inscriptions]] call him Raḫiānu. The palace annals and the Iran Stele include him in lists of western rulers whose payments reached the Assyrian king. [[menahem|Menahem of Samaria]] is among those listed alongside him. The payments are conventionally assigned to 738 BCE, several years before Rezin and Pekah threatened Judah. They show that Rezin's confrontation with Assyria followed a period of submission. [@rinap1, nos. 14, 32, 35]

Fragments of the annals describe the Assyrian campaign against Damascus. Rezin takes refuge in his city, the Assyrians devastate his territory, and the king claims to have captured Rezin's ancestral settlement and deported its people. The surviving campaign notices are incomplete. Read alongside summaries and the Assyrian eponym records, they place the conquest of the kingdom in the campaigns of 733–732 BCE. [[damascus|Damascus]] subsequently became an Assyrian provincial center. [@rinap1, nos. 20–23] [@glassner-chronicles, no. 9, entries for 733–732 BCE]

## Biblical Portrayal

2 Kgs 15:37 presents Rezin and [[pekah|Pekah]] as threats to Judah late in Jotham's reign. In 2 Kgs 16:5–9 they besiege [[ahaz|Ahaz]] in Jerusalem and fail to take the city. Ahaz sends treasure and appeals to Tiglath-pileser as a subordinate. Kings then reports that Tiglath-pileser captured Damascus, deported its inhabitants to Kir and killed Rezin. The account's focus is Ahaz's decision and its consequences. [@abd, 6:283–285]

Isa 7:1–9 identifies Damascus as the capital of Aram and Rezin as its ruler, while portraying the two hostile kings as a danger that will pass. It gives the coalition a specific aim, the installation of a ruler in Judah identified as the son of Tabeel. The Hebrew wording of Isa 8:6 has been understood as saying that some people rejoice over Rezin and Pekah, but the expression and its force in context are disputed. Isa 17:1–3 pairs the anticipated end of Damascus's kingdom with the downfall of Ephraim's stronghold. [@abd, 6:283–285]

## Historical Assessment

The Assyrian and biblical sources agree on the principal rulers and the defeat of Damascus. Reconstructing the coalition's wider strategy depends on inference. Resistance to Assyria gives a plausible reason for Damascus and Israel to seek control over Judah. The encounter at Elath in 2 Kgs 16:6 is harder to reconstruct. The written Hebrew says that Rezin recovered Elath for Aram and that Arameans came there. The traditional reading changes the latter group to Edomites. Some reconstructions make a broader change to connect the episode with Edom. The variants leave the political result uncertain. [@rinap1, nos. 20–21] [@abd, 6:283–285]

The difference between Assyrian Raḫiānu and Hebrew *rĕṣîn* has also prompted proposals to reconstruct an Aramaic form such as *Raḍyān*. The reconstruction depends on how Assyrian writing represents the sounds of the local name. [@rinap1, nos. 20–21]
