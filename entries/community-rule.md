## Overview

The Community Rule is a set of instructions for a Jewish association in the Second Temple period. Its longest surviving copy is a Hebrew scroll found in Cave 1 near Qumran, above the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea. Other copies preserve substantially different forms. The rule describes how members entered the covenant, ordered their common life, and understood the conflict between truth and injustice. It is a particularly detailed witness to one form of ancient Jewish communal life. Its rites and scriptural interpretations supply comparisons for the Gospels and for descriptions of the earliest Christian communities.

## Description and Provenance

The main copy is known as 1QS, the manuscript designation identifying the rule from Cave 1. The scroll belongs to the group of [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] first discovered near [[qumran|Qumran]] in 1947. Its handwriting is usually dated around the beginning of the first century BCE, while the work underwent development before and after that copy was made. Fragments of other copies from Cave 4 attest substantial variation, including shorter versions of some sections.

The Cave 1 manuscript also preserves two shorter related works, the Rule of the Congregation and the Blessings. In the Rule of the Congregation, families have a more visible place than in the Community Rule. Another work from the Qumran library, the Damascus Document, gives instructions for communities with households and children. Together these works show that ancient Jewish associations could organize daily life in more than one way.

## Contents

The group calls itself the *yaḥad* (“community”). Membership involved a staged admission process, with assessments by existing members and rules about when an applicant's goods entered the common pool. Members participated in communal meals and were expected to follow rules of rank and conduct. Penalties were imposed for offenses such as inappropriate speech or disorder at meetings.

The opening ceremony describes members entering or renewing their covenant with blessings and curses. The section often called the Treatise on the Two Spirits explains how truth and injustice operate in human life. The rule also speaks of expected figures, including a prophet and the anointed ones of Aaron and Israel. These figures belong to a wider range of Jewish [[messianic-expectations|expectations for the future]].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The Cave 1 rule applies Isa 40:3 to preparing God's way in the wilderness through study and practice of the law. Mark 1:2–4 connects the same verse with John the Baptist's appearance in the wilderness. These are two ancient readings of a shared passage. A direct relationship between John the Baptist and the Qumran community remains speculative.

The pooling of goods offers a comparison with the sharing described in Acts 2:44–45 and 4:32–35, where people sell property and distribute the proceeds according to need. The rule's provision for correction within the group has a comparable setting in Jewish interpretation of the duty to reprove a neighbor (Lev 19:17–18). Matt 18:15–17 describes a sequence of private correction, witnesses, and appeal to the assembly. These practices illuminate how different ancient Jewish and Christian groups understood communal responsibility.

## Scholarly Issues

The shorter versions in Cave 4, especially the manuscripts designated 4QSᵇ and 4QSᵈ, preserve passages different from the longer Cave 1 text. Sarianna Metso has argued for multiple lines of textual development. Philip Alexander and Geza Vermes also distinguish forms of the text, although scholars differ over the priority of particular readings and how to reconstruct an earlier rule. The date of a manuscript by itself cannot establish the age of every reading it preserves.

Accounts of the Essenes by Josephus and Philo describe common property, strict discipline, and practices of admission comparable to those in the rule. These agreements underpin the widely held association of Qumran with the [[jewish-sects|Essenes]]. The timing and details of admission differ, and ancient descriptions of Essene marriage practices are more varied than the Cave 1 rule's silence on the subject. The comparisons leave open how broadly the rule was used.
