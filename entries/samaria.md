## Overview

Samaria was the capital of the northern kingdom of Israel. It occupied the hill beside modern Sebastia in the central highlands, northwest of Nablus. Its royal acropolis, inscribed potsherds and carved ivory furniture fittings preserve evidence for the administration and wealth of the city described in Kings and Amos. The city became a provincial center under Assyria in the late eighth century BCE. Centuries later Herod the Great refounded it as Sebaste and dedicated a temple there to Augustus.

## Location and Identification

The capital stood on a defensible hill commanding agricultural land and routes through the highlands. 1 Kgs 16:24 attributes its founding to [[omri|Omri]] and explains its name through his purchase of the hill from Shemer. Excavators have found activity predating the royal complex. The name Samaria later designated a wider territory as well as the city, so references to Samaria may concern the capital, its district or a province. [@neaehl] [@abd]

## Archaeological History

The Harvard expedition under George Reisner, with Clarence Fisher and David Lyon, excavated from 1908 to 1910. A joint project led by John Crowfoot, with Kathleen Kenyon and Eleazar Sukenik among its principal staff, excavated in the 1930s. The teams exposed remains of an upper royal complex including fine ashlar masonry and a casemate enclosure. Some construction features have parallels on the Phoenician coast. The relative dates of the walls and palace installations are debated. Reassessment of Kenyon's building periods and pottery groups also affects attempts to attribute individual phases directly to Omri or Ahab. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

The [[samaria-ostraca|Samaria ostraca]] record dated shipments of oil and wine and contain both Yahwistic and Baal names. The [[samaria-ivories|carved ivories]] attest elite display and connections with workshops across the Levant, though many were found in later deposits. Following Assyrian conquest the city remained an administrative center. Later remains belong to the Hellenistic and Roman periods. [[herod-the-great|Herod]] gave it the name Sebaste, the Greek equivalent of the honorific Augustus, and erected a temple on the acropolis. [@cos4, 4.18] [@neaehl]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

1 Kgs 16:32 credits [[ahab|Ahab]] with building a temple to Baal. The location of this temple is uncertain. The ivory house of 1 Kgs 22:39 fits a setting in which inlays embellished wooden furnishings. The same material makes sense of Amos's references to ivory houses and beds in Amos 3:15 and 6:4. The elite criticized in Amos 4:1 are imagined on the hill of Samaria, while Mic 1:6 uses the prospect of exposed foundations and fallen stones to announce judgment on a built city. [@abd] [@neaehl]

2 Kgs 17:3–6 names Shalmaneser and reports a three-year siege and the city's capture, and 2 Kgs 17:24 reports resettlement in the region. Inscriptions of [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] claim the conquest and describe resettlement. The different accounts leave questions about who completed the [[fall-of-samaria|conquest]] and how many military operations took place. [@abc] [@cos2, 2.118]
