## Overview

The Wisdom of Solomon is a Greek Jewish composition on righteousness, death and immortality, personified Wisdom, and the worship of created things. It probably comes from the late Hellenistic or early Roman period and is often associated with Alexandria. Its language about Wisdom and its interpretation of Israel's story give context for several New Testament images and arguments. The book forms part of the Old Testament in Catholic and Orthodox traditions, while its placement and status differ across biblical canons.

## Manuscripts and Date

The book was composed in Greek and is preserved in the Greek biblical tradition, alongside other works conventionally grouped under the name [[septuagint]]. The speaker adopts a royal persona associated with Solomon, most clearly in the prayer of chapter 9. The language and setting of the work point instead to a later Jewish writer. The place and precise date of composition remain uncertain [@abd].

## Contents

Wisdom 1–5 contrasts the conduct and fate of the righteous with those of the wicked. Chapters 6–9 praise Wisdom as God's agent in the world and present the ruler's prayer for her. Wis 7:25–26 uses images of emanation, radiance, a mirror, and an image to describe her relation to God. Chapters 10–19 reinterpret episodes of Israel's story, especially the exodus, and develop a sustained criticism of idolatry [@nabre-wisdom, Wis 2; 7; 13–19].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Heb 1:1–4 describes the Son with language of radiance and representation that resembles Wis 7:25–26. Col 1:15–17 similarly speaks of divine image and cosmic agency. Those affinities show that the New Testament language has antecedents in Greek Jewish writing about Wisdom. The claims made about the Son and Christ take their force from the arguments of Hebrews and Colossians.

In Wis 2:12–20 opponents plan to test a righteous man who claims a special relationship to God. Matt 27:41–43 likewise presents mockers challenging God to rescue one who claims to be God's Son. Rom 1:18–32 and Wis 13–15 associate the failure to recognize the creator with idolatry and disorder. Acts 17:24–29 likewise reasons from the creator's relation to the world against images made by people. The extent to which any of these writers used the book directly remains debated.
