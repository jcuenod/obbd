## Overview

Textile production in ancient Israel and its neighbors involved preparing fibers, spinning them into thread, and weaving the thread into cloth. Wool, flax, and goat hair furnished materials for clothing, tents, household items, and sanctuary furnishings. Textile work took place in households and in settings that produced specialized goods. The tools survive more often than the fabric. Biblical texts describe spinning and weaving as skilled work and draw on their movements and equipment for images of speed, strength, and the shortness of life.

## Archaeological Evidence

Preparing fibers came before weaving. Wool and goat hair had to be sorted and prepared, while flax required processing to free its fibers. A spindle, often fitted with a whorl to keep it turning, drew out and twisted the fibers into yarn. A loom held the lengthwise warp threads under tension so that the crosswise weft could pass between them. Clay weights from Iron Age houses are often evidence of a vertical, warp-weighted loom. Egyptian images also show horizontal ground looms, among other types.

Spool-shaped loom weights in early Philistine contexts have parallels in the Aegean. They are one feature of a wider body of evidence for contact and technological exchange. Spindle whorls and weights can show where work took place, although different tools might leave similar material traces. Textile fragments survive where conditions are favorable. They occasionally preserve details beyond what the tools reveal, such as the fibers, twist, weave, and [[purple-dye|dyes]] used.

At [[kuntillet-ajrud|Kuntillet ʿAjrud]], some textiles contain both linen and wool. Finds from [[timna|Timna]] include wool fragments dyed with true purple made from marine mollusks. The dry setting at Timna preserved evidence of high-quality cloth in a community supported by copper production. These examples reveal regional access to skilled textile work, including in places where textile tools alone would leave an incomplete picture.

## In the Bible

Exod 35:25–26 describes skilled women spinning yarn, linen, and goat hair for the [[tabernacle|tabernacle]]. Exod 26:1 calls for curtains made from linen and dyed yarn and worked with cherubim. Prov 31:13, 19, 22, 24 pictures a woman handling wool and flax, spinning, and making garments for household use and sale. In 2 Kgs 23:7 women weave objects for [[asherah|Asherah]] at the Jerusalem temple. The Hebrew calls them “houses,” but the intended form, perhaps textile hangings or coverings, is debated.

In Judg 16:13–14 Delilah incorporates Samson's hair into the web and uses a pin associated with a loom. The details have been interpreted in relation to different loom designs. The spear shaft in 1 Sam 17:7 resembles a weaver's beam. Yigael Yadin proposed that the comparison refers to a rod with loops used to control threads, analogous to a thong on a spear. In Isa 38:12 the end of life is compared with cloth cut off the loom. Job 7:6 uses the movement of a weaver's implement to picture how quickly the days pass.

Deut 22:11 prohibits wearing a mixture of wool and linen, and Lev 19:19 also restricts mixed clothing. The fabrics at Kuntillet ʿAjrud show that the combination was technically possible and practiced. The tabernacle texts make extensive use of costly dyed fibers and linen. How the rules for ordinary clothing relate to such specialized contexts remains a question of interpretation.
