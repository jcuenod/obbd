## Overview

The Nazareth Inscription is a Greek text on marble that presents itself as an ordinance of Caesar protecting tombs against interference and the removal of bodies. The stone entered a nineteenth-century collection with a note that it had been sent from Nazareth. The note records its reported route of sale or shipment, and its original setting is uncertain. An analysis of the marble points to a quarry on the island of Kos. The inscription provides evidence for concerns about burial rights in the Greco-Roman world.

## Description and Provenance

Wilhelm Froehner acquired the slab in 1878, and his note associates it with Nazareth. Franz Cumont published the text in 1930. It is now held in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. Its excavation context is unknown, and the lettering and content give only a broad basis for dating it. The title names Caesar without specifying which emperor issued the order.

A study published in 2020 compared stable carbon and oxygen isotopes in the stone with those in samples of ancient quarry marble. The results point to a source on Kos. Marble was moved and reused in antiquity, so a geological source identifies the origin of the material more directly than the place where the text was inscribed or displayed.

## Contents

The ordinance requires graves and tombs to remain undisturbed. It condemns acts such as destroying a tomb, moving a buried person, or shifting material used to seal a burial. The penalty pronounced in the text is death. The inscription treats the violation as a serious public offense and frames the decree as protection for the obligations owed to the dead. Without a secure find context, the community that first received or displayed it is unknown.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Matt 27:62–66 recounts a request to guard and seal Jesus's tomb to prevent the disciples from removing his body. Matt 28:11–15 describes the report that the body was stolen, while John 20:2, 13–15 has Mary initially assume that it was carried away. The ordinance illustrates the gravity attached to disturbing a body and other acts of tomb violation in the Greco-Roman world. The Gospel scenes have their own concerns about what an empty tomb could mean.

## Scholarly Issues

Early proposals connected the decree with Claudius and a Roman reaction to reports of Jesus's resurrection. That argument depends on an original Galilean or Judean setting as well as a particular date and imperial motive. Neither the emperor nor a case involving Jesus is named. A proposed setting on Kos can be compared with literary evidence for attacks on a tyrant's grave there, but the identification of the event also remains uncertain. The safest historical use of the ordinance is as evidence of imperial protection against interference with tombs.
