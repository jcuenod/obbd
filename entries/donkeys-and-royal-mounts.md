## Overview

Donkeys carried people and goods throughout the ancient Near East. Bronze Age records from Anatolia and Syria also show that donkeys and mules could carry social and ceremonial significance. The Bible depicts them as ordinary working animals, mounts for prominent families, and markers of royal succession. This range of uses helps explain why a king riding a donkey in Zechariah can be both lowly and recognizably royal. Matthew evokes that image when it describes Jesus entering Jerusalem.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Old Assyrian merchants carried tin and textiles from Assyria to Anatolia in donkey caravans. Their accounting tablets track animals, expenses, and goods, showing how much long-distance trade depended on the donkey. In Middle Bronze Age cemeteries at Tell el-Dabʿa in the eastern Nile Delta, archaeologists also found donkeys buried outside some substantial tombs. Their placement underscores the importance of the animals in some elite funerary customs.

The eighteenth-century BCE [[mari-letters|letters from Mari]] show other uses. The official Baḫdi-Lim advised King Zimri-Lim to honor his kingship by using a chariot or a mule instead of riding a horse. The advice in tablet ARM 6 76 treats both of the proposed options as appropriate for a ruler in that setting. Another letter, ARM 2 37, associates the killing of a donkey foal with making peace between groups. The evidence gives both animals a wider ceremonial range than their role in transport.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Abraham saddles a donkey for his journey in Gen 22:3, a detail consistent with Bronze Age and later travel. The prophet Balaam rides a female donkey in Num 22:21–35. In that narrative the animal sees the divine messenger before its rider does. In Judg 5:10, donkey riders form a distinct audience addressed by the song. The sons of Jair and the descendants of Abdon ride numerous donkeys in Judg 10:4 and 12:14, where family size and access to mounts signal status [@king-stager-life, 112–122, 186–189].

Mules appear at crucial moments in stories of the Davidic household. Absalom rides one in 2 Sam 18:9. In 1 Kgs 1:33, 38, 44, Solomon rides David's own mule to be anointed, making the reigning king's support visible. Lev 19:19 forbids breeding different kinds of livestock together. That rule creates a question for readers of the court accounts, since mules are hybrids. The narratives leave the origins of the royal animals unstated.

## Interpretive Significance

Gen 49:11 pictures Judah tethering a donkey to a vine, an image of abundance in a blessing that includes [[royal-ideology|royal imagery]]. In Zech 9:9–10 Jerusalem's king rides on a donkey, specified in the parallel line as a young donkey, and the poem then promises the removal of the war horse and chariot. The Mari letter and Solomon's anointing illustrate the dignity a royal mount could convey. The contrast with the horse in Zechariah itself helps explain how the donkey also conveys a form of kingship associated with peace. Matt 21:1–11 cites Zechariah and has the disciples bring two animals. This has often been explained as a narrative development of the two descriptions in the Hebrew poetic parallelism.

Some scholars have connected the rite at Mari with Hamor, a name meaning “donkey,” and the cult of [[baal-berith|Baal-berith]] (“Baal of the covenant”) at [[shechem|Shechem]] in Judg 9:4. The names make the comparison possible. Evidence for the Mari rite is explicit, while evidence for the same rite at Shechem remains indirect.
