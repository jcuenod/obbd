## Overview

Nineveh, the capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, fell to a Babylonian and Median coalition in the summer of 612 BCE. Its capture followed the loss of other Assyrian centers and dealt a decisive blow to the empire. A Babylonian chronicle records the siege and sack, and excavated remains show destruction and deaths in the city's defenses. The event gives historical context to Nahum's poem on Nineveh's fall and to other biblical portrayals of the city's fate. Assyrian forces continued to resist from Harran for a few years after losing their capital.

## Sources

The [[fall-of-nineveh-chronicle|Fall of Nineveh Chronicle]] records the campaign as part of the reign of the Babylonian king [[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]]. It identifies the Babylonian army and its Median ally and places the fall in the month of Abu, in the summer of 612 BCE. It reports a siege lasting about three months and the removal of plunder. The ruler of the Medes is commonly identified as Cyaxares, the name given in Greek sources [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles].

Excavations at the Halzi Gate uncovered a narrowed passageway and the remains of people who died amid debris. The changes to the entrance fit preparations for a defense, and the destruction fits a violent end. The identities of the dead cannot be established from their position alone. Some may have been defending the city and others trying to flee.

## Reconstruction

The Medes captured Assur in 614 BCE, and Nabopolassar reached an agreement with their king. Two years later, the allied armies attacked [[nineveh|Nineveh]] itself. The Assyrian king Sîn-šarra-iškun appears to have died during the fall. The loss of the capital and its resources left the Assyrian state unable to regain its former position.

Aššur-uballiṭ II maintained a government and army at [[harran|Harran]] until the city fell in 610 BCE. Egyptian troops joined an unsuccessful attempt to retake it in 609 BCE. Nineveh's destruction was therefore a decisive stage in Assyria's collapse, followed by a short period in which remaining Assyrian forces and their allies tried to reverse events [@abc].

## Biblical Accounts

Nah 2:1–13 (MT 2:2–14) pictures an assault, the opening of river gates, and the plunder of Nineveh. Nah 3:8–10 cites the earlier defeat of Egyptian Thebes, captured by Assyria in 663 BCE. Scholars debate whether the poem took shape before Nineveh fell, afterward, or in more than one stage. A later Greek account preserved by Diodorus describes flooding that opened a breach. The Babylonian chronicle gives no such detail, and Nah 2:6 (MT 2:7) does not establish that a flood breached the wall.

Zeph 2:13–15 pictures Nineveh reduced to a desolate place. Jonah 3:1–10 imagines a different episode, in which the inhabitants repent and the city is spared. Later Jewish readers could know the city through stories of its eventual defeat as well as its earlier reprieve. Tob 14:15 recounts news of the destruction, and surviving forms of Tobit give different names for its conquerors. Xenophon's account of a deserted city called Mespila is commonly associated with the ruins of Nineveh and supplies an example of its later place in Greek tradition.
