## Overview

Amulets were objects people wore or placed in buildings and graves in the hope of protection. In Iron Age Judah, tombs yielded small amulets with Egyptian designs, and two silver rolls from Jerusalem bear Hebrew words close to the priestly blessing in Numbers. Mesopotamian objects sometimes carry images intended to ward off harmful beings. These finds help explain why biblical texts associate small objects, images and written words with the body, while leaving room for differences between jewelry, protective objects and reminders of religious obligations.

## Archaeological Evidence

Egyptian and Egyptian-style amulets occur in Iron Age tombs in Judah. Examples include the *wedjat* (the restored eye of the god Horus), figures of the protective deity [[bes|Bes]], small figures often called Pataikos, and scarabs shaped like beetles. The objects form part of the ordinary archaeological record of burial and personal piety. Their designs show the circulation of Egyptian images, although the circumstances of burial rarely tell us precisely what the wearer called a figure or expected it to do. [@keel-uehlinger-ggig] [@king-stager-life]

Two tiny [[ketef-hinnom-amulets|rolled silver strips from Ketef Hinnom]] were excavated in a Jerusalem tomb. Their inscriptions include wording closely comparable to Num 6:24–26, where priests are instructed to ask Yahweh to bless and protect Israel. Most scholars date the rolls to the late Iron Age, as argued by Gabriel Barkay and his colleagues on the basis of the burial context and the forms of the letters. Some have proposed a later date. The overlap with Numbers demonstrates that a form of the blessing was already in circulation by the date of the amulets, while its relationship to the completed book remains uncertain. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions] [@ahituv-echoes]

In first-millennium Mesopotamia, [[lamashtu-pazuzu-amulets|objects depicting Pazuzu and Lamashtu]] were used to counter threats associated with harmful beings. Two plaques acquired at Arslan Tash in northern Syria are often discussed alongside protective texts of this kind. They were acquired without a controlled excavation context, and their authenticity is disputed. The Mesopotamian objects provide firmer evidence for the use of images and inscriptions together. [@ddd]

## In the Bible

Isa 3:18–23 names charms and crescent necklaces among the ornaments that will be taken away from the women of Zion. In Judg 8:21, 26, crescents appear among the spoils associated with the Midianite kings and their camels. Such lists mix objects of different kinds, and the shape of a pendant by itself leaves its particular function open. In 2 Macc 12:40, tokens associated with the idols of Jamnia are found beneath the clothing of the dead, and the narrator condemns their presence.

Deut 6:8 and 11:18 direct hearers to bind the words on the hand and place them between the eyes. The Hebrew *ṭôṭāpōt*, often rendered “frontlets,” has an uncertain derivation. Some readers see an original metaphor for keeping the words continually in mind, comparable to binding teaching on the heart in Prov 6:21 and 7:3. A physical interpretation is documented by [[dead-sea-scrolls|Qumran finds]] containing scriptural passages in small leather cases. These objects are called *tefillin* or phylacteries, the latter term also used in Matt 23:5. [@abd]
