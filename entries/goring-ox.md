## Overview

The laws of the goring ox in Exodus 21 distinguish a first attack from an attack by an animal already known to be dangerous. Similar distinctions appear in two Mesopotamian law collections from the early second millennium BCE. One provision about an ox that kills another ox is especially close to a ruling from Eshnunna. The comparison helps explain how these collections reason about responsibility, negligence, and compensation. It also raises a question about how the biblical writers came to use cases found in earlier legal traditions.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[laws-of-eshnunna|Laws of Eshnunna]] address both one ox killing another and a dangerous ox killing a person. In the former case, the owners divide the value of the living animal and the carcass. The ruling on a dangerous ox makes the owner's prior notice and failure to control it relevant to the payment. Different payments apply according to the status of the person killed. These cases organize liability around what the person responsible for the animal could have known and done. [@roth-law, 65–67] [@cos2, 2.130]

The [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] distinguish an ox that gores a person in the street from a known gorer whose owner fails to restrain it. The first case gives no grounds for a claim against the owner. In the second, the owner owes silver if the animal kills someone. The amount again depends on the status of the victim. Neither Mesopotamian collection prescribes the execution of the negligent owner in these cases. [@roth-law, 129–130] [@cos2, 2.131]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 21:28–32 also distinguishes a first fatal attack from a known owner's failure to restrain a habitual gorer. The ox is stoned and its meat may not be eaten. When an owner who has received warning fails to restrain the ox, the owner is liable to death, although a ransom may be imposed. The law sets a separate payment when the victim is an enslaved person. The ransom provision differs from Num 35:31, which rules out accepting a ransom for someone judged guilty of murder. The two texts address different kinds of responsibility for a death.

Exod 21:35 provides for the sale of the live ox and division of the proceeds and the carcass. The sequence is particularly close to the Eshnunna provision. Exod 21:36 adds a rule for an ox known to gore. Its owner replaces the dead ox and takes the carcass. The placement of these laws beside rules for damage caused by an uncovered pit (Exod 21:33–34) brings several related questions of liability together.

## Interpretive Significance

J. J. Finkelstein interpreted the stoning and the ban on eating the animal in light of the place of human life in the created order. Gen 9:5, where God requires a reckoning from an animal for human blood, offers a related way to think about the gravity of the killing. The biblical provisions combine these concerns with practical rules about compensation and prior knowledge. Their shared legal pattern with the cuneiform texts remains clear even where outcomes differ.

David P. Wright argues that the writers of the Covenant Code used and revised written Mesopotamian law, drawing on Hammurabi and on a provision like the one from Eshnunna. Other scholars explain the resemblances through a wider body of shared legal and scribal teaching that circulated orally or in writing. The surviving collections give hypothetical cases in a form useful for legal reasoning. They give little evidence for how often such disputes reached an actual court.
