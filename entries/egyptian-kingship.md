## Overview

The Egyptian king represented the gods in temple ritual, claimed a special relationship with Horus and the sun god Re, and was responsible for maintaining right order. These claims appear in royal names, temple reliefs, and narratives of divine birth from different periods of pharaonic history. They provide a major comparison for biblical descriptions of the Davidic king as God's son and for the imagery of coronation. The books of Exodus and Ezekiel depict pharaoh from a different perspective, giving readers a biblical critique of the authority that Egyptian kings claimed.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

An Egyptian ruler was responsible for [[maat|maat]], the order understood to sustain both the world and human society. Temple scenes show the king making offerings and carrying out rites on behalf of Egypt, even though priests performed much of the daily service. Monumental art also presents the king defeating enemies and restoring order. The [[narmer-palette|Narmer Palette]] already represents the ruler striking a defeated enemy, an image that continued in later royal art [@anep] [@keel-symbolism].

The developed royal titulary, the formal series of names and titles, had five parts. It included the Horus name, the Two Ladies name invoking the protective goddesses of Upper and Lower Egypt, the Golden Horus name, the throne name, and the personal name introduced by the title Son of Re. Other texts and images give royal sonship a narrative form. The divine-birth scenes of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahri portray Amun as the founder of the child's royal claim [@ael] [@anep].

Egyptologists debate the language of royal divinity. Henri Frankfort emphasized the king's identity as a god. Georges Posener and later scholars gave greater weight to distinctions between a mortal individual, the divine character of the office, and the king's representation in ritual. The evidence varies with the source and period. Titles, divine-birth stories, cults of individual kings, and routine court documents give different access to Egyptian ideas of the king.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ps 2:7 announces the king's sonship in a divine decree, and 2 Sam 7:14 expresses a father-son relationship in the promise to David's dynasty. These texts are commonly read in terms of the king's installation or adoption as God's son. Egyptian divine-birth narratives give a comparison for [[king-as-son-of-god|royal sonship]], while Assyrian evidence also uses divine parentage and nurture as language of royal election. Ps 110:1–4 associates the king's place at the deity's right hand with a role expressed in priestly terms. The distinction between royal and priestly action varies across the ancient settings.

Archaeological and textual comparisons have been proposed for individual features of Judah's coronation. Albrecht Alt connected the titles in Isa 9:6 (MT 9:5) with the Egyptian fivefold titulary. Scholars disagree about how to divide the Hebrew names and whether the structure reflects Egyptian practice. Gerhard von Rad interpreted the object given to the newly crowned Joash in 2 Kgs 11:12, called *ʿēdût* (“testimony”), in light of an Egyptian royal protocol. The Hebrew term does not establish what sort of object or document the passage describes.

## Interpretive Significance

Exod 5:2 presents pharaoh refusing to recognize YHWH's authority over his decision. Ezek 29:3 depicts pharaoh as a river monster claiming the Nile for himself. Ezek 32:2 turns images of the lion and sea monster into a lament over Egypt's king. Biblical descriptions of pharaoh can be heard against claims about the king's protection of the land and control of disorder. Parallels at that level illuminate the texts without requiring every biblical image to reproduce an Egyptian royal title.
