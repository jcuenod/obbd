## Overview

*Enki and Ninhursag* is a Sumerian poem preserved in Old Babylonian copies from the early second millennium BCE. It opens in Dilmun, a land whose quiet and purity are balanced by a lack of fresh water. The god Enki makes water available, but his later actions lead to a curse that the goddess Ninhursag eventually lifts. The poem has been compared with the garden and rib in Genesis. Its opening description of animals also provides a parallel for prophetic portraits of a world without predation.

## Manuscripts and Date

The surviving witnesses are written in Sumerian, one of the principal written languages of ancient Mesopotamia. The poem is known from clay tablets, including copies from the Old Babylonian period. Their dates give a point of reference for the history of the work, although its first composition may be older. Some wording and the arrangement of particular episodes remain matters of interpretation. [@anet, 37–41]

## Contents

The poem begins by describing Dilmun as pure and free of familiar dangers. The lion does no harm, and the wolf does not carry off a lamb. Fresh water is initially lacking. Enki makes it available, transforming the settlement into a place with water for its inhabitants and fields. Historical Dilmun is linked with Bahrain and the Gulf, while the poem presents the place as a setting for the deeds of gods. [@anet, 37–41]

The story then turns to a succession of births and to plants that Ninhursag causes to grow. Enki consumes the plants, and Ninhursag curses him. When he becomes ill, she returns and produces healing deities associated with the parts of his body that hurt. One of these is Ninti, who appears in the exchange about Enki's rib. Fertility, harm and healing drive the narrative. [@anet, 37–41]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The provision of water and the growth of plants invite comparison with the [[garden-of-god|garden in Eden]] (Gen 2:8–14), though the plots give those features different purposes. The closest thematic comparison is with the absence of animal harm in Isa 11:6–9 and 65:25. There the prophets use peace among animals to describe an expected transformation. In the Sumerian poem the absence of predation helps characterize Dilmun at the outset. [@anet, 37–41] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Samuel Noah Kramer proposed a link between Ninti and the formation of the woman from the man's side or rib in Gen 2:21–22. Sumerian words written with the same sign could refer to a rib and to life, permitting a wordplay in the Sumerian account. Hebrew uses different terms for the side or rib and for the name Eve, linked with life in Gen 3:20. The proposal is famous but the Hebrew wording gives little support for direct use of the Sumerian poem. The parallels involving eating plants and consequences (Gen 3:6, 17–19) are also disputed. [@anet, 37–41] [@abd]
