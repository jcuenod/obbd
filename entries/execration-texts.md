## Overview

The Egyptian Execration Texts are lists of enemies inscribed on pottery and figurines for rites intended to neutralize threats to Egypt. The best-known groups date to the Middle Kingdom, in the early second millennium BCE. They identify foreign rulers and territories as well as Egyptians and other potential sources of danger. The lists preserve early spellings of Levantine place names, including a name widely identified with Jerusalem. The ritual handling of inscribed objects also provides a comparison for biblical uses of broken vessels to represent judgment.

## Manuscripts and Date

Kurt Sethe published a group of inscribed pottery fragments now in Berlin in 1926. Georges Posener published a group of inscribed clay figurines associated with Saqqara and now chiefly in Brussels in 1940. The figures resemble bound captives and are usually dated slightly later than the Berlin bowls. Both groups are assigned to the Middle Kingdom. The deposits associated with the Egyptian fortress at Mirgissa in Nubia include broken red pottery and figures in an excavated context. Human remains at Mirgissa have been interpreted as evidence of a ritual killing, and the person's identity and the circumstances of death are disputed. [@cos1, 1.32] [@anet, 328–329]

## Contents

The lists refer to rulers, their associates and peoples in regions including Nubia, Libya and the Levant. They also target hostile or potentially rebellious Egyptians. Formulas anticipate various acts of enmity, extending the rite to people who might threaten Egypt in the future. Inscription identified the object with the target, and the handling and breaking of the object enacted the harm or rendered the threat ineffective. Deposition completed the rite in some cases. The treatment of objects varied among the surviving groups. [@cos1, 1.32]

The names preserve Egyptian scribes' approximations of foreign words. The name often rendered Rushalimum is widely identified with [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]]. On that reading it is the earliest surviving reference to the city. The lists are also a source for West Semitic personal names, including names comparable to those associated with [[amorites|Amorites]] elsewhere in the second millennium BCE. [@anet, 328–329] [@cos1, 1.32]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The lists give a historical setting for the later literary presentation of Jerusalem with its own ruler in Josh 10:1. They do not establish the chronology of the events in Joshua. Jer 19:10–11 asks Jeremiah to break a vessel before witnesses and makes the shattering an enacted analogy for the fate of Jerusalem. Ps 2:9 describes nations being shattered like pottery. Both draw on the intelligibility of deliberately broken vessels as images of defeat. Num 22:6 shares the political premise that a curse could weaken opponents before battle, though Balak commissions a seer to speak and the story describes a different practice. [@cos1, 1.32] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Egyptian spelling and changing settlement names complicate individual place identifications. The lists reflect the ritual concerns and information available to Egyptian scribes, so frequency of mention cannot directly measure a settlement's size or influence. The analogy to prophetic sign-acts is strongest at the level of how an action represents an intended result. In Jeremiah the prophet announces the Lord's judgment, while in the Egyptian rites the practitioners use the prescribed act to neutralize designated threats. [@cos1, 1.32] [@anet, 328–329]
