## Overview

Slavery and debt servitude appear in ancient Near Eastern and Greco-Roman records and throughout the Bible. Some people entered service for a limited time because of debt, while others could be sold, inherited, or held permanently. Biblical laws specify releases and protections in some cases and recognize lasting ownership in others. The Hebrew word often translated “slave” can also denote a servant or official. The circumstances of each passage matter for understanding the loss of freedom it describes, and surviving legal and documentary evidence shows substantial differences among ancient communities.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] provide for the release after three years of members of a debtor's family compelled to serve. Other provisions punish kidnapping, assisting flight, or concealing a fugitive. Cutting a slave's ear could be a punishment for repudiating a master, a different circumstance from the ear rite in Exodus. The [[edict-of-ammi-saduqa|Edict of Ammi-ṣaduqa]] gives another example of release from debt bondage and distinguishes those eligible from other enslaved people [@roth-law].

Documents from Babylonia record debt arrangements and sales. Later documents from [[samaria-papyri|Wadi Daliyeh]] and [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine]] likewise place enslaved people within transactions concerning households and property. The documents offer direct evidence of individual cases. The law collections express wider legal expectations and distinctions that were not necessarily applied in the same way everywhere [@cos3] [@tad].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 21:2–6 provides release in the seventh year for a Hebrew male slave and describes piercing the ear if he remains permanently. The meaning of “Hebrew” here is debated. A connection with the social designation [[habiru|ḫabiru]] has been proposed, without establishing that the terms identify the same class. Exod 21:7–11 addresses a daughter sold into a household with marriage expectations and provides protections in that arrangement. Deut 15:12–18 explicitly includes women in its release provisions and requires that the released person receive supplies.

Exod 21:16 condemns kidnapping for sale, and Exod 21:20–21, 26–27 gives penalties and release provisions in cases of injury by a master. The legal distinctions still recognize the master's ownership. Lev 25:39–46 grants a different status to impoverished Israelite kin and permits Israelites to hold foreigners as permanent, inheritable slaves. Deut 23:15–16 (MT 23:16–17) protects a fugitive and permits settlement. Its scope is debated, and its stated response differs from the return required in many neighboring laws.

2 Kgs 4:1 portrays a creditor threatening to take a widow's children. Neh 5:1–5 describes families facing loss of property and the enslavement of children in a debt crisis. Jer 34:8–22 describes a release of Hebrew slaves and the reversal of that decision. These narratives give practical settings for [[debt-release|laws and decrees of release]].

## Interpretive Significance

Phlm 8–21 concerns Onesimus within the setting of Roman slavery. The letter asks that he be received as a beloved brother, while readers debate whether Paul expects legal release. Roman institutions, Israelite debt law, and foreign chattel slavery in Leviticus involved different rules and routes into bondage. Distinguishing those systems helps readers take the coercion in each passage seriously and avoids assigning every occurrence of the same term a single legal status.
