## Overview

The Marduka Tablet is a Babylonian administrative record from the Persian period, often placed near the transition from Darius I to Xerxes I in the early fifth century BCE. It names a man called Marduka in connection with Susa, a Persian royal residence. Arthur Ungnad proposed that this man was Mordecai, the Jewish court figure in the book of Esther. The name and administrative setting fit the world of the story, but neither identifies the man in the record as the biblical Mordecai.

## Description and Provenance

The document is a clay tablet written in Babylonian cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script used for administrative records in Babylonia. Ungnad discussed it in a study published in 1940–41. ## Contents

The entry concerning Marduka is administrative, not a biography. The official designation is reported as *sipīru*, commonly understood as a scribe or secretary; a more specific assignment or rank should not be inferred from that gloss. <!-- VERIFY: the signs behind the reading sipīru and its meaning in this document. --> The reference to Susa connects the man to business involving a royal center, but it does not by itself show that he lived there or served at the king's gate. The later [[murashu-archive|Murašû archive]] supplies a separate body of evidence for personal names and administrative roles in Persian-period Babylonia.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Esth 2:5 introduces Mordecai as a Jew of Benjaminite descent living in the citadel of Susa. Esth 2:19, 21 places him at the king's gate, where the story introduces a plot by two royal doorkeepers. His presence there suggests access to the court, but neither verse specifies his title or establishes that he held the same post as the man on the tablet. For the distinction between positions and titles, see [[court-officials|court officials]].

Ezra 2:2 and its parallel in Neh 7:7 list a Mordecai among those who returned with Zerubbabel. The occurrence is useful as evidence for the name, not as a reason to identify the returnee with Esther's courtier. The Hebrew name is commonly associated with [[marduk|Marduk]], the chief god of Babylon, and compared with the Babylonian name Marduka. A Babylonian name alone does not establish its bearer's ethnicity or religious allegiance.

## Scholarly Issues

Ungnad's proposal depends on the closeness of the names and the link to Susa. David J. A. Clines challenged the inference that such a match identifies one person: the document does not independently give Marduka the same position that interpreters infer from Esther. The [[persepolis-fortification-tablets|Persepolis Fortification texts]] and Babylonian records attest more than one bearer of the name or related forms, weakening an argument based on its supposed rarity. A Persian-period parallel is therefore stronger evidence for the plausibility of the setting than for the disputed identification.

Esther is often read as a court tale or novella, a short narrative organized around palace intrigue and reversals. Herodotus names Amestris as Xerxes' wife, so his account does not corroborate Esther's account of its queen. A second difficulty arises if the exile notice in Esth 2:6 refers to Mordecai himself: he would have been more than a century old during Xerxes' reign. If it refers instead to his ancestor Kish, that particular chronological objection falls away. Neither reading makes the tablet evidence for the events of the book.
