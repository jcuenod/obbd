## Overview

A seal is an engraved object used to produce an impression. In ancient Israel and Judah, officials and other owners used stamp seals or signet rings to identify themselves and authenticate documents and goods. A bulla is a small piece of sealing material, often clay, that can preserve the design after the original seal or document has disappeared. Biblical accounts use sealing to mark ownership, authorize action and protect records. Seal imagery also expresses trust, delegated authority and the withdrawal of royal favor.

## Archaeological Evidence

Levantine stamp seals were carved in stone and sometimes mounted as rings or worn on cords. Their engraved faces produced designs in relief when pressed into clay. Mesopotamian [[cylinder-seals|cylinder seals]] worked by rolling an engraved surface across the material. The backs of some bullae preserve impressions of papyrus fibers and string. Others show textiles or attachment to containers. Such marks can identify what a seal protected even where the impression's face has no inscription. Excavated groups from Jerusalem include bullae from the City of David and the Ophel, where an impression of [[hezekiah-bulla|Hezekiah's seal]] was recovered. [@avigad-sass-wsss, 21–46, 167–170] [@ophel-bullae, catalog no. B1] [@neaehl, 2:705–712]

Names on West Semitic seals are often preceded by *l-*, marking ownership, and may be followed by a patronymic or office. Titles include “servant of the king” and “who is over the house.” The [[seal-of-shema|Seal of Shema]] combines a lion with its owner's name and service to Jeroboam. Many Judahite seals of the late eighth and seventh centuries BCE favor inscriptions with few or no images. Administrative and religious explanations have been proposed, and some contemporary examples retain figural designs. Unprovenanced objects pose problems of authenticity and date. Excavated finds provide a firmer basis for tracking such changes. [@avigad-sass-wsss, 22–27, 49–60, 512–515, 548–551] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig]

## In the Bible

In Gen 38:18, 25, Judah's signet and cord serve first as a pledge and then as evidence identifying him. Pharaoh gives Joseph his signet ring as part of his appointment in Gen 41:42. Jezebel's use of Ahab's seal in 1 Kgs 21:8 gives her letters the appearance of royal authority. Esth 3:10–12 and 8:8–10 portray the royal signet as authorizing decrees, which the story presents as irrevocable. Exod 28:9–11, 21, 36 uses seal engraving as a comparison for inscriptions on the high priest's stones and gold plate. [@abd, 3:829–830]

Jer 32:10–14 describes the sealing of a purchase deed, and Neh 9:38 (MT 10:1) associates named leaders with a sealed agreement. In Dan 6:17 (MT 6:18), the king and his nobles seal the stone blocking the den. The act would make interference apparent. Job 38:14 likens the changed appearance of the earth at dawn to clay taking an impression. Song 8:6 uses a seal as an image of enduring attachment. Jer 22:24 threatens the removal of Jehoiachin even if he were God's signet ring. Hag 2:23 makes Zerubbabel like a chosen signet, an image often understood as reversing the earlier threat. [@abd, 3:829–832]
