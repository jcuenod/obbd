## Overview

The Instructions for Temple Personnel is a Hittite text setting out duties for people who served the gods. Surviving copies come from the Late Bronze Age capital at Ḫattuša. The instructions address the handling of offerings, bodily purity, and the need to keep a night watch. They present temple service as a serious obligation, with penalties for neglect and theft. These rules give a close comparison for the duties and restrictions of priests and Levites in the Bible, especially the charge to guard sacred space and handle holy food properly.

## Manuscripts and Date

The text survives in cuneiform copies from Ḫattuša and is listed as CTH 264 in the modern catalog of Hittite texts. The surviving copies belong to the later Hittite kingdom, and the composition may draw on earlier instruction. It belongs to a group of rulings that assign obligations to royal and temple personnel. The writing gives a formal account of expected conduct, while the penalties describe how serious an offense could be in the eyes of the authorities [@cos1, 1.83] [@anet, 207–210].

## Contents

Temple workers are responsible for bringing offerings promptly and ensuring that what belongs to the god reaches the god's table. The text condemns the diversion of provisions. It also requires clean preparation and sets rules about bodily purity, including washing after sexual intercourse before approaching the sacred setting. The temple and its holdings require a night watch, and negligent or dishonest workers face severe penalties.

The instructions compare a deity with a human master whose servant must keep food and vessels clean. This gives a direct expression of the ancient [[care-and-feeding-of-the-gods|practice of providing for the gods]]. The analogy helps explain why the text addresses ordinary matters such as cleanliness, intact deliveries, and guards alongside serious threats of punishment.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Jacob Milgrom compared the Hittite guard duty with the Hebrew expression for keeping charge at the sanctuary. Num 3:38 assigns responsibility for the sanctuary to personnel stationed near it. Num 18:1–7 distributes duties among priests and Levites and threatens death to an unauthorized person who approaches. The texts compare in the seriousness of assigned custody. The biblical threat against encroachment also protects a division of sacred access within Israel's own system.

The Hittite concern for purity gives context for Lev 22:1–9, where an impure priest must refrain from sacred food. Lev 15:16–18 describes washing and impurity after emission or intercourse, with consequences for later contact with holy things. In 1 Sam 21:4–5 (MT 21:5–6), abstinence from women matters when holy bread is offered to David's men. The biblical requirements for purity belong to their own categories, and the Hittite text illustrates the importance of a body's condition when handling divine provisions.

Other passages concern the integrity of offerings. Exod 22:29–30 (MT 22:28–29) forbids withholding or delaying certain offerings, and 1 Sam 2:12–17 depicts attendants improperly taking portions of sacrifices. These examples show pressures that a temple administrator might recognize in either setting.

## Scholarly Issues

The Hittite analogy with a human master and divine meals illuminates ancient understandings of service by means of food. In Ps 50:12–13 YHWH rejects the idea that he eats the flesh of sacrificial animals or needs human provision. The biblical texts can require careful handling of offerings while using a different explanation for what God receives from sacrifice. Such similarities and differences can arise in related institutions without demonstrating the direct use of the Hittite instruction text.
