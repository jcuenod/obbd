## Overview

The Nabataeans were an Arab people whose kingdom developed around Petra and extended through parts of the southern Levant and northwestern Arabia. Their towns and inscriptions record a society involved in trade, agriculture, and the management of scarce water. The kingdom was a regional power from the Hellenistic period until Rome annexed it in 106 CE. Jewish and Christian texts place Nabataeans in settings that involve diplomacy, refuge, trade and political conflict. Paul's account of an official serving King Aretas provides the most direct New Testament connection.

## Sources

Diodorus Siculus describes campaigns against the Nabataeans in the late fourth century BCE (Library of History 19.94–100). His account emphasizes their mobility, their defense of desert strongholds, and their trade. Inscriptions and coins from later centuries name kings and patrons. The rock-cut tombs, sanctuaries, and water installations of [[petra-and-sela|Petra]] and sites such as Hegra show what developed as the kingdom became more settled.

Many Nabataean inscriptions are written in a variety of Aramaic, while personal names and other forms reflect Arabic. Nabataean letterforms are widely understood as a major ancestor of the Arabic script. The similarity of the name Nabataean to biblical Nebaioth (Gen 25:13) has encouraged an identification. The names differ in their consonants, leaving the proposed equation without a firm linguistic basis.

## History

Nabataean centers provided links between Arabia and the Mediterranean and took part in the [[incense-trade|aromatic trade]]. Cultivation, collection of water, and support of travel were also important. The kingdom interacted with the Hasmonean rulers and later with the Herodian family. Aretas III controlled Damascus for a period in the first century BCE and issued coins there.

Aretas IV reigned from 9 BCE to 40 CE. According to [[josephus|Josephus]], Herod Antipas planned to divorce Aretas's daughter to marry Herodias. The abandoned marriage and a boundary dispute contributed to war, conventionally dated around 36 CE. Antipas's defeat was interpreted by some as divine punishment for executing John the Baptist (Ant. 18.109–119). In 106 CE Rome incorporated the kingdom into the province of Arabia. Nabataean communities and institutions persisted under new political arrangements.

## In the Bible

In 1 Macc 5:25 Nabataeans meet Judas Maccabeus's group peaceably and give information about events in Gilead. 2 Macc 5:8 names a ruler of the Arabs called Aretas, usually identified as Aretas I. Mark 6:17–18 associates John's imprisonment with his criticism of Antipas's marriage to Herodias. Josephus's account supplies additional political background to the marriage and war.

Paul says that the ethnarch of King Aretas guarded [[damascus|Damascus]] to apprehend him and that he escaped through a window in the wall (2 Cor 11:32–33). Acts 9:23–25 also describes an escape over the wall. Aretas IV is the likely king, but the ethnarch's jurisdiction is debated. He might have governed the city for Aretas or directed a Nabataean community there. A gap in surviving civic coinage cannot decide between those explanations.

In Gal 1:17 Paul says he went to Arabia after his calling. Nabataean territory is a reasonable geographic reading, although Paul gives no destination. Gal 4:25 places Sinai in Arabia as part of an argument about two covenants. The use of the regional name there gives little basis for fixing an exact route or location.
