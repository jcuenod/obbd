## Overview

Long-distance travelers in the southern Levant used corridors along the coastal plain, across the central highlands and along the plateau east of the Jordan. These routes carried armies, traders and messengers between Egypt, Syria and Arabia. Their precise courses changed with political boundaries and local conditions. Egyptian descriptions, excavated forts and patterns of settlement help reconstruct some stretches. The biblical texts present roads as places to negotiate passage and conduct trade, and as images of danger, return and divine arrival.

## Location and Identification

The coastal route linked the eastern Nile Delta and northern Sinai with [[gaza|Gaza]]. Roads continuing north along the plain branched inland through passes toward the Jezreel Valley and onward to Syria. Ancient journeys might follow different branches depending on their destination. Modern maps often label this international corridor *Via Maris*, a Latin phrase derived from the scriptural expression in Isa 9:1 (MT 8:23). The Latin phrase figured in medieval geographical discussion, and its modern application to the whole corridor does not establish an ancient road name. [@abd, 5:776–788] [@mazar-archaeology]

East of the Jordan, routes crossed the plateau through [[edomites|Edom]] and [[moabites|Moab]] and continued toward Damascus. Deep valleys made some crossings difficult and offered several alternative courses. The Roman Via Nova Traiana later followed much of this broad corridor from Syria toward the Red Sea. Roman engineering documents one phase in the history of the route. [@abd, 5:776–788] [@neaehl]

## Archaeological History

The campaign reliefs of Seti I at Karnak depict a series of named installations on the Egyptian route toward Canaan. [[papyrus-anastasi|Papyrus Anastasi I]], a scribal composition that tests knowledge of places and routes, supplies additional names and descriptions. Excavation at Tell el-Borg and at Tell Hebua, commonly identified with the frontier stronghold of Tjaru, gives material evidence for military control of the northern Sinai corridor associated with the Ways of Horus. Changes in shorelines and waterways complicate the identification of individual stretches. [@anet, 475–479] [@anep, figs. 322–326] [@neaehl, 4:1385–1393]

An ancient route can leave few traces of its surface. Archaeologists therefore compare the positions of settlements, forts, springs, passes and fords with descriptions of travel. Such evidence can locate corridors more securely than individual tracks. Paving or milestones from later periods document the use of a stretch at a particular time. [@mazar-archaeology] [@abd, 5:776–788]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Num 20:17 and 21:22 describe requests to pass through Edom and Sihon's territory along the king's road without using fields or wells. The Hebrew expression can designate a public or royal road and need not be the name of one continuous highway. Deut 2:27 likewise presents remaining on the road as a condition of peaceful passage. Exod 13:17 describes Israel avoiding the nearer route through the land of the Philistines. Its geography fits the use of the northern Sinai corridor to reach the southern coastal plain. [@abd, 5:776–788] [@anet, 254–255]

The Ishmaelite caravan in Gen 37:25 exemplifies movement from Gilead toward Egypt, carrying aromatic goods. Judg 5:6 depicts insecurity through the abandonment of the main tracks for byways. In Jer 31:21, road markers guide the return to home towns, while Isa 40:3 uses a road built through the wilderness as an image of divine arrival. [@abd, 5:776–788]
