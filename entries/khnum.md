## Overview

Khnum was an Egyptian god often shown with the head of a ram. He was especially important at Elephantine near the First Cataract of the Nile and at Esna farther north. In royal birth scenes he shapes the future ruler on a potter’s wheel, and hymns from Esna praise him as a creator. This imagery provides an Egyptian comparison for biblical passages that portray God forming human beings or working clay. Khnum’s temple also helps explain the setting of a dispute involving Judean worship at Elephantine.

## Sources and Iconography

At the southern frontier, Khnum was associated with the Nile at the First Cataract and with the sustaining power of its waters. The [[famine-stele|Famine Stele]] presents him as the god who can restore the Nile flood and end a period of scarcity. The inscription is Ptolemaic and sets its story much earlier, in the reign of Djoser. It reflects later claims about the importance of Khnum’s cult in the region. [@ael, 3:94–103] [@abd, 4:1114–1116, 6:369–371]

In Hatshepsut’s birth scenes at Deir el-Bahari and those of Amenhotep III at Luxor, Khnum fashions the child and the child’s *ka*, a vital aspect of the person represented as a counterpart. The potter’s wheel gives concrete form to the idea of divine craftsmanship. At Esna, hymns inscribed in the Roman period address him as a creator of living beings and develop the imagery beyond the birth of a royal person. [@anep, fig. 569] [@ael, 3:109–115]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 2:7 says that God forms the human from the ground and breathes life into him. The verb can describe a potter’s work, though Genesis does not mention a wheel. Job 10:8–9 explicitly compares divine formation to shaping clay, and Ps 139:13–16 pictures the intricacy of formation in the womb. Isa 45:9 uses the relationship between potter and clay to address human protest, while Isa 64:8 (MT 64:7) makes clay an image of dependence. Jer 18:1–6 uses a potter’s reworking of a vessel to illustrate divine freedom in dealing with Israel. [@anep, fig. 569] [@abd, 5:429–431]

## Scholarly Issues

Other ancient traditions also use clay in stories of [[creation-of-humanity|human formation]]. In the Babylonian [[atrahasis|Atrahasis epic]] clay is mixed with divine material, and in the Gilgamesh epic the goddess Aruru forms Enkidu from clay. The similarities give a regional setting for biblical imagery. Egyptian examples are particularly vivid because they represent the creator as a potter at work. The biblical uses serve different literary purposes and do not establish a particular source. [@lambert-millard-atrahasis, 42–67] [@george-gilgamesh, 1:544–45] [@abd, 4:1124–1126]

A petition from [[elephantine|Elephantine]] implicates priests of Khnum in the destruction of the Judean temple in 410 BCE. Some scholars have proposed that sacrifices of sheep or rams provoked tensions because of the god’s animal associations. The petition does not identify that motive, and political or institutional conflict may have been involved. [@tad, A4.7–A4.8] [@cos3, 3.51]
