## Overview

Elyon means “Most High” in Hebrew and is a biblical title for God, used alone or in the expression El Elyon. A related form appears in an eighth-century BCE Aramaic treaty, and a much later account from Phoenicia names a figure called Elioun. These sources have led scholars to ask whether Elyon once designated a deity distinct from El. The question bears especially on the Song of Moses, where the Most High apportions the nations and Israel is named as Yahweh's portion.

## Sources and Iconography

The [[sefire-treaties|Sefire treaty]] invokes “El and Elyon” in a list of divine witnesses. Some read the expression as naming two deities. Others understand it as a compound expression for one deity or take Elyon as a title further identifying El. Philo of Byblos's account of Phoenician traditions portrays Elioun as distinct from the figure equivalent to El. The account is preserved by later authors and is far removed in date from the Aramaic treaty. [@kai, no. 222 A] [@cos2, 2.82] [@ddd, 293–298]

The Ugaritic poems use a related expression of height for Baal. They do not clearly attest an independent god called Elyon. The comparison shows that terms for height could function in titles of divinity. The two sources normally invoked for an independent deity are Sefire and Philo, and their interpretation remains disputed. [@ktu3, 1.16 III 5–9] [@ddd, 293–298]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 14:18–22 presents Melchizedek, king of Salem, as priest of El Elyon and describes him blessing Abram. Abram subsequently invokes Yahweh in connection with the same title. Some scholars associate the title here with pre-Israelite worship at Jerusalem, identifying the Salem of the narrative with Jerusalem and comparing later temple language. That historical reconstruction is possible, while the final narrative directly associates the title with Yahweh. [@ddd, 293–298] [@abd, 4:922–923]

In Deut 32:8–9 the Most High apportions the nations and Yahweh's allotted portion is Israel. The traditional Hebrew Masoretic Text reads “sons of Israel” in the description of the apportionment. A [[4qdeut-j|Qumran manuscript]] has a reading commonly understood as “sons of God,” and the main ancient Greek tradition refers to angels of God. The readings differ on the beings after whose number the peoples are apportioned. [@ddd, 293–298]

## Scholarly Issues

One interpretation of Deuteronomy sees Elyon as the head of a [[divine-council|divine assembly]] who apportions peoples to other gods, including Yahweh. Another understands the Most High and Yahweh as two ways of referring to Israel's God. The heavenly reading in earlier witnesses strengthens the comparison with divine assemblies without settling the identity of the one who distributes the nations. Ps 82:6–7 provides further background for the language of beings described as children of the Most High. [@ddd, 293–298]

Ps 91:1–2 brings several names and titles together. The psalmist associates shelter under the Most High and the Almighty with trust in Yahweh. Here the succession of titles expresses divine protection and gives little reason to distinguish their bearers. [@abd, 4:922–923]
