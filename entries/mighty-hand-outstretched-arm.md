## Overview

A mighty hand and an outstretched arm are images of God's power in the biblical account of Israel's departure from Egypt. They occur together especially in Deuteronomy and in texts that share its language. Some scholars compare the expressions with Egyptian royal titles and images that celebrate the force of Pharaoh's arm. Letters from Late Bronze Age Canaan show that this language could be used in diplomatic correspondence as well. The biblical formula can describe deliverance, the public reputation of Israel's God, or judgment turned against Israel itself.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian royal texts celebrate the power of the king's arm, sometimes using *ḫpš*, a term associated with a strong arm. The Middle Kingdom Tale of Sinuhe praises Senusret I in language about the force of his arm. Royal art also portrays a king holding a weapon high above a defeated enemy. The raised arm in the [[smiting-the-enemy-motif|smiting scene]] makes royal victory visible and places the body of the king at the center of that portrayal.

The [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] show how widely this imagery could travel. Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem writes in the Akkadian of international correspondence that the strong arm of the Egyptian king put him in his position (EA 286:9–13 and 288:7–15). He also uses the image of the king's arm for Egyptian power beyond Jerusalem (EA 288:34–47). These examples place the expression in both an Egyptian royal setting and a wider culture of Near Eastern diplomacy.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Exod 6:6 God promises to redeem Israel with an outstretched arm and judgments. The victory song in Exod 15:6 gives God's right hand the power to shatter the enemy. Both passages associate parts of the body with acts of deliverance and judgment. Through this imagery the narrative presents the contest between Israel's God and Pharaoh as a contest over which power can prevail.

Deut 4:34, 5:15, and 26:8 use both hand and arm in recalling deliverance from Egypt. The expression supports different arguments in each passage. It illustrates the uniqueness of God's acts in Deut 4, grounds the Sabbath command in the experience of emancipation in Deut 5, and forms part of the confession spoken over first produce in Deut 26. The formula recurs in the prayer of Jer 32:21 and in the praise of Ps 136:12. In 1 Kgs 8:42, the foreigner hears of God's great name, mighty hand, and outstretched arm.

## Interpretive Significance

One proposed reading is that the biblical authors applied to God language that an Egyptian audience might associate with its king. Its use against Pharaoh would turn royal rhetoric against the ruler it ordinarily honored. Ezek 30:21–25 develops a related reversal explicitly when God breaks Pharaoh's arm and strengthens the arms of Babylon's king. Ezek 20:33–34 directs the paired formula toward Israel, as God vows to assert royal authority over the people and gather them under judgment.

The distribution of the full expression points especially to Deuteronomic and related biblical writing. The age of the comparative Egyptian material and the date at which a biblical author used the imagery are separate questions. The royal and diplomatic parallels help explain how the image could communicate formidable power, while the surviving evidence leaves the precise history of borrowing uncertain.
