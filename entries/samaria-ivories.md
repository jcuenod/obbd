## Overview

The Samaria ivories are hundreds of carved pieces found near the royal palace of the northern kingdom of Israel, many of them during excavations in the 1930s. Most were fittings for furniture or other costly objects. They are generally assigned to the ninth and eighth centuries BCE, though later debris contained many of the pieces and limits precise dating. Their international styles and Egyptian-derived designs illuminate the material culture behind references to ivory houses, beds and luxury goods in Kings, Amos and Ezekiel.

## Archaeological Evidence

Excavations at [[samaria|Samaria]] between 1908 and 1935 found ivory plaques, strips and fragments in the vicinity of the palace. Many pieces were separated from their original settings before burial. Holes, carved recesses and traces of inlay show how craftsmen combined materials. Some pieces bear traces of gold or colored inserts. They were probably mounted on wooden furniture or fittings, often no longer preserved. The surviving material thus documents expensive interiors even where the placement of particular pieces is unknown. [@neaehl, 4:1305–1307] [@anep, figs. 129–130, 566]

The carvings include winged sphinxes, lotus and palmette designs, a woman framed by a window and the infant Horus. Comparisons with the collections from [[nimrud|Nimrud]] and Arslan Tash have led specialists to distinguish several Levantine workshop traditions. The terms Phoenician, North Syrian and South Syrian often refer to styles inferred from the objects. The location of workshops and the possibility of carving at Samaria remain debated. Tribute or booty could carry western ivories to Assyria. A place where ivories were recovered need not be their place of manufacture. [@anep, figs. 129–130, 566] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 180–202, 249–263]

Egyptian-derived garments, crowns and figures show that artisans drew on a visual repertoire that circulated widely. Their presence in the capital indicates what courtly display could include. A plaque representing Horus supplies evidence for familiarity with the image, while its use by a household gives only limited grounds for reconstructing worship. [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 180–202, 249–263]

## In the Bible

1 Kgs 22:39 credits [[ahab|Ahab]] with an ivory house. Furniture and fittings provide an archaeological explanation of what a designation of that kind could signify. The lost original contexts prevent assigning particular plaques to his reign. Amos 3:15 foresees the destruction of ivory houses, and Amos 6:4 pictures affluent people on ivory beds. Both passages use costly decoration to make elite wealth visible. Ps 45:8 (MT 45:9) uses ivory palaces as part of a royal wedding scene. Ezek 27:6, 15 treats ivory as an inlay and an item of trade in its lament over Tyre. [@abd, 5:915–920] [@neaehl, 4:1305–1307]

The winged figures and ornamental plants also offer comparisons for the [[cherubim|cherubim]], palms and flowers carved in wood in 1 Kgs 6:29, 32, 35. The woman at the window is sometimes compared with [[jezebel|Jezebel]] appearing at a window in 2 Kgs 9:30. The resemblance shows how a scene could evoke a familiar pose. The image type circulated widely and cannot identify a historical sitter. [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 180–202, 249–263] [@anep, figs. 129–130, 566]
