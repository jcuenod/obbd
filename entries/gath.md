## Overview

Gath was one of the principal cities of the [[philistines|Philistines]] and is widely identified with Tell eṣ-Ṣafi on the western edge of the Shephelah. Its Bronze Age settlement preceded the Iron Age city familiar from the biblical narratives about David. Excavations have revealed a large Iron Age settlement, remains of buildings used for worship, and evidence of a siege and destruction in the late ninth century BCE. A pottery sherd from the site also gives a glimpse of early writing and personal names in Philistia.

## Location and Identification

The tell stands near the opening of the Elah Valley onto the coastal plain. This position gave it access to farmland and to routes into the foothills. The place name Gimti in the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]], associated with the ruler Shuwardata, is often identified with Gath. The proposal fits the Bronze Age occupation at the site, although similar names complicate a precise location for every mention of Gimti.

Several sites have been proposed as the Gath of the Hebrew Bible. The excavations begun by Aren Maeir in 1996 established the exceptional extent and importance of the Iron Age settlement at Tell eṣ-Ṣafi. Its location and tenth- and ninth-century prominence, followed by a major ninth-century destruction, are important reasons for the identification.

## Archaeological History

The site preserves Bronze Age occupation and an early Iron Age settlement with locally made pottery that includes forms and decoration related to Aegean traditions. Later material combines local developments with features shared across the region. Excavators have uncovered cult buildings and a stone altar with two horns in the lower city. Together the finds show the scale of the settlement and the range of activities once carried on there.

A large trench cut into the bedrock and a parallel bank survive outside the city. Excavators interpret them as parts of a siege system associated with a widespread destruction in the late ninth century BCE, often dated around 830 BCE. They connect the destruction with the capture of Gath by [[hazael|Hazael of Damascus]] in 2 Kgs 12:17 (MT 12:18). Occupation continued on a reduced scale after the event.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 11:22 groups Gath with Gaza and Ashdod as places where Anakim remain. In 1 Sam 5:8–9, Gath receives the ark from Ashdod and suffers an outbreak of disease. Goliath also comes from Gath (1 Sam 17:4). An inscribed sherd from late Iron I or early Iron II preserves letters read as two names, *ʾlwt* and *wlt*. The excavators compared the names with non-Semitic names from the eastern Mediterranean and with the linguistic background of the biblical name Goliath. The letters preserve distinct names, and the proposed linguistic connection remains a matter of comparison.

David flees to Achish of Gath and feigns madness there in 1 Sam 21:10–15 (MT 21:11–16). He later receives Ziklag from Achish and operates from there as his subordinate (1 Sam 27:2–12). Gittites who have come from Gath appear among David's supporters in 2 Sam 15:18. These accounts portray both Gath's political authority in its region and the movement of people between Gath and Judah.

Amos 6:2 invokes Gath with other prominent cities, while the Philistine lists in Amos 1:6–8 and Zeph 2:4 omit it. This distribution fits a city that remained known after it lost its former standing. Mic 1:10 also uses Gath in a lament over towns in and along the approaches to Judah. Each passage uses the city for its own rhetorical purpose.
