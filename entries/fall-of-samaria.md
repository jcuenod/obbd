## Overview

Samaria, capital of the northern kingdom of Israel, fell to Assyria in the late eighth century BCE. Biblical accounts name Shalmaneser V at the beginning of the siege, while Sargon II's inscriptions credit him with victory and the deportation and resettlement of people. A Babylonian chronicle also records a campaign by Shalmaneser against a place generally identified as Samaria. Scholars differ over whether the sources reflect one conquest or two stages of Assyrian intervention. The fall ended the northern monarchy, but many people remained in the region and lived under Assyrian rule.

## Sources

A [[babylonian-chronicles|Babylonian chronicle]] says that [[shalmaneser-v|Shalmaneser V]] ravaged Šamaraʾin, a name generally understood as Samaria. [[sargon-ii-inscriptions|Inscriptions of Sargon II]] also claim a victory at Samaria. In versions of his account, Sargon reports removing more than twenty-seven thousand people, incorporating chariot personnel into his service, and resettling the area. The figures differ slightly among copies. They are royal claims and give no total for the population that had lived in the region [@abc] [@cos2].

Assyrian administrative texts provide independent evidence that Samarian personnel were serving in chariot units. Documents associated with places such as Gozan also attest people with names suggestive of Israelite or Judean origin. A name can indicate a cultural connection, although it rarely establishes the circumstances in which an individual or a family moved. The evidence is consistent with relocation to the Assyrian heartland and other provinces.

## Reconstruction

A widely followed reconstruction, associated with Hayim Tadmor, places a first conquest under Shalmaneser about 722 BCE and a second intervention under [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] in 720 BCE, when he suppressed revolts in the west. Some scholars alter the date of the first capture or place parts of the deportation at different stages. Others argue for a single decisive capture, explaining the conflicting credit through the transition between kings or the claims of Assyrian royal inscriptions. No surviving source gives an explicit account of two captures in sequence.

The surviving evidence places people from other parts of the empire in Samaria and its region, alongside people who remained. Parts of the countryside continued to be occupied despite considerable disruption and changes in settlement. The image of ten tribes disappearing in their entirety goes beyond what these sources establish. [[deportation|Assyrian deportation]] changed the region's population without removing all its inhabitants.

## Biblical Accounts

2 Kgs 17:1–6 describes [[hoshea|Hoshea]] withholding tribute, an Assyrian siege lasting three years, and the deportation of Israelites. 2 Kgs 18:9–12 again associates the beginning of the siege with Shalmaneser and links the event to the reign of Hezekiah of Judah. The reports give several destinations for deportees, including Gozan on the Habur. Their wording leaves some questions about responsibility for the final capture open.

2 Kgs 17:24–41 describes the placement of deportees from other regions in the towns of Samaria and interprets the resulting religious practices. Sargon's claims corroborate resettlement as a policy, but the account in Kings has its own explanation for how the newcomers worshiped. Its connection with the much later [[samaritan-origins|Samaritan community]] is a separate historical question. In 2 Kgs 17:7–23, the fall itself becomes the occasion for an account of Israel's disobedience.
