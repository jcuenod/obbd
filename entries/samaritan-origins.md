## Overview

The Samaritan community developed around the worship of the God of Israel at Mount Gerizim. Its origins lie in the shared history of northern Israel and Judah and in the political and religious changes that followed Assyrian conquest. Inscriptions and excavated remains show the importance of Gerizim by the Persian and Hellenistic periods. Biblical and later accounts preserve disputes over ancestry and the proper place of worship. Much recent scholarship understands the emergence of distinct Jewish and Samaritan communities as a gradual process.

## Sources

The term Samarian can designate an inhabitant of the city or province of [[samaria|Samaria]]. Samaritan usually designates the religious community associated with [[mount-gerizim|Mount Gerizim]]. The categories can overlap, and it can be difficult to use the later religious name for the earlier inhabitants. The Elephantine correspondence names Delaiah and Shelemiah, sons of [[sanballat|Sanballat]] the governor. Yahwistic names in these records and in the [[samaria-papyri|Wadi ed-Daliyeh papyri]] show the endurance of northern traditions associated with Israel's God. Votive inscriptions at Gerizim attest worship there. Two Greek inscriptions on Delos identify their dedicators as Israelites who made offerings to holy Gerizim, showing the importance of the sanctuary to people abroad. [@tad, A4.7–A4.9] [@abd]

## History

The Assyrian [[deportation|deportations]] removed part of the northern population and introduced groups from elsewhere. Many inhabitants remained. In the Persian period Samaria had its own governor, and the relationship with Judah included rivalry as well as shared religious traditions. Excavator Yitzhak Magen dated the first monumental sanctuary on Gerizim to the fifth century BCE. Some scholars place its beginnings later. The sanctuary was expanded in the Hellenistic era and destroyed by John Hyrcanus I, conventionally around 111–110 BCE. [@neaehl] [@abd]

Josephus (*Antiquities* 11.302–347) connects the building of the sanctuary with a Sanballat under Darius III and Alexander and with his son-in-law Manasseh. Its similarity to the marriage conflict in Nehemiah has prompted proposals that Josephus combined traditions from different times. The excavator's Persian dating also makes a foundation under Alexander difficult. Arguments for a gradual separation emphasize that sanctuary, politics and communal identity need not have changed together. [@abd]

## In the Bible

2 Kgs 17:24–41 portrays resettled peoples combining devotion to YHWH with service to their own gods. The passage explains the north's fate from a polemical standpoint. Ezra 4:1–5 narrates a refusal to share in rebuilding the Jerusalem temple, and Ezra 4:10 associates resettlement with Osnappar, commonly identified with Ashurbanipal. Neh 2:10, 19 identifies Sanballat as an opponent, while Neh 13:28 connects his family with the Jerusalem high priesthood through marriage. These accounts attest rivalry and contact. [@abd]

Sir 50:25–26 disparages the people of Shechem. 2 Macc 6:2 records the renaming of the Gerizim sanctuary for Zeus alongside an equivalent action at Jerusalem. In John 4:9, 20, a Samaritan woman acknowledges communal distance and invokes worship on the mountain. Luke 10:33 places a Samaritan in the role of the compassionate neighbor. Both narratives use the recognizable distinction between Samaritan and Jewish communities for their own purposes. [@abd]
