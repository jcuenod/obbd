## Overview

Caesarea Maritima was a harbor city founded by Herod the Great on the Mediterranean coast of Judaea in the late first century BCE. Its artificial harbor served a planned city with a palace, theater, and temple honoring Rome and Augustus. Under direct Roman rule the city housed the governors of Judaea. In Acts it is associated with Philip, the Roman centurion Cornelius, and Paul's detention and hearings. Excavations give context to these episodes through the remains of public buildings and an inscription naming Pontius Pilate.

## Location and Identification

Herod developed the earlier settlement known as Straton's Tower, on the coast between modern Haifa and Tel Aviv. Scholars generally date the main construction to around 22–10 BCE. Its position gave it access to coastal routes and seaborne traffic. This coastal Caesarea is distinct from Caesarea Philippi inland [@neaehl].

## Archaeological History

[[herod-the-great|Herod]] named the harbor Sebastos, the Greek equivalent of Augustus. Underwater excavation has identified breakwaters built with hydraulic concrete, which hardens in contact with water. On land, the temple of Roma, the goddess personifying Rome, and Augustus occupied a platform overlooking the harbor. The theater, aqueducts, and remains of a palace on a promontory attest to the scale of the settlement [@neaehl].

Caesarea became the seat of the Roman governors after direct administration began in 6 CE. The promontory palace is a plausible location for the governor's residence mentioned in Acts, though the text does not identify a specific building. The [[pilate-stone|Pilate inscription]], found reused in the theater in 1961, names Pontius Pilate as prefect of Judaea and refers to a Tiberieum, a monument or building named for Tiberius. Its original location is uncertain.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Acts 8:40 places Philip in Caesarea, where Acts 21:8–14 later portrays him hosting Paul and his companions. Acts 10:1–48 associates the city with Cornelius, a centurion of the Italian Cohort who receives Peter and hears his message. The military and administrative setting helps explain that portrayal. Luke 3:1 names Pontius Pilate among the rulers of the time. The inscription establishes his title independently, while Acts uses the later governors to frame a series of hearings. The port also serves as a link between Paul’s travels and the Roman administration. In Acts 23:23–35 Roman soldiers take Paul to Felix, who orders him held in Herod's headquarters. Acts 25:1–26:32 presents further proceedings under Festus and Agrippa II.

Acts 12:19–23 depicts Agrippa I's death after public honors at Caesarea. [[josephus|Josephus]] also associates his death with a public appearance there and describes an illness that lasts several days. The accounts differ in detail and explanation. Josephus also describes conflict among the city's inhabitants in the events leading to the revolt against Rome in 66 CE. Caesarea was later a center of Christian learning associated with Origen and Eusebius.
