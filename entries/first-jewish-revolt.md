## Overview

The first major Judean revolt against Rome began in 66 CE and led to the destruction of Jerusalem's temple in 70 CE. Resistance continued at several strongholds, ending at Masada in 73 or 74 CE. Josephus, who fought in Galilee before joining the Roman side, gives the fullest surviving account. Archaeology and Roman sources both supplement his narrative. The revolt transformed worship and political life in Judea. It also supplies historical context for New Testament passages about Jerusalem's siege and the loss of the temple, although the precise relationship of those passages to the events remains debated.

## Sources

[[josephus|Josephus]] describes the revolt in the Jewish War and discusses his own role as a Galilean commander in his Life. His account gives extensive detail, but his position changed during the war, and he wrote under the patronage of the victorious ruling family. Roman histories by Tacitus and Cassius Dio supply different perspectives, often with greater interest in the conquest and the success of the Flavian emperors.

Evidence from Yodefat, also called Jotapata, and Gamla places some of Josephus's accounts in identifiable archaeological settings. The Burnt House in Jerusalem and fallen masonry beside the temple platform attest the destruction in the city. Roman siege works survive at [[masada|Masada]]. Coins struck by the [[jewish-revolt-coinage|rebel authorities]] and the [[judaea-capta-coins|Roman victors]], and images on the [[arch-of-titus|Arch of Titus]], express the claims of the opposing sides through different symbols [@neaehl].

## Reconstruction

Tensions included disputes between communities in Caesarea, dissatisfaction with Roman government under the procurator Gessius Florus, and the rejection of sacrifice on behalf of the emperor. In 66 CE the rebels defeated a force commanded by Cestius Gallus. Vespasian then campaigned in Galilee in 67 CE, bringing towns including Yodefat and Gamla under Roman control. Josephus attributes important decisions to identifiable leaders and factions whose authority was not constant throughout the conflict.

Rival groups in Jerusalem included the Zealots and the forces around John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora. The Sicarii, whose name refers to their use of concealed daggers, were a distinct group associated with Masada. The sources support distinctions among these groups. Treating all opponents of Rome as members of a single Zealot movement obscures their varied aims and rivalries.

Vespasian became emperor in 69 CE, and Titus led the siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE. After months of fighting, the [[herods-temple|temple]] burned in the summer and Roman forces took the city. The final resistance at Masada ended in 73 or 74 CE, depending on the chronology used. The destruction ended sacrificial worship at the Jerusalem temple, and Rome imposed a tax on Jews for the temple of Jupiter in Rome. Later rabbinic tradition remembers Yavneh as a center of study and leadership, while the Sadducees cease to appear as a distinct group in later evidence.

## Biblical Accounts

Mark 13:1–2 and 13:14–20 predict the temple's destruction and speak of a crisis requiring flight. Luke 19:41–44 and 21:20–24 portray Jerusalem surrounded by enemies and its people killed or taken captive. Many scholars date Mark near 70 CE and Matthew and Luke later. The dating draws on several strands of evidence, and the specificity of Luke's language has been read either as knowledge of the siege or as the use of familiar prophetic descriptions.

Some scholars see an allusion to Rome's destruction of Jerusalem in Matt 22:7. Others understand the burning of a city as a conventional element of the story, and the proposed reference remains debated. The link between Rev 11:1–2 and the events of 70 CE is likewise disputed. Eusebius later reported that the Jerusalem church fled to Pella. The historical basis for that account is uncertain.
