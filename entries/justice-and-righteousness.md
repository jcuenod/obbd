## Overview

Justice and righteousness are paired ideals for rulers and communities in the Hebrew Bible. Comparable expressions in Mesopotamia and Egypt describe the ruler’s obligation to establish fair order and protect vulnerable people. A king could enact that obligation by judging fairly or, in some settings, by releasing people from debts or forced service. Biblical poetry describes justice and righteousness as the basis of God’s rule. Prophets apply the same ideals to kings and institutions, using them to expose violence, abuse and neglect of the poor.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Akkadian texts pair *kittum*, meaning truth or reliable order, with *mīšarum*, meaning equity or rectitude. Related Hebrew vocabulary includes *mêšārîm*, meaning equity. In Babylon the prologue of the [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] portrays the gods as commissioning the king to make justice prevail and to keep the strong from oppressing the weak. The epilogue presents Hammurabi as a king of justice. Royal edicts such as the [[edict-of-ammi-saduqa|Edict of Ammi-saduqa]] show that the language of equity could have concrete effects, including cancellation of specified debts and release from debt bondage. [@roth-law] [@cos2]

Mesopotamian texts also personify Truth and Equity as divine figures associated with [[shamash|Shamash]], the sun god and guardian of justice. Egyptian [[maat|maat]], the order expressed through truth and right conduct, could be personified and represented in association with the royal throne. These traditions made the work of judging and governing part of a larger account of divine order. Their terminology and institutions differed according to place and period. [@ddd] [@cos1] [@ael]

## In the Hebrew Bible

2 Sam 8:15 makes justice and righteousness a summary of David’s rule. Ps 72:1–4 asks that the king judge the people fairly and defend the poor. Isa 9:7 (MT 9:6) envisages a Davidic throne sustained by justice and righteousness. Isa 11:3–5 likewise describes a ruler who gives the poor a fair hearing. Jer 22:15–16 remembers [[josiah|Josiah]] for judging the cause of poor and needy people, making conduct toward the vulnerable a test of knowing the Lord. [@abd]

Ps 89:14 (MT 89:15) and Ps 97:2 describe righteousness and justice as the foundation of God’s throne. Ps 85:10–13 (MT 85:11–14) personifies righteousness and other virtues, and Ps 99:4 celebrates equity established by the deity. The ancient images of virtues surrounding a god or throne give a regional setting for the poetry. The psalms do not name a company of separate divine figures equivalent to the Mesopotamian pair. [@ddd] [@abd]

## Interpretive Significance

Moshe Weinfeld emphasized concrete social measures such as royal proclamations of release. Jer 34:8–10 gives a point of comparison in Zedekiah’s proclamation to free enslaved Judeans, although its terms and covenant setting differ from those of Babylonian edicts. Judicial fairness and care for vulnerable people are also prominent where no release is envisaged. In Isa 5:7 Hebrew wordplay links expected justice and righteousness with the bloodshed and outcry actually produced. Amos 5:24 imagines justice and righteousness as continually flowing water. The prophets apply comparable royal ideals to a whole society and use them to evaluate rulers and subjects. [@roth-law] [@abd]
