## Overview

The calendars used in ancient Israel and Judah linked months to the moon and festivals to the agricultural year. Biblical texts date events by month numbers, older West Semitic names, and, in later books, names of Babylonian origin. These different forms reflect the region's contacts and changing conventions of writing. The Bible can place the beginning of the year in spring while referring to autumn harvest as the turn of the year. Some Jewish writings from the Second Temple period also describe a calendar of 364 days.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

A lunisolar calendar follows the moon for its months and keeps the year in step with the seasons. Twelve lunar months are shorter than a solar year, so another month is inserted when necessary. This adjustment is called intercalation. Babylonian calendars could insert a second Adar or Elul, and the pattern became more regular in the Persian period. The [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine papyri]], some of which give dates in both Egyptian and Babylonian forms, provide evidence for how these reckonings were used together [@cohen-cultic-calendars, 3–12] [@tad, A4.1].

The agricultural cycle could also serve to describe a period of time. The [[gezer-calendar|Gezer calendar]] names periods by work such as harvesting and sowing. Its sequence illustrates how tasks could organize a year, though the purpose of the inscription is debated. It does not list the official month names used in dated documents [@ahituv-echoes, 252–256].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 13:4 and Deut 16:1 name Abib in connection with the departure from Egypt and Passover. Abib refers to grain at the stage of ripening ears. Kings names Ziv, Bul, and Ethanim, terms that belong to an older West Semitic naming tradition with Phoenician parallels. Ziv is identified as the second month in 1 Kgs 6:1, Bul as the eighth in 1 Kgs 6:38, and Ethanim as the seventh in 1 Kgs 8:2. The month number can serve as a gloss for readers familiar with a different convention.

Later books use names of Babylonian origin. Esth 3:7 calls the first month Nisan and the twelfth Adar. Neh 1:1 and 2:1 use Chislev and Nisan, and Zech 1:7 explains Shebat as the eleventh month. The old custom of numbering months remained intelligible alongside the new names.

## Interpretive Significance

Exod 12:2 assigns the first month to the season of Passover. Exod 23:16 and 34:22 connect the [[festivals-agricultural|feast of ingathering]] with the year's end or turn. The evidence allows a spring reckoning for numbered months and an autumn reckoning related to harvest. Scholars debate whether both kinds of calendar operated concurrently or the passages reflect different periods and settings.

The [[first-enoch|Astronomical Book of Enoch]], [[jubilees|Jubilees]], and some Qumran texts preserve calendars organized around a year of 364 days. That total is exactly fifty-two weeks, so dates recur on the same weekdays in the scheme. It differs from the lunar months of other Jewish calendars and illustrates calendrical diversity in the period.
