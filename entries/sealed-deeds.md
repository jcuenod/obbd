## Overview

Sealed deeds recorded transactions and allowed the parties or authorities to verify their authenticity. Ancient Near Eastern and Roman-period examples preserve several methods, including clay envelopes and rolls with protected and accessible texts. Jeremiah's purchase of a field is the fullest biblical description of such a transaction. Its combination of payment, witnesses, sealing and storage shows how documentary procedures could protect a claim over time. Biblical writers also use the sealed document to evoke messages that are secured, inaccessible or reserved for a future occasion.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In Mesopotamia a legal tablet could be encased in a clay envelope. The outside could carry a copy or summary and the seals of people involved. A dispute could require breaking the envelope to consult the protected text. Some Aramaic papyri from [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine]] were rolled, tied and sealed as single documents. The fourth-century BCE [[samaria-papyri|Wadi ed-Daliyeh deeds]] and their clay sealings provide other examples of legal authentication on papyrus. [@cos3] [@tad] [@avigad-sass-wsss]

Some documents in the Babatha archive from the second century CE present two versions, normally on a single sheet. The inner version was rolled and tied, while the outer one could be read without opening it. The exact duplication and means of fastening varied. This combination offered both routine access and a more securely preserved record. [@abd]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Jer 32:6–15 describes a land purchase witnessed by others during the threat to Jerusalem. The prophet weighs silver, signs and seals the deed, and directs Baruch to put the sealed and open material in an earthen vessel. Archaeological examples from Elephantine and the Judean Desert show that pottery vessels could protect papyri or scrolls. Jer 32:44 anticipates renewed buying and sealing of deeds as a sign of restored ordinary life. [@abd] [@tad]

Gen 23:16–20 describes the purchase of a burial property in public. The negotiation and transfer have been compared with Neo-Babylonian dialogue documents, which record the description and sale of property in a stylized exchange. The episode does not mention a deed. Ruth 4:7–11 instead confirms redemption in the presence of witnesses through a sandal ceremony. Deut 24:1, 3 requires a written certificate of divorce, an example of written evidence for a change in legal status. [@abd] [@cos3]

## Interpretive Significance

Jeremiah's wording has been read as describing a single double document or two distinct copies. Both reconstructions explain the need to preserve the terms and leave them available for consultation. Isa 8:16 uses binding and sealing for testimony and instruction. In Isa 29:11–12 the contents of a sealed document are inaccessible. Dan 12:4, 9 imagines revelations preserved until the end. Rev 5:1–5 portrays a scroll written inside and on the back and closed with seven seals. The image has invited comparisons with double deeds and with an *opisthograph*, a manuscript written on both sides. Its role in the vision depends especially on who is worthy to open it. [@abd]
