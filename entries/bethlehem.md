## Overview

Bethlehem is a town in the hills of Judah south of Jerusalem. A clay seal impression from Iron Age Jerusalem probably names it, although relatively little from that period has been excavated in Bethlehem itself. The Hebrew Bible connects the town with Ruth and David, and both Matthew and Luke place Jesus' birth there. The Church of the Nativity preserves a Christian tradition associated with a cave at the site since antiquity. Bethlehem's significance in biblical literature rests especially on its place in the remembered history of David and in expectations about a future ruler.

## Location and Identification

The Bethlehem associated with David is generally identified with modern Beit Lahm, south of Jerusalem. Ruth 1:1–2 calls it Bethlehem in Judah and describes the family as Ephrathites, a name also associated with Bethlehem in Mic 5:2 (MT 5:1, using the Hebrew numbering). Gen 35:19 places Rachel's burial on the way to Ephrath and identifies it with Bethlehem. The location remains debated because 1 Sam 10:2 places Rachel's tomb on the border of Benjamin. Some scholars understand the identification with Bethlehem in Genesis as a later explanation. [@abd, 1:712–715]

## Archaeological History

A small clay [[seals-and-bullae|seal impression]] found in Jerusalem and published in 2012 is commonly read as naming Bethlehem. The fragmentary text belongs to a type conventionally associated with taxation or deliveries to the royal administration. If the reading is correct, it supplies an Iron Age attestation of the place from outside the Bible. A proposed identification in the much earlier [[amarna-letters|Amarna correspondence]] remains disputed, as the place named in the letter has also been identified with other towns. [@moran-amarna, EA 290]

Justin Martyr mentions a cave associated with Jesus' birth in the second century CE. A church was dedicated at the traditional site in the fourth century, and the present building largely reflects a sixth-century rebuilding. Parts of the earlier pavement survive. The church and cave document the early development of Christian pilgrimage and commemoration. [@neaehl, 1:203–209]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The story of Ruth begins with a family leaving Bethlehem in a famine and has Naomi and Ruth reach the town at the barley harvest (Ruth 1:1–2, 19–22). In 1 Sam 16:1–13, Samuel visits Bethlehem and anoints [[david|David]] there. Mic 5:2 associates a ruler of Israel with Bethlehem Ephrathah. The combination of traditions about David and the expectation of another ruler from his town gives Bethlehem its importance in later writings.

Matthew places the birth in Bethlehem during the reign of [[herod-the-great|Herod the Great]] and connects the town explicitly with the prophetic text (Matt 2:1–16). Luke has Joseph travel to the city of David because of a [[census-of-quirinius|registration]] (Luke 2:4–15). John 7:42 presents Bethlehem as an expected origin of the Messiah. Scholars differ over whether the two birth accounts preserve a historical birthplace or locate the birth there because of its association with David and the prophetic expectation. The evidence from the town does not decide between those readings. A suggestion that Jesus was born instead at the Bethlehem in Zebulun (Josh 19:15) remains a minority proposal.
