## Overview

Philo of Alexandria was a Jewish interpreter and author who lived from the late first century BCE into the first century CE. His Greek works explain the laws and stories of Moses using forms of argument and interpretation familiar in Greek philosophy. He also describes conflicts involving the Jewish community of Alexandria under Roman rule. His writings provide a contemporary Jewish context for New Testament language about the divine Word, the visible and heavenly sanctuaries, and the administration of Judaea.

## Manuscripts and Date

Philo's works survive chiefly in later Greek manuscripts, with some works also preserved in ancient translations. Their composition belongs to the Roman imperial period, even where the subjects are biblical events. The account of the embassy to Gaius belongs to the final decades of Philo's life, when he represented Alexandrian Jews in a delegation to the emperor.

## Contents

Philo wrote series of commentaries on the books of Moses, accounts of biblical figures, philosophical works, and accounts of contemporary affairs. He sometimes reads details of biblical stories and laws as images of the world or the soul. In *On the Life of Moses*, for example, he associates the seven lamps of the sanctuary with the celestial bodies. This is his interpretation of furnishings such as those prescribed in Exod 25:31–40 [@philo-life-of-moses, 2.74–76, 102–105].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In *On the Confusion of Tongues* Philo describes the *logos*, or divine Word, as firstborn and uses language of God's image [@philo-confusion, 146–147]. This provides a Jewish comparison for the Word in John 1:1–18 and the language of image and firstborn in Col 1:15–17. The New Testament texts apply their language to Jesus and make claims that should be read within their own arguments. The comparison itself gives no evidence that their authors were using Philo's books.

Philo's account of Moses seeing incorporeal patterns for the sanctuary, that is, patterns without physical substance, can be compared with the distinction between earthly service and heavenly pattern in Heb 8:1–5. In a different kind of writing, Philo's *Embassy to Gaius* presents [[pontius-pilate|Pontius Pilate]] as the Roman official in Judaea in an episode about shields set up in Jerusalem. It attests his governorship independently of the trial narrative in John 18:28–19:16 [@philo-embassy, 299–305].
