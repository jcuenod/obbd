## Overview

The statue of Udjahorresnet bears an Egyptian account of an official who served under the Persian rulers Cambyses and Darius I. He describes helping define Cambyses's Egyptian royal titles, intervening at the sanctuary of Neith in Sais, and restoring an institution known as the House of Life. The statue records how a member of the Egyptian elite represented his service under new rulers. It also provides a comparison for the ways Ezra and Nehemiah portray Persian kings granting support to local officials and sanctuaries.

## Description and Provenance

The stone statue, now in the Vatican Museums, depicts Udjahorresnet holding a miniature shrine with an image of Osiris. Archaeologists use the term *naophorous*, meaning shrine-bearing, for this form. Hieroglyphic inscriptions on the statue recount his titles, his service, and his actions for the sanctuary. The modern history of the statue is documented more securely than its original placement.

## Contents

Udjahorresnet gives his titles, including chief physician, and describes his service under Cambyses after the Persian conquest. He claims to have explained the importance of Sais and its goddess and helped supply Cambyses with Egyptian royal titulary. He also presents himself as obtaining the removal of foreigners from the Neith sanctuary and arranging the restoration of its practices.

Under [[darius-i|Darius I]], he says he was sent back to Egypt to restore the House of Life, an institution associated with learning and temple traditions. The text emphasizes personnel and support for renewed work. It represents Darius using someone familiar with Egyptian religious and administrative institutions. Read alongside the separate Demotic Chronicle tradition about Darius ordering Egyptian laws to be collected, it illustrates several forms royal intervention could take.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ezra 7:12–26 presents Ezra as authorized by the Persian king to support worship and administer law. Neh 1:11–2:8 describes a courtier obtaining royal backing for work in Jerusalem. Ezra 6:3–12 gives a different set of notices about royal support for the Jerusalem sanctuary. Udjahorresnet's career shows that an official familiar with a local cult and administration could serve as an intermediary under Persian rule. The Egyptian and Judean cases operate in different settings and address different tasks.

## Scholarly Issues

The inscription gives a favorable portrait of Cambyses's concern for the sanctuary and of Udjahorresnet's role. Herodotus's depiction of Cambyses is much less favorable. The difference reflects the aims and circumstances of the writers. Scholars debate whether Udjahorresnet's account is best understood as cooperation with conquerors, an attempt to protect Egyptian institutions, or both. The texts also bear on the debate over [[persian-imperial-authorization|Persian imperial authorization]], while leaving open whether the measures reflected a uniform imperial policy.
