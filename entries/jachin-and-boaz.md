## Overview

Jachin and Boaz were two bronze pillars described at the entrance to Solomon’s temple in Jerusalem. The account in Kings names their Tyrian maker, gives their dimensions and portrays elaborate capitals decorated with plant forms and rows of pomegranates. Chronicles and the accounts of the temple’s destruction preserve somewhat different dimensions. Excavated temple entrances and clay models supply comparisons for the placement and visual effect of paired pillars. The precise function of the Jerusalem pair and the significance of their names remain uncertain.

## Archaeological Evidence

Temples elsewhere in the Iron Age Levant sometimes had a pair of columns at the entrance. At ʿAin Dara in northern Syria, two surviving bases indicate a columned porch. Temples at Tell Tayinat also provide examples of columns framing an entrance. Some Iron Age clay shrine models show columns alongside a doorway. These examples demonstrate a recognizable architectural choice, and their forms may reflect structural or ornamental uses depending on the building. [@mazar-archaeology] [@neaehl] [@anep]

Herodotus describes two conspicuous pillars in a sanctuary of the deity he calls Heracles at Tyre, generally identified there as Melqart. His account is centuries later than the reign assigned to Solomon. It is helpful as evidence for the association of prominent pillars with a Phoenician sanctuary, especially alongside the biblical account’s mention of a Tyrian artisan. No surviving pillar can be securely associated with the Jerusalem pair. The separate description and naming of these objects has led many scholars to reconstruct them as freestanding. [@abd] [@hurowitz-exalted-house]

## In the Bible

In 1 Kgs 7:15–22 Hiram casts the pillars and sets them at the porch. Each shaft is eighteen cubits high, with capitals, latticework and rows of pomegranates. A cubit was a unit of length roughly comparable to the forearm. The inventory in 1 Kgs 7:41–42 again distinguishes the shafts, capitals and networks. In 2 Chr 3:15–17 the pillars measure thirty-five cubits and are set before the temple. Jer 52:17, 20–23 describes the bronze carried away by the Babylonians and gives eighteen cubits for a hollow shaft with walls four fingers thick. Its five-cubit capital differs from the three-cubit capital in 2 Kgs 25:13, 16–17. These differences remain part of the textual record. [@abd] [@hurowitz-exalted-house]

The name Jachin can be read as “he establishes,” while a proposed reading of Boaz is “in strength.” One suggestion is that the names reflect the first words of royal or dynastic declarations. The accounts do not preserve such inscriptions, and neither the purpose nor the linguistic explanation of the names is secure. The texts concerning Joash’s enthronement (2 Kgs 11:14) and Josiah’s covenant (2 Kgs 23:3) give a pillar a ceremonial role as the king’s station. They leave its identity unspecified. In Ezek 40:49 the visionary temple also has a pair of pillars flanking its porch. These references support the importance of marked places at a sanctuary entrance for both architectural design and public ritual. [@abd] [@hurowitz-exalted-house]
