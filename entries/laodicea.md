## Overview

Laodicea on the Lycus was a prosperous city in Phrygia, a region of what is now western Turkey. Founded under Greek-speaking rule in the third century BCE, it became a commercial center in the Roman province of Asia. Colossians places its Christian community in close contact with those of nearby Colossae and Hierapolis, and Revelation addresses it in the last of its seven messages to churches. The city's wealth and water supply provide possible background for some of Revelation's images, although not every proposed local reference can be demonstrated.

## Location and Identification

The ruins lie near Eskihisar, north of modern Denizli, on a plateau above the Lycus valley [@abd, 4:229–230]. The site is securely identified: its position agrees with ancient descriptions, and inscriptions name the Laodiceans. Hierapolis lay to the north, near modern Pamukkale, and Colossae lay farther up the Lycus valley to the east. The valley gave the three communities a shared regional setting, while Laodicea's location at a crossroads helped it develop as a trading center.

## Archaeological History

The Roman historian Tacitus reports that Laodicea recovered from an earthquake in 60 CE using its own resources rather than imperial aid. His account shows that the city could finance its recovery; it does not say the citizens turned down a formal offer from Rome. The geographer Strabo describes the district's soft black wool as a source of income and records a medical school in the region. Laodicea also handled substantial financial transactions, although its commercial wealth need not be attributed to banking alone.

Turkish excavations directed by Celal Şimşek began in 2003 and have uncovered streets, public buildings, and parts of the water system. Stone pipes and distribution tanks show how spring water was brought into the city and distributed. The excavations also exposed a later church building, discovered in 2010 and dated to the fourth century CE. That building attests to Christianity in a much later phase of the city; it does not identify where the first-century congregation met.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Col 2:1, Paul describes his struggle for the people of Laodicea as well as for his immediate readers. Col 4:12–16 names Epaphras and describes his concern for the communities at Colossae, Laodicea, and Hierapolis. It also greets Nympha and the congregation that met in her home, though the manuscripts vary in the form of the name and the accompanying pronoun. The instructions to exchange letters show contact between the congregations, but the identity of the letter coming from Laodicea is uncertain.

In Rev 3:14–22, the final message rebukes a church whose confidence in its wealth masks its need. The speaker urges it to obtain purified gold to relieve its poverty, white garments to cover its nakedness, and a remedy to restore its sight (Rev 3:17–18). Laodicea's resources make the claim of self-sufficiency especially pointed, and its black wool trade provides a possible contrast with the white clothing. Regional medical activity makes the eye remedy a plausible local association, but it does not establish that the author had a particular Laodicean product in mind.

In Rev 3:15–16, the speaker prefers either hot or cold to lukewarm. A popular interpretation, associated with M. J. S. Rudwick and E. M. B. Green, connects the contrast with the hot springs of Hierapolis, the presumed cold water of Colossae, and Laodicea's piped supply. The pipes establish how water reached Laodicea, not how it tasted or what temperature it was when served. Craig Koester instead relates the image to ordinary dining, where both hot and cold drinks can be welcome and a lukewarm drink can be unpleasant. The image works on that reading without requiring readers to identify three separate water supplies.
