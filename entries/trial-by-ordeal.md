## Overview

A trial by ordeal referred a disputed matter to divine judgment through a physical test. Mesopotamian laws and letters describe river ordeals in cases where ordinary evidence could not settle the dispute. Numbers 5 gives a different procedure for a woman suspected of adultery. It uses a priest, an oath, and water associated with a conditional curse. The comparison raises a question about what counts as an ordeal and about how ancient law understood divine action when witnesses or other evidence were unavailable.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] §2, an accusation of sorcery that cannot be proved is submitted to the river. Its outcome is understood as divine judgment and has legal consequences for the accuser and the accused. In §§131–132, a wife accused by her husband without direct evidence swears an oath, while public accusation can entail recourse to the river. The identity of the accuser and the kind of evidence thus affect the procedure.

The [[middle-assyrian-laws|Middle Assyrian Laws]] include river ordeals in certain cases, including some allegations of sexual wrongdoing. Letters from [[mari-letters|Mari]] refer to ordeals at Hīt, an important place on the Euphrates. These cases show that the ordeal could form part of legal practice. Other procedures, including oaths without a physical test, also referred disputed matters to divine judgment.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Num 5:11–31 prescribes a rite when a man suspects his wife of infidelity and the case lacks a witness. A priest administers an oath with a conditional curse and gives the woman water connected with the written curse. The procedure anticipates bodily consequences if she is guilty and release from the effects of the curse if she is innocent. The difficult language of Num 5:21–22, 27 concerns the abdomen and “thigh.” Proposed understandings include loss of fertility or other reproductive harm. The text does not support a precise medical diagnosis.

Exod 22:7–11 (MT 22:6–10) refers disputes over entrusted goods to proceedings before God or to an oath. 1 Kgs 8:31–32 imagines people swearing at the altar and asks God to judge the parties. In Exod 32:20 Moses makes the Israelites drink water containing the powdered golden calf. The act is sometimes compared with Numbers, though Exodus does not present it as a test to determine any person's guilt.

## Interpretive Significance

One reading treats the rite in Numbers as an ordeal because the woman's body is expected to disclose God's judgment. Another emphasizes the conditional curse and the distinction between this rite and a hazardous river test. Some interpreters, including Jacob Milgrom, argue that the priestly procedure could restrain private retaliation by a suspicious husband. The law nevertheless places a burden on the woman and assigns no comparable procedure to a husband in the same chapter.

Some scholars also distinguish stages of composition within the law, partly because instructions or effects recur. Later discussion in the Mishnah describes the procedure and traditions about discontinuing it (m. Soṭah 9:9). The material illustrates the diversity of practices that referred a dispute to a deity when ordinary evidence was missing.
