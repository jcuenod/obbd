## Overview

Herod the Great was Rome’s client king in Judea. Appointed in 40 BCE, he captured Jerusalem in 37 BCE and ruled until his death, conventionally dated to 4 BCE. He rebuilt the Jerusalem temple and commissioned harbors, palaces, and fortresses that reshaped the region. Matthew sets Jesus’ birth and the flight to Egypt within his reign, and Luke places the opening of its infancy narrative in his days. Josephus and archaeology offer extensive evidence for Herod’s rule and building, while some events reported by Matthew remain independently unattested.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Josephus gives the fullest surviving narrative, especially in *Jewish Antiquities* 14–17 and *Jewish War* 1. Herod came from an elite family of Idumean origin. His father Antipater gained influence working with Rome and the [[hasmoneans|Hasmonean]] leadership. Roman backing made Herod’s kingship possible, but he secured Jerusalem only after defeating the last reigning Hasmonean, Antigonus. The sources show him navigating changes of patron at Rome and maintaining authority at home through patronage, military power, and coercion.

Herod’s projects give material evidence on a scale unusual for a ruler of Judea. His expansion of the [[herods-temple|Jerusalem temple]] transformed its precinct. At [[caesarea-maritima|Caesarea Maritima]] an artificial harbor and a city dedicated to Augustus expressed his connection with the empire. Palaces and fortifications such as [[masada|Masada]] and Herodium reveal how royal living quarters and defensible sites could be combined. Herod’s bronze coins supply contemporary evidence for the royal title and the symbols of his court.

## Biblical Portrayal

Matthew 2:1–12 presents Herod as alarmed by the visitors who ask about a newly born king of the Jews. Matthew 2:16–18 assigns him responsibility for the killing of children in Bethlehem and connects their deaths with Jeremiah’s image of Rachel weeping. The episode also belongs to a narrative with echoes of Moses and Pharaoh. Matthew 2:19–22 associates the family’s return from Egypt with Herod’s death and identifies fear of his successor Archelaus as a reason to settle in Galilee.

Luke 1:5 uses Herod’s reign to place the story of Zechariah and Elizabeth before John’s birth. The forty-six years mentioned in John 2:20 reflect the continuing scale and duration of Herod’s temple project. Acts 23:35 mentions Herod’s praetorium at Caesarea as the place where the Roman governor orders Paul to be held. The reuse of the complex shows how a client king’s building could serve a later provincial administration.

## Historical Assessment

Josephus places a lunar eclipse shortly before Herod’s death and Passover soon after it (*Ant.* 17.167, 213). The sequence, synchronisms with Roman events, and the chronology of his successors usually lead historians to date his death to 4 BCE. A date of 1 BCE remains a minority alternative. On the conventional reconstruction Matthew’s setting places Jesus’ birth before 4 BCE, without settling a precise year.

Josephus reports executions within Herod’s own family. His surviving account contains no episode corresponding to the killing of children at Bethlehem. Herod’s record of violence makes Matthew’s characterization intelligible, but independent evidence for the event is lacking. Josephus identifies Herodium as Herod’s burial place. Ehud Netzer’s excavations uncovered a monumental mausoleum on its slope, which he identified as the king’s tomb. Other archaeologists contest the identification on architectural and spatial grounds.
