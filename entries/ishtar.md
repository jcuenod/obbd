## Overview

Ishtar was a Mesopotamian goddess associated with sexual desire, warfare and the planet Venus. She was identified with the Sumerian goddess Inanna and received worship in distinct local forms across many centuries. Uruk was an important center in southern Mesopotamia, while the goddesses called Ishtar of Nineveh and Ishtar of Arbela were prominent in Neo-Assyrian royal worship. Her poetry and oracles provide comparisons for biblical love poetry and expressions of divine reassurance. Her identification with Jeremiah’s Queen of Heaven remains debated.

## Sources and Iconography

Inanna appears in Sumerian hymns, narratives and love songs. In the [[descent-of-inanna-ishtar|Descent of Inanna]], the goddess enters the realm of the dead and eventually returns, while Dumuzi, called Tammuz in later traditions, is taken as her substitute. The Akkadian descent narrative retells the story with significant differences. Sumerian poems also portray Inanna actively pursuing a lover and acquiring power. The range of the sources reflects traditions that do not reduce her role to a single function. [@cos1, 1.108, 1.160, 1.169] [@ddd, 450–451, 452–456, 829–834]

An eight-pointed star can represent Ishtar in her association with Venus, visible as morning or evening star. Lions are associated with her in various media, including the Processional Way near the [[ishtar-gate|Ishtar Gate]] at Babylon. Assyrian texts distinguish the manifestations at Nineveh and Arbela, both of which are closely associated with kingship. It is uncertain whether every image proposed as Ishtar depicts her. The [[burney-relief|Burney Relief]], for example, has no identifying inscription. [@anep, figs. 760–763] [@ddd, 452–456] [@saa9, no. 1.1]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Jer 7:18 and 44:17–19 depict offerings of cakes and drink to the [[queen-of-heaven|Queen of Heaven]]. Ishtar bore a similar title, and cakes are attested in her worship. These comparisons make her an important candidate, while other goddesses and local forms of worship have also been proposed. Ezek 8:14 describes women lamenting [[tammuz|Tammuz]] at the temple in Jerusalem. His link with Ishtar in Mesopotamian literature helps explain the wider ritual background. The verse does not name the goddess or establish her participation in the rites at Jerusalem. [@ddd, 678–680] [@abd, 3:521–523]

In Neo-Assyrian oracles for Esarhaddon, Ishtar of Arbela reassures the king and promises to deal with his enemies. Isa 41:8–13 similarly puts reassurance and promises of aid in the mouth of the deity, addressing Israel as the Lord’s servant. The association of reassurance with imminent divine help makes the [[neo-assyrian-prophecies|Assyrian oracles]] an important formal comparison. Song 4:9–12 shares some expressions of intimacy with [[sumerian-love-songs|Sumerian songs]] about Inanna and Dumuzi, but proposals about cultic origins for the Song remain disputed. [@saa9, no. 1] [@nissinen-prophets, 101–110] [@ddd, 452–456]

## Scholarly Issues

Ishtar and the West Semitic [[astarte|Astarte]] have related names and some comparable associations. Their cults and literary roles differ according to region and time. Interaction and identification between goddesses occurred, but a proposed link needs evidence in its specific setting. The name Esther in Esth 2:7 has been linked to Ishtar and, alternatively, to a Persian word for star. Its origin remains disputed. [@ddd, 109–114, 452–456] [@abd, 3:521–523]

Herodotus’s account of compulsory intercourse in a Babylonian sanctuary has often shaped descriptions of Ishtar’s cult. Modern work has questioned both the Greek account’s historical reliability and the interpretation of Mesopotamian terms commonly translated as temple prostitutes. The existence of such a general institution in Ishtar’s temples has no secure support in the contemporary evidence. [@ddd, 452–456] [@abd, 3:521–523]
