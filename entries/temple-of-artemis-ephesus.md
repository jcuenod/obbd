## Overview

The temple of Artemis at Ephesus, called the Artemision, was a major sanctuary of western Asia Minor and one of the ancient Seven Wonders. It belonged to a city with influential civic institutions, busy trade, and provincial honors under Rome. Inscriptions attest ceremonies that linked the sanctuary to the city, and the theater remains visible at the site. Acts uses these institutions in its account of opposition to Paul from artisans. The material evidence establishes the setting and local titles more clearly than it establishes the particulars of the episode.

## Location and Identification

Ancient Ephesus lies near modern Selçuk in western Türkiye. The Artemision stood outside the more densely built Roman city. The major theater was within the city and accommodated public assemblies as well as performances. Ancient inscriptions recording a processional route between the temple and city helped guide John Turtle Wood’s search. He located the temple site in 1869, and subsequent excavations documented several phases.

## Archaeological History

An Archaic temple was built on a site of earlier worship. Ancient sources associate its destruction by fire with 356 BCE, and a new monumental temple followed. Architectural and sculptural fragments attest its scale and decoration. Images of the Ephesian Artemis frequently show ornaments on the figure’s front. These have been interpreted as breasts, eggs, sacrificial objects, or adornments. The image alone cannot fix their original meaning, and labeling the cult simply as fertility worship obscures its civic importance.

The foundation inscription of Gaius Vibius Salutaris, dated to 104 CE, regulates processions that carried sacred images between the sanctuary and city, including the theater. It shows how patrons, civic officials, and the cult worked together in the early second century. Ephesus also participated prominently in the [[imperial-cult|imperial cult]]. The city’s temple honors could concern different sanctuaries and need to be identified from each inscription’s wording and date.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Acts 19:23–41 presents artisans whose business depends on producing objects associated with Artemis. In 19:24 Demetrius makes silver shrines, objects that make sense within a local economy of dedication and religious display. The account moves to the theater, mentions Asiarchs in 19:31, and gives the town clerk a role in calming the crowd. The title *neōkoros* in 19:35 evokes Ephesus’s status as guardian of the sanctuary. Civic inscriptions supply evidence for such offices and honors. The archaeological theater and epigraphic evidence do not independently record Demetrius’s assembly or its outcome.

Acts 19:19 also portrays the public destruction of books associated with magic, a distinct episode in the city’s religious setting. First Corinthians 15:32 uses fighting beasts at Ephesus as an image whose literal or figurative force is debated. The Artemis sanctuary does not resolve that question. Rev 2:1–7 addresses the Christian community at Ephesus within a network of cities in Asia. The site helps readers picture the communal setting without locating every activity of the congregation within the Artemis cult.
