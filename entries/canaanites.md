## Overview

Canaan and Canaanite are names used in ancient texts for parts of the Levant and some of their inhabitants. In the Late Bronze Age, Egyptian rule linked many towns of the southern Levant, each with its own leaders and local traditions. The Hebrew Bible often uses Canaanites for inhabitants of the land whom Israel encounters. Archaeology also shows substantial continuity from Bronze Age communities into many early Iron Age settlements. These sources help explain both the historical connections between the populations and the sharp divisions drawn by some biblical texts.

## Sources

Forms of the name Canaan or Canaanite appear in texts from Mari, Alalakh, and Egypt. The inscription of Idrimi, ruler of Alalakh, recalls a stay in a town in the land of Canaan. The [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] give evidence for the place of Canaan within the territory controlled by Egypt in the Late Bronze Age. A list from [[ugarit|Ugarit]] labels one merchant as a Canaanite alongside people designated by other places of origin. The geographic scope of the name differs across contexts, so Ugarit need not have counted itself as part of Canaan [@cos2] [@moran-amarna].

## History

Late Bronze Age Canaan included towns ruled by local dynasties under Egyptian oversight. Archaeology reveals regional traditions in ceramics, architecture, and ritual, together with trade and exchange across the eastern Mediterranean. Scholars use “Canaanite” for some of these material patterns, but objects alone cannot establish an individual's ancestry or the name a community used for itself [@mazar-archaeology].

The growth of hill-country settlements in the early Iron Age is central to the study of the [[emergence-of-israel|emergence of Israel]]. Continuities in pottery and the close relationship of Hebrew to other regional languages support the view that much of early Israel developed from local populations. New settlements and ways of life also took shape during the transition. A study by Lily Agranat-Tamir and colleagues found considerable shared ancestry among samples from different Bronze Age southern Levantine sites, alongside admixture and variation. This evidence concerns population history and cannot assign biblical identities to the individuals sampled.

## In the Bible

Gen 10:15–19 maps Canaan and its neighbors through genealogy and a description of territory. Deut 7:1–5 lists Canaanites as one of seven peoples and commands separation from them. The list defines the religious and political boundary in the book's account of taking possession of the land. Judg 1:27–33 presents a different emphasis, describing communities that remained among the tribes or lived alongside them. Such lists and narratives describe the past according to the aims of their texts.

The name has a wider range elsewhere in the Bible. Isa 19:18 imagines people in Egypt speaking “the language of Canaan” (ESV) and pledging loyalty to Israel's God. In Prov 31:24 a word for Canaanite is used to mean a merchant. Matt 15:22 calls the woman from the region of Tyre and Sidon a Canaanite, while Mark 7:26 identifies her as Syrophoenician. The differing labels show how an older biblical designation could be retained for a later setting.
