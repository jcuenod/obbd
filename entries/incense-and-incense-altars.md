## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern worshippers burned aromatics in temples, at local shrines, and in other ritual settings. Some offerings used imported frankincense. In Judah, residues on two small stone altars from the shrine at Arad provide unusually direct evidence for what was burned. Biblical texts describe incense on a fixed golden altar and in portable pans, and they use its rising smoke as an image of prayer. The evidence helps distinguish the furnishings, substances, and different ritual purposes gathered under the term incense.

## Archaeological Evidence

Small stone altars occur at Iron Age sites, including some with projecting corners. Their scale is compatible with small offerings, and the precise contents cannot usually be established from shape alone. Burners and offering stands offer additional evidence for early cultic use. These finds qualify the older view associated with Julius Wellhausen that incense offerings of the kind described in biblical ritual belong only to a late stage of Israelite worship [@neaehl] [@keel-symbolism].

Two small limestone altars came from the shrine at [[arad|Arad]]. Analysis published in 2020 found chemical markers of frankincense on the larger altar and markers of cannabis on the smaller. The authors interpreted these as residues of substances burned during the shrine's use. The analysis identifies the compounds without settling the ritual purpose of either substance. The frankincense gives physical evidence for long-distance [[incense-trade|trade in aromatics]] reaching a Judahite sanctuary.

## In the Bible

Exod 30:1–10 places a gold-covered incense altar near the inner curtain of the [[tabernacle]]. Exod 30:34–38 specifies a sacred mixture conventionally translated with terms such as stacte, onycha, galbanum, and frankincense. Some of the ingredient identifications remain uncertain. The passage reserves the mixture for sacred use. The altar has projections like the [[horned-altars|horns]] on some excavated altars, although a similarity of form cannot identify an excavated altar with the tabernacle furnishing.

A portable censer, or pan for burning incense, serves other purposes. In Lev 10:1–2 Nadab and Abihu bring unauthorized fire. In Lev 16:12–13 an incense cloud covers the ark's cover during the Day of Atonement. In Num 16:46–48 (MT 17:11–13) Aaron brings a censer into the assembly during a plague. 2 Chr 26:16–21 depicts King Uzziah taking on a duty reserved for priests and suffering a skin disease. Luke 1:9–11 likewise places incense within the assigned duties of priests, with Zechariah selected by lot.

Jer 6:20 associates frankincense with [[sheba|Sheba]] and condemns offerings that fail to please God. Jer 44:15–25 mentions offerings to the [[queen-of-heaven|Queen of Heaven]], evidence for the diversity of places and aims of burning offerings. Lev 26:30 refers to objects called *ḥammānîm* that are to be destroyed. Related terms in later Aramaic inscriptions have encouraged the translation “incense altars,” although other meanings have been proposed.

In Ps 141:2 incense serves as a comparison for prayer. Mal 1:11 connects offering and the honor of God's name among the nations. These texts show how the sensory action could be used to express prayer and recognition even when the details of the equipment are unspecified.
