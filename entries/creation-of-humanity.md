## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern accounts describe the creation of humans through materials and actions drawn from everyday life. Mesopotamian stories mix clay with something from a god and assign humans work previously done by the gods. Egyptian texts and images describe humans arising from a creator's tears or being shaped on a potter's wheel. In Genesis, God forms a human from the soil and creates humanity in the divine image. These comparisons clarify two questions the stories address in different ways. They ask what human beings are made from and what responsibilities they receive in the world.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the Old Babylonian [[atrahasis|Atrahasis]] epic, lesser gods rebel against their labor. The gods have a deity killed, and the birth goddess mixes his flesh and blood with clay to make humans. The new beings are to take over the gods' work. In the Babylonian poem [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]], the blood of Kingu, who had led Tiamat's forces, supplies the material for humanity. This act likewise relieves the gods of service previously required of them [@lambert-millard-atrahasis] [@lambert-bcm].

Egyptian images sometimes show the creator god [[khnum|Khnum]] shaping human figures on a potter's wheel. Other accounts associate the formation of humans with the creator's tears, through a play on similar-sounding Egyptian words for people and tears. The Instruction for Merikare calls people the creator's cattle and speaks of them as his images. These metaphors express care, likeness, and dependence in different ways [@ael].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 2:7, YHWH forms the human from dust of the ground and breathes life into him. The account connects human life with the ground and with divine breath. The human receives responsibility for tending and keeping the garden (Gen 2:15). The Mesopotamian texts also connect creation with labor, but they describe the transfer of toil from a group of gods to human beings.

In Gen 1:26–28, humanity is made in the [[image-of-god|image and likeness of God]] and receives authority over other creatures. Ps 8:3–8 (MT 8:4–9) likewise portrays people as given honor and authority within a world much larger than themselves. The themes of service and rule both matter to the biblical portrayal of human vocation. They are developed through different scenes and images.

The metaphor of being shaped also appears apart from stories of beginnings. Job asks why the God who fashioned him like clay would now bring him back to dust (Job 10:8–9). Isa 64:8 (MT 64:7) addresses God as maker and represents the people as clay in a potter's hands. The same act can thus be used to question divine treatment or appeal to a creator's care.

## Interpretive Significance

In the Sumerian text [[enki-and-ninhursag|Enki and Ninhursag]], the goddess Ninti is born in connection with healing Enki's rib. The Sumerian word for rib resembles the word for life, and some scholars have proposed that this wordplay lies behind the formation of the woman from the man's side in Gen 2:21–22. The connection is contested. The two stories give the rib different roles, and the Sumerian wordplay does not carry over into the Hebrew vocabulary. The similarity leaves the historical relationship uncertain.
