## Overview

Jehoiachin succeeded Jehoiakim as king of Judah and surrendered Jerusalem to Nebuchadnezzar II after a reign of about three months. He was taken to Babylon in 597 BCE. An independent Babylonian chronicle dates the capture of the city, and palace records name Jehoiachin and members of his household among recipients of oil. The biblical accounts remember both the circumstances of his removal and his later release from confinement. His continuing royal title and the use of his exile as a point of chronological reference illustrate his importance after his reign ended.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[nebuchadnezzar-chronicle|Babylonian Chronicle]] dates the capture of the city of Judah to the second day of Adar in Nebuchadnezzar’s seventh year, conventionally converted to 16 March 597 BCE. It reports that the king of Babylon captured the king, chose a replacement and took substantial tribute. It identifies Judah but leaves both Judahite kings unnamed. Matching the episode with 2 Kgs 24:8–17 identifies the captive as Jehoiachin and his successor as Zedekiah. [@abc, 102] [@glassner-chronicles, no. 24, 226–229]

The [[jehoiachin-ration-tablets|palace ration records]] give forms of Jehoiachin’s name and describe him as king of Judah. They include an allocation to five royal sons. One relevant text dates to the thirteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar, or 592/591 BCE. These records place Jehoiachin in the Babylonian system of provision long before the biblical report of his release. [@anet, 308]

## Biblical Portrayal

2 Kgs 24:8–17 recounts the siege, Jehoiachin’s surrender with his household and the removal of officials, soldiers and craftspeople. In the concluding account of 2 Kgs 25:27–30, Nebuchadnezzar’s successor [[amel-marduk|Amel-Marduk]], called Evil-merodach in the Hebrew Bible, raises Jehoiachin’s standing, releases him from prison and provides him with a regular allowance. Jer 52:31–34 gives a parallel account with a difference in the day of the release. This change of status is generally placed around 562/561 BCE. [@abd, 3:661–662]

Jer 22:24–30 portrays Coniah, another form of the king’s name, as a royal signet that the Lord will remove. Its declaration that he is to be recorded as childless is accompanied by a statement that none of his offspring will prosper on David’s throne. 1 Chr 3:17–18 lists his descendants under the name Jeconiah. The genealogical and administrative evidence therefore fits a reading of Jeremiah’s judgment as the loss of a reigning line in Judah. [@anet, 308] [@abd, 3:661–662]

## Historical Assessment

2 Kgs 24:12 counts the capture in Nebuchadnezzar’s eighth year. One way to account for the difference is that Babylonian scribes treated the partial year after a ruler’s accession separately, while the biblical total can include it as the first year. The use of the exile as a dating point in Ezek 1:2 indicates how memorable the deportation remained. Some infer continued loyalty to Jehoiachin as the legitimate king. The date formula by itself cannot establish that political position. Stamp impressions naming Eliakim as a subordinate of Yokan were once connected to Jehoiachin, but their much earlier archaeological context undermines the identification. [@abc, 102] [@abd, 3:661–662] [@avigad-sass-wsss, 244, no. 663]
