## Overview

A standing stone could mark a grave, commemorate a person, witness an agreement, or stand in a sanctuary. The Hebrew word *maṣṣēbâ* designates an erected pillar, and its plural is *maṣṣēbôt*. Excavated examples are known at Bronze and Iron Age sites in the Levant and in desert sanctuaries. Biblical texts assign different values to these objects. Jacob erects a pillar at Bethel, while Deuteronomy prohibits erecting one in worship. The uses and settings of the stones help explain why the objects could receive such different treatment.

## Archaeological Evidence

At [[gezer|Gezer]], a row of large standing stones is generally associated with Middle Bronze Age worship, although its exact purpose is uncertain. A Late Bronze Age shrine at [[hazor|Hazor]] contained a row of smaller stelae. The Iron Age sanctuary at [[arad|Arad]] had at least one standing stone, with debate about whether another also belonged to its original arrangement. In open-air ritual sites in the Negev and Sinai, standing stones occur in groups with other installations. Their shape alone seldom identifies a god or a ritual [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology].

Tryggve Mettinger used the term “material aniconism” for the representation of a deity through an object that lacks a human or animal figure. Some standing stones in sanctuaries may have served in this way. Others could commemorate an individual, mark a boundary, or give a material focus to an agreement. Context, an inscription, or the surrounding installations can help distinguish these functions [@mettinger-no-graven-image].

## In the Bible

In Gen 28:18–22 and 35:14, Jacob puts up a pillar at [[bethel|Bethel]] and anoints it or pours a libation. Gen 31:45–52 uses a pillar and stone heap as witnesses and markers of an agreement. In Gen 35:20 a pillar marks Rachel's grave, and in 2 Sam 18:18 Absalom erects one to preserve his name. Exod 24:4 pairs an altar with twelve pillars corresponding to the tribes of Israel. These uses show how an erected stone could preserve a claim or memory.

Josh 24:26–27 describes a great stone as a witness, and Josh 4:20 describes the erection of twelve stones at Gilgal. The Hebrew passages refer to the stones with a more general word rather than *maṣṣēbâ*. They offer functional comparisons without establishing the meaning of the specific term. In Isa 19:19, an altar and a pillar to YHWH in Egypt express recognition of him in foreign territory.

Exod 23:24 commands the destruction of the pillars connected with the gods of the land. Deut 16:22 prohibits setting up a pillar, and 1 Kgs 14:23 and 2 Kgs 18:4 connect pillars with criticized worship or royal reform. Hos 3:4 includes a pillar among institutions that Israel will lose. The changing evaluations reflect different literary settings and claims about appropriate worship. They should be read alongside the archaeological evidence for regional variation in sacred and commemorative practices.
