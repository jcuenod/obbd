## Overview

The Great Isaiah Scroll is a Hebrew manuscript found in Cave 1 near Qumran. It was copied in the late Second Temple period, probably in the second century BCE, and preserves nearly the whole book of Isaiah. Its writing spans fifty-four columns on sheets of prepared animal skin. The scroll agrees broadly with the later traditional Hebrew text but also has differences in spelling, wording, and smaller textual details. It gives readers direct evidence both for the transmission of Isaiah and for the choices made by ancient scribes.

## Description and Provenance

The scroll is one of the first [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] recovered near [[qumran|Qumran]] in the late 1940s. Its designation, 1QIsaᵃ, identifies the cave, the biblical book, and the manuscript within the group. Seventeen sheets make up the scroll. It is held at the Shrine of the Book in Jerusalem, and the Israel Museum makes photographs available in a digital edition. Paleography, which compares the forms of written letters, commonly places its copying in the late second century BCE, often around 125 BCE. Radiocarbon measurements provide broader evidence for the date of the writing material.

## Contents

The manuscript contains the text of all sixty-six chapters of Isaiah in the familiar order. Modern chapter numbers were added long after its production. Its scribes frequently used letters to mark vowel sounds, a practice more common in this scroll than in the later Masoretic Text, the traditional Hebrew form of the book. The smaller, fragmentary 1QIsaᵇ often agrees more closely with the Masoretic text. The two finds show that copies of the same work could circulate with different spelling practices and readings. ## Significance for Biblical Studies

At Isa 53:11, the Great Isaiah Scroll includes a Hebrew word meaning light in the statement about the servant seeing after suffering. The later Masoretic wording lacks that word. The Greek [[septuagint|Septuagint]] also has light in this verse, although its rendering differs in other respects. The NIV renders the phrase “see the light of life.” The manuscript gives direct Hebrew evidence for a reading that can otherwise be considered through ancient translation and other manuscripts.

Differences of this sort need to be weighed individually. Many involve common spelling or grammar and make little difference to translation. Others affect the sense of a sentence. The overall similarity and the existence of particular variants are both important for understanding the history of the book.

## Scholarly Issues

The manuscript contains alterations by more than one hand. A conspicuous break after what is now Isaiah 33 has been discussed as evidence for a change of scribe, a division in an exemplar, or both. A digital study of handwriting found systematic differences across the midpoint and concluded that two scribes probably copied the scroll in closely related styles. That result speaks to the production of this manuscript. It cannot independently settle questions about the composition of Isaiah itself.
