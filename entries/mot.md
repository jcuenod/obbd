## Overview

Mot is the Ugaritic word for death and the name of the powerful figure who confronts the storm god Baal in the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]]. The poem imagines Death as a being with an enormous mouth and an appetite that can consume even a god. Biblical Hebrew uses the related word *māwet* for death and sometimes gives Death a voice, an appetite, or a capacity to act. The Ugaritic poems give these biblical images a regional setting and make visible the force of texts that picture God putting an end to Death.

## Sources and Iconography

In the Ugaritic poem, Mot is associated with the world below and described as beloved of El. The account of his confrontation with [[baal|Baal]] spans KTU 1.4–1.6. Mot threatens to consume Baal, and the poem gives him such a vast mouth that his lips reach from the earth to the heavens (KTU 1.5 II). The imagery emphasizes his capacity to swallow. Parts of the scene are damaged, so details of Baal's death and return depend on reconstruction.

Baal's absence prompts mourning, and [[anat|Anat]] finds his body. She subsequently attacks Mot with actions expressed through the vocabulary of harvesting and processing grain. Mot reappears and again fights Baal until a warning delivered by the sun goddess changes the outcome. The poem dramatizes the competition between powerful divine figures. No image can securely be identified as Mot.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Hab 2:5 compares insatiable greed with Death, whose appetite is as wide as [[sheol-and-the-netherworld|Sheol]]. Isa 5:14 pictures Sheol opening its throat wide to receive people going down to it. Isa 25:8 reverses the familiar direction of the image. God swallows Death forever. Another set of images gives Death a place in political or social language. Isa 28:15, 18 speaks of a covenant with Death and an agreement with Sheol, while Ps 49:14 (MT 49:15) portrays Death as a shepherd for those who go down to Sheol.

Jer 9:21 (MT 9:20) pictures Death coming up through windows and reaching people indoors. Job 18:13–14 refers to the firstborn of Death and the king of terrors in its depiction of the wicked person's end. Song 8:6 says love is as strong as Death and passion as fierce as Sheol. This use depends on the unyielding force of Death, even in a poem concerned with love.

Hos 13:14 addresses Death and Sheol in a verse whose questions can be read in different ways in context. In 1 Cor 15:54–55 Paul takes up language from both Isaiah and Hosea as an affirmation that Death's power is overcome. The threatening force of the personification makes the declaration of victory more vivid.

## Scholarly Issues

Comparisons with Mot help explain the personifications and appetite imagery in biblical poetry. The Hebrew texts discussed here contain no direct evidence of an Israelite cult of Mot. Some interpreters have connected Death's entry through windows in Jeremiah with the window in Baal's palace. Others have compared Near Eastern stories involving dangerous beings such as the Mesopotamian Lamaštu. Jeremiah supplies no specific marker that settles the source of the image.
