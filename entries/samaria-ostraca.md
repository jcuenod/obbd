## Overview

The Samaria ostraca are about a hundred potsherds bearing brief Hebrew inscriptions, found in 1910 in the royal compound of Samaria. Most legible records concern deliveries of wine or oil and give a year, a place or district and a person's name. They are commonly assigned to the early eighth century BCE, often to the reign of Jeroboam II. They provide evidence for the administration of Israel's capital, for northern Hebrew and for the geography behind some biblical lists of Manassite clans.

## Description and Provenance

The Harvard expedition found the sherds in the area it called the Ostraca House at [[samaria|Samaria]]. The inscriptions were written in ink on pottery. Some are preserved in little more than fragments. The deposits help place the collection at the royal acropolis, but its exact relationship to the building and the moment at which the sherds were collected are debated. The regnal dates in the texts give the numbers 9, 10 and 15 without naming the king. [@neaehl] [@cos4, 4.18]

## Contents

A typical notice gives a year, a place of origin, a person and a commodity. Notices often mention a jar of wine or oil. The wine can be described as aged and the oil as washed or refined, though the exact processing implied by those terms is debated. The compact formula shows what the scribes needed to identify deliveries. It provides little explicit information about the terms of ownership or why a delivery was due. The personal names include northern forms ending in *-yw* and names incorporating [[baal|Baal]]. [@cos4, 4.18A–O] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Names such as Helek, Abiezer and Shemida occur as places or district designations in the records and have counterparts in the Manassite lists of Num 26:30–33 and Josh 17:2–3. The overlap gives evidence that some names preserved in biblical [[genealogies|genealogy]] correspond to units in the Samaria highlands. Hos 2:16–17 (MT 2:18–19) criticizes the use of Baal language. The personal names show that this element remained intelligible in northern Israel alongside Yahwistic names. The distribution of the forms alone cannot establish the worship practices of those named. [@ahituv-echoes] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]

1 Sam 8:14–15 describes royal appropriation and redistribution of land and produce. Amos 6:4–6 condemns the consumption of wine and fine oils by the elite. The ostraca give an administrative comparison for both passages. 2 Kgs 14:23–29 sets Jeroboam II in Samaria during a long reign, one reason his rule is often proposed as a setting for the documents. [@abd] [@cos4, 4.18]

## Scholarly Issues

The sherds have been read as tax receipts, deliveries from royal estates to officials, and records of produce assigned to landholders attached to the court. The personal names and the directions of transfer can be understood differently. Some chronologies place all the dates in one reign, while others assign the records from year 15 to a different ruler. Handwriting analysis of a group of legible sherds has suggested two scribes and supports recording at the capital for that subset. The conclusion does not settle the institutional purpose or date of the whole collection. [@cos4, 4.18] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]
