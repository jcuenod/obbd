## Overview

In Exodus, Pharaoh repeatedly refuses to release the Israelites despite signs, plagues, and promises of relief. English versions often describe this as a hardening of his heart, but the Hebrew uses verbs for making it strong, heavy, or hard. Sometimes Pharaoh acts, sometimes the heart is described in a particular condition, and sometimes God acts. The heavy heart has prompted comparison with Egyptian scenes of judgment after death. Exodus also develops its own wordplay between the weight of Pharaoh’s heart and the glory God gains over him.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

New Kingdom copies of the Egyptian [[book-of-the-dead|Book of the Dead]] depict the deceased before a divine tribunal. In the Papyrus of Ani, Anubis attends a balance with the deceased’s heart in one pan and a feather signifying [[maat|maat]], truth and right order, in the other. Thoth records the outcome. The creature Ammit embodies the threatened consequence if the judgment goes against the deceased. The image gives moral weight a physical form. Spells could also ask the heart to refrain from testifying against its owner, as in Book of the Dead spell 30B.

In the Egyptian scene the heart is evidence in a judgment after death. The heart of Pharaoh in Exodus figures in a series of decisions during his lifetime. The narrative never depicts an Egyptian balance, a feather, or a tribunal. The comparison turns on the meaning the audience might attach to a heart described as heavy in a story about an Egyptian ruler. The images and texts show that the Egyptians imagined weighing hearts. A further argument is needed to show that the Hebrew idiom evokes that specific practice.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Exod 4:21 YHWH announces that he will strengthen Pharaoh’s heart, using the root *ḥzq*. Exod 7:3 uses *qšh*, the root associated with making something hard. In 7:14 the heart is heavy, from *kbd*. Pharaoh makes his heart heavy after the frogs in Exod 8:15 (MT 8:11), and he does so again after the hail in 9:34. In Exod 10:1 YHWH says that he has made the hearts of Pharaoh and his officials heavy. Attending to the verb and its subject at each point preserves distinctions that a single rendering such as “harden” obscures.

The root *kbd* also supplies the word for God gaining glory over Pharaoh and his forces at the sea in Exod 14:4, 17. The verbal forms differ, but the repetition joins the king’s resistance to the display of divine glory. Proverbs 21:2 uses the idiom of weighing hearts to explain the difference between people’s judgment of their own conduct and YHWH’s appraisal. That verse directly combines judgment and weighing, while Exodus uses the language of weight to describe the king’s resistance.

## Interpretive Significance

Some source-critical accounts associate much of the *ḥzq* language with the Priestly material and the *kbd* language with a different strand, often identified as J. The distribution is uneven and reconstructions differ. The final narrative juxtaposes the forms and assigns agency to both Pharaoh and YHWH. That literary pattern accounts for much of the interpretive significance regardless of the passages’ compositional history.

The suggested Egyptian allusion fits the narrative’s interest in the limits of [[egyptian-kingship|Egyptian royal power]]. Its plausibility depends partly on the weight the interpreter gives the Egyptian setting and partly on whether a conventional Hebrew expression needs a more specific explanation. The shared Hebrew root that connects Pharaoh’s disposition and YHWH’s glory is visible even if the allusion to funerary judgment remains open.
