## Overview

The new moon marked the beginning of a month in ancient Israel and was an occasion for offerings, trumpet blasts, and gatherings. The Hebrew word *ḥōdeš* can refer to the new-moon observance or to the month it begins. Biblical texts associate the day with a royal meal, a pause in trade, and worship alongside the Sabbath. Especially in agricultural societies using lunar months, marking a new month helped coordinate communal time. Records from Mesopotamia and Ugarit show a comparable association between lunar phases and ritual observance.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian ritual calendars assigned offerings and festivals to particular days of the lunar month. Occasions called *eššēšu* are attested at several stages in the cycle, including near the beginning of a month. Details varied by city, temple, and period. Observation of the moon and the preparation of calendars had political as well as religious significance.

The ritual tablets from Ugarit also tie sacrifices and other acts to phases of the moon. Offerings at the beginning of a month show how a lunar marker could organize a temple's work. These precedents belong to a wider Near Eastern practice of using the changing moon to arrange recurring communal and ritual events.

## In the Hebrew Bible

David's absence from Saul's new-moon meal is part of the plan he makes with Jonathan in 1 Sam 20:5, 18, 24–27. The royal gathering makes his absence conspicuous on successive days. In 2 Kgs 4:23 the new moon and Sabbath are mentioned as ordinary occasions for visiting a man of God. Amos 8:5 imagines sellers eager for the new moon and Sabbath to end so they can resume selling grain. Together the passages connect the day with households, worship, and the conduct of trade.

Num 10:10 directs that silver trumpets be sounded at the beginnings of months, and Num 28:11–15 prescribes burnt offerings and a purification offering at the start of each month. In Ezek 46:1, 3, 6–7 the gate is open on Sabbaths and new moons, and the prince brings offerings. Ps 81:3 (MT 81:4) associates blowing the horn with the new moon and a day denoted by *kēseh*, commonly understood as the full moon. Prov 7:20 uses a related form in a notice about the time of someone's return.

## Interpretive Significance

Hos 2:11 (MT 2:13) includes new moons among festive occasions that God will end. Isa 1:13–14 rejects the community's festivals as part of a call for changed conduct, while Isa 66:23 uses the recurring new moon and Sabbath to picture regular worship by all people. Col 2:16 names a feast, new moon, and Sabbath among observances on which its readers should resist being judged. The pairing of Sabbath and new moon reflects their prominence in communal life.
