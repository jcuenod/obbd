## Overview

A Hebrew inscription on a potsherd from Tell Qasile names gold of Ophir, Beth-horon, and a weighed quantity. It is the clearest inscriptional evidence for the expression “gold of Ophir” outside the Bible. The sherd was found on the surface, so its usual eighth-century BCE date depends on the script. A second inscription from the site mentions oil. Together they illustrate short records of valuable goods while leaving open where the gold originated and what institution received it.

## Description and Provenance

Tell Qasile lies near the Yarkon River in modern Tel Aviv. The gold inscription was reportedly found on the surface in 1946 and was published with another sherd by Benjamin Maisler, later Mazar, in 1951. A surface find lacks the sealed layer that might independently date the inscription. Its usually assigned eighth-century date comes primarily from comparison of its letters with other Hebrew writing. The sherd’s manufacture and the act of inscribing it need not have occurred at the same time.

## Contents

The short incised text places gold of [[ophir|Ophir]] beside the expression *byt ḥrn* and a number generally understood as thirty shekels. The shekel here is a unit of weight. Many interpreters read *byt ḥrn* as Beth-horon, the place known in biblical geography. Another interpretation understands it as a house or temple associated with the deity Horon. The preposition can be read in more than one way in a brief label. The record gives no longer explanation of the owner, recipient, or circumstances of the weighing.

The other Tell Qasile ostracon mentions oil and the king and gives a quantity. It is often understood as an administrative notation. The existence of both texts illustrates how sherds could carry short records of goods in a place connected with routes of exchange. Their find association does not establish that they belonged to the same transaction or archive.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

First Kings 9:28 and 10:11 describe gold brought from Ophir in connection with Solomon’s trading ventures. First Kings 22:48 describes a later fleet that failed to reach its destination. The sherd attests the use of the name with gold, independently of those stories. It does not identify the port from which any given consignment came. First Kings 9:17 names Beth-horon in Solomon’s building program. If the sherd refers to that place, it gives a comparison for the name in a record of trade or collection.

Ps 45:9 (MT 45:10) invokes Ophir gold in a royal context, while Job 28:16 and Isa 13:12 use it as a standard of value or rarity. Such uses are consistent with a recognized designation for valuable gold. The inscription makes that designation visible in a different genre from poetry or accounts of kings.

## Scholarly Issues

The expression may concern geographic origin, an established type of gold, or both. It can occur in a local transaction even if its name derives from distant trade. Proposed locations for Ophir in Arabia, Africa, or South Asia depend on other lines of evidence. Neither the wording of the ostracon nor its surface provenance decides among them.
