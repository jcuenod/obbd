## Overview

The Narmer Palette is a large ceremonial stone palette from Hierakonpolis, an early center of Egyptian kingship. Its carvings show King Narmer wearing two different crowns and asserting power over defeated enemies. Among the scenes are a king striking a captive and a bull breaking into a fortified enclosure. These images are early examples of visual conventions that Egyptian artists used for many centuries. Related images later circulated in the Levant and provide background for biblical descriptions of rulers overcoming enemies.

## Description and Provenance

Excavators working at Hierakonpolis in the late nineteenth century recovered the palette from the temple district. It is carved from a dark fine-grained stone often described as siltstone or greywacke. Like smaller palettes used for grinding pigments, it has a circular working area. Its size, elaborate carving, and temple context give it a ceremonial character. It is catalogued as JE 32169 in the Egyptian Museum collection.

## Contents

In the main scene on one face, Narmer wears the White Crown associated with Upper Egypt and raises a mace over a kneeling captive. A falcon above the scene appears with signs that may identify the defeated territory or people. On the other face Narmer wears the Red Crown associated with Lower Egypt and approaches rows of dead enemies. The palette therefore presents the same named king in the regalia of both regions.

Other scenes include a bull breaking through the wall of an enclosure and trampling a fallen figure. The bull is usually understood as embodying the ruler's strength. A pair of long-necked animals frame the round depression on the same face. The composition brings together royal regalia, conquered enemies, and mastery over formidable animals or forces.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The palette provides a starting point for tracing the long history of the [[smiting-the-enemy-motif|smiting ruler]] in Egyptian art. Images of rulers striking foes or placing them underfoot traveled through Egyptian royal monuments and portable objects, including scarabs. Such material supplies the regional background for later biblical poetry and narrative.

In Josh 10:24 victorious military leaders place their feet on the necks of defeated kings. Ps 110:1 pictures the king's foes made into a [[footstool|footstool]], and Ps 110:5–6 speaks of kings and heads being shattered. Ps 68:21 (MT 68:22) attributes the crushing of enemies' heads to God. These scenes draw force from a visual vocabulary of subjugation. Deut 33:17 compares Joseph's strength to that of a bull and pictures it goring peoples. The Near Eastern [[bull-iconography|royal bull imagery]] supplies a broader context for such a comparison.

## Scholarly Issues

The two crowns have encouraged the view that the palette commemorates the unification of Egypt, possibly in connection with a victory by Narmer. Another interpretation emphasizes the portrayal of kingship and the defeat of disorder in a ceremonial image. Identifying a specific battle from the carvings requires information the object does not provide. For biblical comparison, the strongest evidence comes from the long circulation of the imagery and its reappearance in later settings.
