## Overview

The Behistun Inscription is a rock relief and multilingual text commissioned by Darius I on a cliff in western Iran around 520 BCE. It gives the new Persian king's account of taking the throne and defeating rivals who challenged his rule. The monument presents these victories as granted by Ahuramazda, the god Darius invokes as his protector. An Aramaic version found in Egypt shows that the account circulated far beyond the cliff. The inscription supplies political context for Haggai, Zechariah, and Ezra, which place the rebuilding of Jerusalem's temple in Darius's reign.

## Description and Provenance

The relief and texts are carved high above a route linking the Iranian plateau with Mesopotamia. [[darius-i|Darius]] appears with a bow, his foot on a fallen figure identified as Gaumata. Bound opponents stand in front of him, and a winged figure appears above the scene. The accompanying texts were carved in Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian, a variety of Akkadian. The monument took shape in stages, and the versions differ in some details.

An [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]] version survives on papyrus among the [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine papyri]] from southern Egypt. The surviving manuscript is generally dated to the late fifth century BCE, long after the events described. Pieces of a Babylonian copy were found at Babylon. The copies and the translation show that the royal account could be read far from the site, in scripts and languages suited to different settings. [@tad, C2.1]

## Contents

Darius claims that Cambyses killed his brother Bardiya in secret. In his telling, a man named Gaumata then pretended to be Bardiya and took the throne, until Darius and his supporters killed him. The text recounts campaigns against multiple rivals, some of whom also claimed established royal identities. Darius treats his opponents' claims as lies and credits the favor of Ahuramazda with his victories.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Hag 1:1 dates an oracle to Darius's second year, 520 BCE. Ezra 4:24 also dates the resumption of work on Jerusalem's temple to his second year. The inscription shows that the beginning of the reign was marked by resistance and military consolidation throughout [[persia|the Persian Empire]]. The proposed connection between those revolts and the shaking of kingdoms in Hag 2:6–7, 21–22 is debated. The report of a quiet earth in Zech 1:11 may likewise be read against imperial pacification, while a specific historical allusion remains uncertain.

Esth 1:22, 3:12, and 8:9 describe royal communications issued in the languages and scripts of their recipients. The versions of the Behistun text illustrate the wider practice of circulating royal claims in more than one language. The inscription also reports the display of defeated opponents on stakes. That evidence provides context for the stake punishment threatened in Ezra 6:11, although the precise manner of punishment can be difficult to establish from the wording.

## Scholarly Issues

Herodotus tells a related story about the removal of a man who claimed Bardiya's identity. Many historians suspect that Darius actually displaced Bardiya or otherwise used the story of an impostor to justify taking the throne. Others allow that an imposture was possible. Both the monument and the Greek account reflect versions of the victorious king's story, so the exact chain of events remains disputed.

In the 1830s and 1840s Henry Rawlinson copied the inscription and worked on the Old Persian text. The ability to compare versions contributed to the nineteenth-century decipherment of cuneiform. The repeated appeals to Ahuramazda also make the inscription central to the study of Persian royal religion. The relationship between these claims and later [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrian tradition]] remains debated.
