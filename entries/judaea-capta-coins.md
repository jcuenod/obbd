## Overview

Judaea Capta coins are Roman issues commemorating victory in the Jewish revolt that began in 66 CE. Their designs present Judaea as a conquered land, often as a mourning woman seated below a date palm. Vespasian and Titus used victory imagery to assert the authority of the new Flavian dynasty, and related types continued under Domitian. The coins document Roman public claims about the fall of Jerusalem. They also offer a visual parallel to biblical portrayals of a city as a woman in mourning.

## Archaeological Evidence

Coins with the legend *IVDAEA CAPTA*, meaning “Judaea captured,” were produced in different metals and denominations. One well-attested bronze issue of Vespasian from Rome dates to 71 CE. The emperor’s portrait appears on one face, with the victory design on the other. The seated woman often appears with a palm tree, weapons, a Roman victor or a bound captive. Details vary by issue. Other legends and regional variants convey comparable victory claims. [@abd, 1:1083–1089] [@ocre, RIC II.1 (2nd ed.) Vespasian 159]

The design belongs to a Roman convention of representing a conquered people or territory as a human figure. A palm supplied a recognizable reference to the region, while the captive’s pose expressed subjection. Related treatments of other peoples, including Germania, show how Rome adapted this visual language. The campaign also furnished a triumph and lasting monuments, including the [[arch-of-titus|Arch of Titus]], whose reliefs emphasize the procession of spoils and architectural display. [@abd] [@neaehl]

The rebels’ own [[jewish-revolt-coinage|coins]] had proclaimed Jerusalem holy and appealed to Zion’s freedom or redemption. The Flavian issues made Judea’s defeat a public claim in the same medium. Other measures gave that victory continuing effects for Jewish communities. After 70 CE the *fiscus Iudaicus*, the Roman treasury fund for the Jewish tax, received a levy of two drachmas in place of the former Jerusalem temple contribution. The coinage commemorates military victory and the tax records a subsequent policy. [@abd, 1:1083–1089]

## In the Bible

Isa 3:26 portrays Jerusalem sitting on the ground after disaster, and Lam 1:1 pictures the bereaved city as a solitary widow. These images and the Roman captive woman convey grief and loss through a female figure representing a place and people. The Roman issues work within their own victory tradition, and no textual dependence is evident. Luke 21:24 depicts people carried away captive and Jerusalem trampled by Gentiles, experiences that form a sharp counterpart to the victor’s account. [@abd]

Rev 18:7 presents Babylon boasting that she is a queen who will never know widowhood. Some readers connect the passage’s broad reversal of imperial fortunes with images like those on the Flavian coins. The specific identification with the coins remains disputed. The boast also draws on a wider biblical tradition of cities personified as proud rulers or grieving women. [@abd, 1:1083–1089]
