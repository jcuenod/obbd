## Overview

Aniconism is the practice of approaching a deity in worship without a figured image of that deity. Biblical writers prohibit images and sometimes speak of Yahweh's presence in a place where no statue is described. Archaeological evidence from Israel and Judah includes plain standing stones and other kinds of sacred object. Comparisons with neighboring traditions help explain how a place or object could mark divine presence without portraying a body. They also help distinguish an absence of images in a particular shrine from a stated requirement to avoid them.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Many ancient Near Eastern temples maintained [[cult-images|statues of their gods]], and other sanctuaries used objects lacking a human or animal shape. The [[nabataeans|Nabataeans]] of Petra, for example, used sacred stones known as betyls, some carved as plain slabs or blocks in a niche. Figured sculpture was also used at Petra. This later regional comparison shows that an unfigured sacred object could be honored in a society that also knew other forms of divine representation. It offers no direct evidence for the practices of early Israel. [@mettinger-no-graven-image]

The Judahite fortress shrine at [[arad|Arad]] contained [[massebot|standing stones]]. Their placement gives grounds for treating them as sacred objects, though no inscription identifies what they represented. An unfigured stone could mark a divine presence, and standing stones also served other purposes in the region. Comparison with the standing stones and sacred slabs of other communities shows a range of possibilities for their use. [@neaehl] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 20:4–6 and Deut 5:8–10 prohibit making an image and bowing before or serving images. Deut 4:15–19 gives a reason for avoiding shaped representations. The people saw no form when Yahweh spoke to them at Horeb. The prohibition of images of Yahweh and the rejection of images used to worship other gods can be distinguished analytically, even though biblical texts often treat these concerns together.

Other passages show that sacred objects could carry different meanings. Jacob sets up a stone, anoints it and associates it with God's house in Gen 28:18–22. In 1 Sam 4:4, Yahweh is described in connection with the cherubim above the [[ark-of-the-covenant|ark]], without a description of a figure occupying the seat. At Bethel and Dan, Jeroboam installs calves (1 Kgs 12:28–29). Scholars debate whether such figures were understood as images, supports for a deity imagined above them, or royal emblems with more than one purpose. [@keel-uehlinger-ggig] [@mettinger-no-graven-image]

## Interpretive Significance

Tryggve Mettinger distinguishes between *de facto aniconism*, in which a shrine has no figured image without an explicit rule against one, and *programmatic aniconism*, in which someone actively rejects such images. He distinguishes within the former between material forms, such as a standing stone, and forms in which an empty place marks where the deity is understood to be present. Even a shrine with no figure could thus draw attention to divine presence through its objects and arrangement. [@mettinger-no-graven-image]

Mettinger interprets Jerusalem's [[cherub-throne|cherub throne]] in terms of an unseen occupant. Herbert Niehr and other scholars have argued that the First Temple once housed a human-shaped statue of Yahweh. Their case depends on reading biblical descriptions of seeing God and related evidence as traces of an earlier practice. With no surviving statue securely identified as Yahweh from that temple, the proposal remains debated, as does the chronology of Israelite image prohibitions. [@mettinger-no-graven-image] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig]
