## Overview

The Magdala Stone is a low carved block found in 2009 in a first-century CE synagogue on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. Its decoration includes a seven-branched lampstand, or menorah, carved while the Jerusalem temple still stood. The carvings are widely understood to evoke that temple, although the meaning of some images and the stone's purpose remain uncertain. The stone shows how a Galilean Jewish community could give the Jerusalem sanctuary a visible place in its own gathering space. The site supplies a setting for the Gospel accounts of synagogue teaching, not evidence that Jesus taught in this building.

## Description and Provenance

The Israel Antiquities Authority excavated the stone in the main hall of a synagogue at Magdala, or Migdal. Stone benches line the walls of the hall, and parts of the building preserve a mosaic floor. The block stands on four short legs. Its form and position in the central space are reasons to treat it as furniture used within the assembly rather than as an architectural detail.

Magdala is widely identified with Taricheae, the lakeside town described by the Jewish historian [[josephus|Josephus]]. That identification places the synagogue in a town with a harbor and a fishing economy, not an isolated settlement. Archaeologists have also found Jewish ritual immersion pools at Magdala, part of the wider material record of [[stone-vessels-and-mikvaot|Jewish purity practices]]. The [[galilee-boat|ancient boat found near Ginosar]] gives a separate example of the craft used on the same lake.

## Contents

On one short face a lampstand appears beneath an arch, flanked by two vessels with handles. Rows of arches run along the long sides, and the upper surface bears a rosette, a design shaped like a flower. The carvings do not have an accompanying inscription that names what they depict. The lampstand is recognizable from its form; the arches and other details leave greater room for interpretation.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 25:31–40 describes a gold [[menorah|lampstand]] for the tabernacle, Israel's portable sanctuary, with six branches extending from a central shaft and seven lamps. The Magdala carving shows how the seven-branched form could serve as a visual reference to the Jerusalem sanctuary in the first century. It is among the earliest surviving depictions found outside Jerusalem. A menorah also appears on earlier coins of the Hasmonean ruler Mattathias Antigonus, showing that the design was already in use before it was carved at Magdala.

Mark 1:39 and Luke 4:15 describe Jesus preaching or teaching in Galilean [[synagogues]]. The hall at Magdala supplies archaeological evidence for the sort of gathering places these accounts presuppose; the synagogue remains at [[capernaum|Capernaum]] have a different building history. Luke 8:2 identifies Mary by the name Magdalene, commonly taken to indicate an association with Magdala, but does not place her in this synagogue. Matt 15:39 names Magadan in the NIV and ESV, while some manuscripts read Magdala. The variant makes the identification disputed, and even a reference to the town would not establish a visit to the building.

## Scholarly Issues

Mordechai Aviam reads the carvings together as a symbolic rendering of the Jerusalem temple, including parts of its interior. Lutz Doering also sees references to [[herods-temple|the Jerusalem temple]], but he argues that the images link local scripture reading to the sanctuary rather than representing the building as a model. The motifs alone cannot establish whether the carver saw the temple or worked from descriptions and familiar images.

Aviam proposes that the stone served as the base of a table for reading the Torah, the books of Moses. The stone's location shows that it belonged to the gathering space, but it does not show how a reader would have used it or whether it held a scroll at all. The evidence for temple imagery in a synagogue is stronger than the evidence for a particular use of the block.
