## Overview

Royal courts in Israel, Judah, and neighboring kingdoms relied on officials who managed households, kept records, carried messages, and commanded troops. Titles survive in biblical narratives, inscriptions, and records written in Akkadian and other languages. Some biblical forms preserve foreign titles in Hebrew spelling, while others name posts known from Hebrew inscriptions and seals. These sources help explain who could speak for a king, control access to him, or oversee royal property. They also show why the same title could have different duties at different courts or periods.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Assyrian court titles help explain the officers sent by Sennacherib in 2 Kgs 18:17. The Tartan represents Akkadian *turtānu*, a senior military commander. Rab-saris reflects *rab ša rēši*, the head of the group of court officials conventionally called [[eunuchs|eunuchs]]. Rabshakeh reflects *rab šāqê*, “chief cupbearer.” A title associated with serving the king could become a high office with responsibilities far removed from the task in its name [@rinap1, no. 5 line 10] [@saa19, introduction n. 221].

A clay tablet in the British Museum records gold sent to a temple on behalf of Nabû-šarrussu-ukīn, who bears the Babylonian title conventionally rendered “chief eunuch.” The tablet is dated to 595 BCE. The name and title are close to those of an official often identified as Nebo-sarsekim in Jer 39:3. The identification is plausible, although the division and interpretation of the Hebrew names and titles in that verse are difficult.

The Hebrew [[royal-steward-inscription|royal steward inscription]] at Silwan in Jerusalem identifies a tomb owner with the title “who is over the house.” The personal name is damaged. The title also occurs on [[seals-and-bullae|seals and clay seal impressions]], along with titles such as “servant of the king.” These inscriptions indicate the use of such language by the officials themselves [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 507–510] [@avigad-sass-wsss, 49–60, 170–177].

The [[jerusalem-governor-sealing|Jerusalem governor sealing]] preserves the Hebrew title “governor of the city,” borne by Joshua in 2 Kgs 23:8 and by Maaseiah in 2 Chr 34:8. The clay was recovered in the Western Wall Plaza. It bears no personal name, and its use as a docket to accompany goods remains a proposal [@weksler-bdolah-western-wall, fig. 3].

## In the Hebrew Bible

The lists of David's and Solomon's officers mention commanders, secretaries, and a recorder, and Solomon's list has a king's friend and an official over the household (2 Sam 8:16–18; 1 Kgs 4:1–6). The titles can suggest broad divisions of responsibility. Their precise duties are less clear, especially for titles such as recorder, which could involve functions beyond keeping a written record. In the story of Ahab, Obadiah is over the king's household (1 Kgs 18:3).

The book of Isaiah makes the authority of a household officer more concrete. Isa 22:15–25 describes Shebna's removal, Eliakim's appointment, and the transfer of a key symbolizing control of access to the house of David. Isa 36:3 identifies Eliakim as over the household, Shebna as secretary, and Joah as recorder. The three represent Hezekiah's court in dealings with Assyria during [[sennacherib-campaign-701|Sennacherib's campaign]].

## Interpretive Significance

T. N. D. Mettinger proposed that the organization or titles of some Israelite offices reflect Egyptian models. Egypt offers useful comparisons for specialized court service, such as the offices associated with Pharaoh's chief cupbearer and chief baker (Gen 40:1–2). Similar needs could also give rise to similar titles and duties independently. The strength of the comparison depends on each title's wording, attestation, and historical setting. Individual matches can tell more than a claim that one court copied another's entire organization.
