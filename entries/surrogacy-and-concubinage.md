## Overview

Biblical households could include more than one wife, and some women were described as concubines, a status that commonly ranked below the principal wife. Genesis depicts wives who give enslaved women to their husbands to obtain children for their households. Cuneiform laws and contracts document related arrangements, though obligations to supply a woman and the rights of mothers and children vary by place and by household. The modern term *surrogacy* describes one aspect of these arrangements. The ancient evidence concerns reproduction within marriage and slavery, where the women who bore the children had limited power over their own lives.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Laws of Hammurabi §§144–147 distinguish cases in which a woman of *nadītu* status gives her husband an enslaved woman who bears children. In one case childbearing limits the senior woman's power to sell her. The text preserves the difference in their rank. Sections 170–171 concern the inheritance rights of sons born to an enslaved woman. The father's recognition of them during his lifetime can give them a share alongside the sons of his wife. This shows how an act of recognition may affect rights even when sons share the same father [@roth-law].

In the Old Assyrian marriage contract of Laqipum and Hatala from Kültepe, Hatala must acquire an enslaved woman if she has produced no child within two years. The contract also anticipates the woman's sale after she has borne a child. A marriage document from Nuzi involving Shennima and Kelim-ninu stipulates that Kelim-ninu provide a woman for Shennima if she bears no child. These documents regulate particular households. They supply parallels for the use of another woman's childbearing to secure heirs, while leaving room for differences in her status and the eventual place of her children [@anet; @cos3].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Sarai gives Hagar to Abram in the hope of obtaining children through her (Gen 16:1–6). The narrator calls Hagar Abram's wife within the arrangement and continues to present her as subject to Sarai. Hagar's pregnancy soon intensifies conflict. Later Sarah presses Abraham to send Hagar and Ishmael away because she opposes Ishmael inheriting alongside Isaac (Gen 21:9–14). Rachel gives Bilhah to Jacob, with the wish that Bilhah bear upon her knees, and Leah gives Jacob Zilpah after she ceases bearing (Gen 30:1–13). The wives name the children. The narrative provides no legal explanation for the gesture of bearing on the knees.

Other stories associate concubinage with household standing and royal power. The woman of Judges 19 is called a concubine, yet her father receives the man as a son-in-law (Judg 19:1–4). Reuben's intercourse with Bilhah violates Jacob's household (Gen 35:22). David's concubines become part of a public claim to royal authority in Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 16:21–22), and Ish-bosheth accuses Abner in connection with Saul's concubine Rizpah (2 Sam 3:7). Solomon interprets Adonijah's request for Abishag as a claim to the kingdom (1 Kgs 2:13–25). The text gives Abishag a different designation.

## Interpretive Significance

The comparative documents show why giving a woman to a husband, bearing children, recognizing them, and assigning an inheritance require separate attention. They can illuminate conflicts in Genesis without establishing which specific rules would have applied to Hagar or Bilhah. The narratives also give unusual visibility to women whose voices the legal documents rarely preserve.
