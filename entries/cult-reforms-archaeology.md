## Overview

The books of Kings and Chronicles credit Hezekiah and Josiah with changing worship in Judah in the late eighth and late seventh centuries BCE. Excavated shrines at Arad and Lachish, and stones from a dismantled altar at Beersheba, have been compared with those accounts. The remains can document the ending or alteration of ritual activity at particular places. Identifying the person who ordered a change and the reason for it is more difficult. Other finds, including a temple at Tel Moẓa and household figurines, show that religious practice in Judah varied across places and periods.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

At [[arad|Arad]], excavations uncovered a shrine inside a Judahite fortress. Zeʾev Herzog placed its deliberate closure in the late eighth century BCE and associated it with Hezekiah. Yohanan Aharoni's earlier reconstruction assigned the disuse of the altar and the shrine to different phases, connecting them with Hezekiah and Josiah respectively. The order and dating of those phases remain disputed. A 2020 study identified residues of frankincense and cannabis on two small altars from the shrine. The residues attest materials used in worship while leaving the circumstances of the shrine's closure open.

At [[beersheba|Beersheba]], blocks from a [[horned-altars|horned altar]] were found in secondary use, including in a storehouse wall. Aharoni linked their reuse to the reform of Hezekiah. The debris provides evidence of dismantling earlier than the construction that reused the blocks. It gives no direct evidence of a royal order or the altar's original setting [@neaehl, 1:82–85, 1:170–173].

Excavators at [[lachish|Lachish]] interpreted a chamber of the Iron Age gate as a shrine. They argued that the horns of its altars were cut away and a perforated stone interpreted as a toilet was installed to make the shrine unfit for worship. David Ussishkin disputed the identification of the chamber and the stone. Other archaeologists accept evidence of cult activity while proposing different functions for some of the installations. The precise relationship between the finds and a reform remains disputed.

## In the Hebrew Bible

2 Kgs 18:3–6 commends [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] for removing [[high-places|local shrines]], pillars, an asherah, and the bronze serpent. In 2 Kgs 18:22, an Assyrian representative cites the removal of shrines to undermine the king's appeal to God. Chronicles gives a much longer account focused on temple worship and its restoration (2 Chr 29–31). Whether these accounts reflect a coordinated reform identifiable at particular sites is debated.

The reign of [[josiah|Josiah]] is also associated with removing objects of worship, defiling shrines, and changing cult practice in Jerusalem and beyond (2 Kgs 23:4–20; 2 Chr 34:3–7). The operation in Kings can be compared to the demand in Deut 12:2–7 to end sacrifice at other places and worship at the place God chooses. The fit between the literary program and historical practice is debated. The earlier story of Jehu making Baal's temple a latrine (2 Kgs 10:27) supplies a comparison for the Lachish interpretation without resolving the function of the perforated stone.

## Interpretive Significance

Lisbeth Fried and Nadav Naʾaman have questioned whether the archaeological evidence establishes the reforms described in Kings. A temple at [[tel-motza-temple|Tel Moẓa]] confirms the existence of formal worship close to Jerusalem outside the capital. Judahite pillar figurines, small clay figures with column-shaped bases, continued to circulate in household settings late in the monarchy [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 329–339, 361–373]. Individual changes in sanctioned worship could coexist with continuity in local or domestic practices. Evaluating a proposed reform requires a secure date, evidence for the nature of the change, and a basis for connecting it to royal action.
