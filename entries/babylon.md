## Overview

Babylon was a city on the Euphrates in what is now central Iraq. It became the capital of Hammurabi's kingdom in the second millennium BCE and of the Neo-Babylonian Empire in the late seventh and sixth centuries BCE. The best-known ruins belong to the city rebuilt by Nebuchadnezzar II, whose palaces and temples display the resources of that empire. In the Bible, Babylon is the destination of Judean deportees and the subject of prophetic judgments. Its memory as a powerful imperial city also gives the name a symbolic role in the New Testament.

## Location and Identification

The remains of [[babylonia|Babylonia's]] capital lie near modern Hillah, south of Baghdad. The ancient city stretched across the Euphrates, with the major excavated monuments concentrated on the east side. Babylonian descriptions name the city's gates, sacred streets, temples, and districts. Several names can be matched with remains through inscriptions found at the site [@cos2].

## Archaeological History

Robert Koldewey directed the German excavations that began in 1899 and continued until the closing years of the First World War. They exposed much of the monumental city rebuilt by [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]], including the Southern Palace and the [[ishtar-gate|Ishtar Gate]] on the Processional Way. This ceremonial street connected the gate with the temple district. Excavators also traced the Esagila temple of the city's chief god [[marduk|Marduk]] and the foundations of [[etemenanki|Etemenanki]], its nearby stepped temple tower. Much of the earlier city lies beneath later buildings and a high water table.

Koldewey proposed that a vaulted structure in the Southern Palace had supported the Hanging Gardens described by classical writers. Administrative tablets, including the [[jehoiachin-ration-tablets|Jehoiachin ration tablets]], were recovered near the stairway to the vaults. The evidence fits a storage or administrative function, though the building's precise use remains open. Stephanie Dalley has proposed that the gardens remembered by classical authors were at Nineveh. No excavated structure has been securely identified as the Hanging Gardens.

Parts of the palace, gate, and ceremonial street were rebuilt above ancient foundations under Saddam Hussein's government. These reconstructions and other modern building projects damaged parts of the ruins. The site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Gen 10:8–12, Babel belongs to Nimrod's realm. Gen 11:1–9 associates the name with Hebrew *bālal* (“to confuse”), playing on the sound of the name. The Babylonian form was understood in Akkadian as “gate of the god.” The city's monumental temple tower provides background for the [[ziggurat-and-babel|Tower of Babel]].

Kings recounts the removal of Jehoiachin and others from Jerusalem to Babylon and later describes his release from confinement and continued support at the royal court (2 Kgs 24:10–17; 25:27–30). The oil ration tablets independently document palace support for him and members of his family during Nebuchadnezzar's reign. Dan 4:30 (MT 4:27) portrays Nebuchadnezzar boasting over the city he built. The king's surviving building inscriptions supply a historical counterpart to the language of his boast, including the [[east-india-house-inscription|East India House Inscription]].

Ps 137 gives voice to the grief of deportees by the waters of Babylon, while prophetic oracles announce judgment on the city and its rulers (Isa 13–14; Jer 50–51). In Rev 17–18 the name Babylon evokes an imperial city of wealth and violence, commonly understood as a reference to Rome. Most interpreters also understand Babylon in the greeting of 1 Pet 5:13 as Rome. Both usages draw on the remembered imperial city.
