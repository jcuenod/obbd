## Overview

Lilith is a figure named in an oracle against Edom in Isaiah. Her name is usually related to Akkadian terms for types of harmful spirits. It appears again in a Jewish text from Qumran and in later protective texts, where the figure takes on more definite traits. Some English Bibles translate the word as an animal or night creature. The ancient evidence helps explain why Isaiah places Lilith with the inhabitants of an abandoned landscape and how much later legend added to the brief biblical description.

## Sources and Iconography

Mesopotamian sources list spirits called *lilû* and *lilītu*, the latter feminine, in contexts of illness or danger. Scholars usually connect the Hebrew *lîlît* with this group. The Hebrew word's sound also suggests a possible association with the ordinary Hebrew word for night. A single fixed portrait cannot be reconstructed from the group names [@ddd].

The figure has often been identified with the winged woman on the [[burney-relief|Burney Relief]], an uninscribed Old Babylonian plaque. Other identifications include Ishtar and Ereshkigal. The image cannot settle the figure's identity. A creature called *ki-sikil-lil-la* in the Sumerian story of Gilgamesh and the ḫuluppu tree has likewise been compared with Lilith. The meaning of that expression and the relation to the later name are debated [@george-gilgamesh] [@ddd].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 34:14 places *lîlît* in an oracle about Edom's ruin, alongside wild creatures and other inhabitants of desolation. The passage gives her a resting place and offers little further description. Translators have used names for nocturnal animals, including “screech owl” in the King James Version, or kept the name Lilith. Rendering it as a night spirit reflects the comparison with Akkadian spirit names. Isa 13:21–22 contains similar imagery of desert creatures and goatlike figures in the ruins of Babylon. The parallel helps explain the literary setting even though that passage does not name Lilith.

## Scholarly Issues

The [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scroll]] 4Q510, part of the group of texts called Songs of the Sage, includes Lilith in a list of threatening spirits. The songs seek protection against such forces, and the list helps show how readers in the Second Temple period could associate figures from biblical oracles with [[demons]]. In late antique Mesopotamia, Aramaic incantation bowls also name Lilith or related spirits in protective texts. Where bowls also have drawings, identifying an image with a particular named figure requires attention to that object.

The medieval *Alphabet of Ben Sira* tells a story of Lilith as Adam's first wife. This is a later development in the figure's history. Isaiah supplies only the brief scene of a desolate landscape. Distinguishing the oracle, the protective traditions, and the medieval narrative helps explain how descriptions of Lilith changed over time.
