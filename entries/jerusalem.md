## Overview

Jerusalem developed from a Bronze Age settlement in the central highlands into the political and religious center of Judah. Egyptian correspondence names a Late Bronze ruler, while later Assyrian and Babylonian records describe imperial campaigns involving the city. Excavations document changing settlement limits and repeated episodes of building, siege and destruction. The scale of tenth-century Jerusalem remains debated. Its later history as the site of the Second Temple and its destruction by Rome are central to understanding how Jerusalem figures across the Hebrew Bible and New Testament.

## Location and Identification

The earliest settlement occupied the southeastern ridge above the Gihon spring, in the area usually called the [[city-of-david|City of David]]. The ridge rises northward toward the [[temple-mount|Temple Mount]], while the Western Hill became part of the larger city in later periods. The second-millennium [[execration-texts|Egyptian Execration Texts]] may preserve an early form of Jerusalem’s name. The fourteenth-century BCE [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] of Abdi-Heba, commonly numbered EA 285–290, give firmer evidence that an Egyptian vassal governed Jerusalem and its territory. The surviving buildings assigned to this period are limited. [@cos1] [@moran-amarna, EA 285–290] [@neaehl]

## Archaeological History

The extent of the early royal center is contested. Eilat Mazar interpreted monumental walls on the southeastern ridge, known as the Large Stone Structure, as remains of a royal building dating to David’s era. Israel Finkelstein and colleagues have questioned whether the walls form a single structure and whether the pottery and building relationships establish a tenth-century date. Recent radiocarbon work supports settlement during the centuries before and including the tenth century and suggests westward growth by the ninth century, with later expansion and fortification. The findings do not settle the identification of a specific royal building. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

Jerusalem expanded markedly in the later Iron Age. Nahman Avigad excavated part of the substantial fortification on the Western Hill called the Broad Wall. Growth in the late eighth century is often associated with immigration after the Assyrian conquest of the northern kingdom and preparations under Hezekiah, although the scale and relative importance of these factors are uncertain. The city escaped capture in [[sennacherib-campaign-701|Sennacherib’s campaign]] in 701 BCE. Destruction deposits on the southeastern ridge attest burning associated with the [[fall-of-jerusalem-587|Babylonian conquest]] in 587 or 586 BCE. [@neaehl] [@rinap3-1]

Persian-period Jerusalem was the temple center of [[yehud-province|Yehud]], though remains for this period are relatively limited. Growth in the Hellenistic and Roman periods produced major fortifications and expansions, including [[herods-temple|Herod’s rebuilding of the temple precinct]]. Rome destroyed the Second Temple in 70 CE, and the city was subsequently refounded as Aelia Capitolina. Modern buildings limit excavation, and access, interpretation and heritage management in East Jerusalem remain politically sensitive. [@neaehl] [@abd]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 14:18 names Melchizedek as king of Salem. Ps 76:2 (MT 76:3) places Salem in poetic parallelism with Zion, supporting the traditional but disputed identification of the earlier passage’s Salem with Jerusalem. Josh 10:1–5 portrays a king of Jerusalem leading a coalition, while Josh 15:63 describes continued Jebusite presence. 2 Sam 5:6–10 assigns the capture of Zion to David, and 1 Kings 6–8 attributes the temple and palace to Solomon. Archaeology and the Amarna letters establish occupation and political importance in earlier periods. The remains alone cannot determine the scale of the city described for the early monarchy. [@moran-amarna] [@neaehl] [@abd]

Isa 22:9–11 describes houses torn down to fortify Jerusalem, a practice comparable to the layout of the Broad Wall where it crosses earlier buildings. The precise correlation remains a proposal. 2 Kgs 18:13–19:37 describes the Assyrian confrontation, and 2 Kgs 25:8–10 describes the subsequent Babylonian destruction. Neh 2:11–3:32 depicts survey and rebuilding in the Persian period. Psalm 48 expresses Jerusalem’s security as the city of God, illustrating the theological significance of Zion that accompanied its political and architectural history. [@abd] [@neaehl]
