## Overview

Olive oil was a staple of life in the ancient Levant, used for food, light, grooming, and worship. Farmers grew olive trees across much of the Mediterranean zone, and workers crushed and pressed the fruit to extract its oil. At seventh-century BCE Ekron, production took place on an industrial scale. Other evidence, including receipts from Samaria, shows how oil entered systems of collection and exchange. These settings explain why the Bible treats oil as a sign of agricultural abundance and also specifies particular qualities of oil for lamps and tribute.

## Archaeological Evidence

Olives must be crushed to release their liquid before the solids are pressed. Ancient installations could include a stone basin for crushing, a place for containing the pulp, and a receptacle below the press to collect liquid. In a beam press, stone weights increase the downward force at one end of a beam, which compresses the pulp. The liquid that drains from the press contains both oil and water. Separation follows as the oil rises. Rotary crushing stones and screw mechanisms became important in later periods, alongside continued use of older technologies.

At [[ekron-royal-dedicatory-inscription|Ekron]] (Tel Miqne), excavators and surveyors identified more than one hundred oil production units of the seventh century BCE. Concentrations of basins, weights, and associated buildings support the interpretation of large-scale production. Ekron flourished under Assyrian hegemony, when rulers there appear in Assyrian records as vassals. Some scholars see investment or encouragement by imperial authorities behind its growth. Others give greater weight to local decisions and regional markets. The installations establish the scale of the industry more directly than they establish the destination of all the oil.

The [[samaria-ostraca|Samaria ostraca]] include records of deliveries of wine and oil. The expression for the oil is commonly rendered “washed” or “refined.” The receipts attest collection from named people or places. Scholars have debated whether they reflect taxation, estate income, or another transfer within administration. Their labels show that the quality or treatment of oil could matter to officials keeping the records.

## In the Bible

Deut 8:8 names olives and oil among the produce that characterizes the land’s abundance. Deut 24:20 forbids going over the olive branches a second time after harvest, leaving part of the crop for people without ordinary access to it. Judg 9:8–9 takes the prestige of olive oil for granted when the olive tree refuses to reign over the trees. Mic 6:15 uses the prospect of treading olives without enjoying their oil as an image of work with no return. Hos 2:8 (MT 2:10) and Ps 104:15 place oil among the goods that sustain and gladden human life.

Exod 27:20 calls for pure beaten olive oil for the [[oil-lamps|lamps]] of the sanctuary. The Hebrew term *kātît* indicates a process of beating or crushing, and the context specifies clarity or purity. It suggests selection of a high-grade product without identifying every step by which it was prepared. First Kings 5:11 (MT 5:25) describes Solomon’s provision of fine oil for Hiram’s household, placing an agricultural product within dealings between rulers. The name Gethsemane in Matt 26:36 is usually explained as an oil press. That etymology evokes the landscape around Jerusalem, though the verse does not describe an operating press.
