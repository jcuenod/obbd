## Overview

Maqlû (Akkadian for “Burning”) is a Mesopotamian series of incantations, or spoken ritual formulas, and instructions for undoing witchcraft. It survives in first-millennium BCE cuneiform manuscripts, including copies from Nineveh in Assyria. In the ceremony, a ritual specialist burned figures representing the suspected attackers, sought to turn their spells back on them, and purified the patient. The series helps explain what ancient readers might have understood by harmful sorcery, while showing why Mesopotamian counter-rituals and biblical prohibitions should not be treated as equivalent.

## Manuscripts and Date

The full series divides the material into eight tablets of incantations and a ninth tablet of ritual directions. The directions identify recitations by their opening words and tell the performer when to use them. The surviving Akkadian copies were written on clay in cuneiform, a script made with wedge-shaped signs; important copies come from seventh-century BCE Nineveh. The date of a copy does not tell us when all the material it preserves was first composed.

## Contents

The ceremony took place toward the end of Abu, a month in the Mesopotamian calendar, and lasted through the night into the following morning. Its early stages call on gods to judge the case and destroy the hostile magic; burning the figures dramatizes the reversal the patient seeks. Later actions concentrate on cleansing and protecting the patient rather than destroying the attackers. At sunrise the incantations welcome the sun god as the patient's rescuer.

The text distinguishes *kišpū* (harmful witchcraft) from the defensive work of the *āšipu* (a trained ritual specialist or exorcist). Burning an image might look aggressive in isolation, but the instructions frame it as a legitimate response to an alleged prior attack. The ceremony is evidence for how specialists described and treated witchcraft, not an independent record of what accused individuals did. Other Mesopotamian ways of countering danger include protective [[amulets]], but those objects should not be confused with the figures destroyed in Maqlû.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 22:18 (MT 22:17) prescribes death for a female sorcerer, *mĕkaššēpâ*, a Hebrew word related to Akkadian *kaššāptu* (female practitioner of witchcraft). The shared root *kšp* helps explain the vocabulary but does not establish that the same actions were in view. Deut 18:10–11 puts sorcery alongside practices such as [[divination]] and [[necromancy]], consulting the dead. Its list has a broader scope than Maqlû's distinction between an illicit attack and a legitimate remedy.

In Mic 5:12 (MT 5:11), Israel's God announces the removal of sorcery and soothsayers. Isa 47:9, 12 taunts Babylon for relying on spells that cannot save it from calamity. Nah 3:4 uses sorcery figuratively, personifying Nineveh as a prostitute whose spells ensnare nations and peoples. These passages show how the vocabulary could serve a prophetic argument without describing a particular incantation or ceremony.

Ezek 13:17–23 condemns women who prophesy and describes bands and veils in connection with the hunting of lives. The comparison with harmful magic is debated: the objects may reflect ritual practice, but the passage also condemns lies, false visions, and divination. Its imagery does not establish what the objects did or identify them with any object used in Maqlû.

## Scholarly Issues

Tzvi Abusch's critical edition assembles the surviving evidence for the series. Abusch reconstructs an earlier, shorter morning rite that developed into the longer overnight ceremony. That sequence is a scholarly reconstruction of how the text grew, not a history supplied by the tablets themselves.
