## Overview

Ammonite inscriptions include a royal text on a bronze bottle from Tell Siran, a fragment from the Amman Citadel, and a seal impression from Tell el-ʿUmeiri. The bottle names three generations of kings. The citadel fragment may invoke the god Milkom, and the seal impression names an official of a ruler commonly identified with the Baalis of Jeremiah. These objects provide information about the institutions and language of Iron Age Ammon. Their preservation and provenance differ, so readings and identifications need to be weighed for each object.

## Description and Provenance

The Tell Siran bottle was uncovered in 1972 on the campus of the University of Jordan, near Amman. Its inscription records a royal genealogy and achievements. The fragment from the Amman Citadel belongs to a different kind of object and survives incompletely. The impression from Tell el-ʿUmeiri came from archaeological excavation. Its context gives it particular importance among seals and sealings attributed to Ammon, many of which are known only through the antiquities market.

## Contents

The bronze bottle names Amminadab, son of Hissalel, son of another Amminadab, in a dynasty of kings of the [[ammonites|Ammonites]]. A ruler of Ammon with a name corresponding to Amminadab appears in Assyrian material from Ashurbanipal’s reign. This can help place the dynasty in the seventh century BCE, although the name’s repetition in the family complicates deciding which bearer is meant. The bottle’s reference to works such as a garden and water installations presents investment in cultivated land as a royal achievement.

The Amman Citadel text is usually read as including the name [[milkom|Milkom]]. Some reconstructions emphasize a divine speech or oracle, and others treat it primarily as a building text. The seal impression names Milkomʾur as a servant of Baʿalyashaʿ. The name provides a plausible link to Baalis, but represents a distinct written form.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Jer 40:14 attributes an effort against Gedaliah to Baalis, king of the Ammonites. The impression gives likely independent evidence for the ruler or his court. It does not describe what happened at Mizpah or verify responsibility for the plot. First Kings 11:5, 33 and 2 Kgs 23:13 associate Milkom with Ammon. Jer 49:1, 3 presents language often read as involving the same deity, with wording and translation differences. The inscriptions show the name in a local setting rather than only in descriptions written by neighboring peoples.

Second Samuel 12:30 describes David taking a crown in Ammon. The name or title associated with the crown can be read in more than one way. Amos 1:13–15 concerns conflict with Ammon and its royal center. Eccl 2:4–6 uses gardens and waterworks in a portrayal of royal activity. The bottle offers a concrete example of such works in a royal inscription while giving no direct evidence for events reported in these biblical passages.

## Scholarly Issues

Ammonite inscriptions are usually distinguished through features of script, language, geography, and names. In very short texts, especially those without documented provenance, the available features can be insufficient to assign an object securely. Excavated examples anchor the comparison. The proposed Baalis identification is stronger than claims that the sealing independently proves any particular biblical event.
