## Overview

Excavated weapons and ancient images document how people in the Bronze and Iron Age Levant armed themselves. Surviving objects include metal blades and projectile heads, stones interpreted as sling ammunition, and pieces of armor. Perishable equipment is less often preserved. The siege of Lachish in 701 BCE is especially well documented because excavation and Assyrian reliefs can be compared. This evidence helps readers picture weapons described in biblical stories and understand later uses of armor and tools as literary images.

## Archaeological Evidence

Swords, spearheads, and arrowheads survive at some settlements, graves, and destruction sites. A metal tip or fastening may outlast the shaft, bow, handle, or shield with which it was used. Distinguishing a projectile from a small blade or a sling stone from an ordinary stone depends on form and find context. A single object found out of context gives limited evidence about its use. Repeated finds and images can support a more specific identification.

At [[lachish|Lachish]] the destruction associated with 701 BCE yielded arrowheads, sling stones, spearheads, and armor scales. The [[lachish-reliefs|Lachish reliefs]] depict archers, slingers, shields, and Assyrian siege equipment. Excavation also exposed the remains of a siege ramp. Together these sources document different equipment used in one campaign. The reliefs were made to present an Assyrian victory. The images select and arrange details for that purpose, while the excavated objects give physical evidence independent of the carving.

Armor made of overlapping scales could use metal attached to material that has decayed. Helmets and shields varied across regions. Similar forms could circulate through trade, service, or imitation. Assigning a piece of equipment to one ethnicity or period therefore requires more than resemblance to an image from a single site.

## In the Bible

First Samuel 17:4–7 describes Goliath’s helmet, armor, and weapons, while 17:38–40, 49–51 portrays David rejecting armor and using equipment familiar to him. Some identify features of Goliath’s equipment with Aegean or Philistine examples. Others see a literary combination that includes aspects comparable with later Greek or eastern Mediterranean equipment. The list’s narrative function is to establish the disparity between the figures. Neither interpretation establishes an exact date for its composition.

The word *kîdôn* in 1 Sam 17:6 and Josh 8:18 can be rendered as a javelin or another hand weapon. Some later Hebrew texts appear to use it for a sword. Translation therefore requires attention to the passage rather than assigning one shape in advance. Judg 3:16 describes a short, concealed weapon and Judg 20:16 skilled slingers. First Samuel 13:19–22 depicts a shortage of weapons within a conflict, without documenting a lasting ethnic monopoly over metalwork.

Second Chronicles 26:14–15 lists defensive equipment and describes devices set on towers. Protective screens like those represented in Assyrian art are one proposal. The text supplies too little detail to identify them as a particular kind of machinery. Neh 4:16–18 connects arms with work in a threatened city. Isa 2:4 imagines a reversal in the social use of metal, and Eph 6:14–17 uses armor as a metaphor for the virtues and resources of the community.
