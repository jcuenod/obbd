## Overview

Carchemish was a city on the Euphrates at what is now the border of Türkiye and Syria. Its river crossing made it important to the Hittite Empire, the later kings of Carchemish, and the Assyrian Empire that annexed it. Excavations have uncovered sculpted stone slabs, inscriptions, and parts of the ancient fortifications. The city's capture by Assyria gives context to Isaiah's reference to it. Its later position at the center of an Egyptian and Babylonian contest also explains its place in the histories of Josiah and Nebuchadnezzar.

## Location and Identification

The ancient citadel stands beside the west bank of the Euphrates, near modern Karkamış in Türkiye and Jarablus in Syria. The walled areas extend beyond the citadel, and the international border crosses the site. The crossing linked routes in northern Syria and southeastern Anatolia, giving successive rulers an interest in controlling the city.

## Archaeological History

The British Museum sponsored excavations in 1911–1914 and again in 1920. David Hogarth, Reginald Campbell Thompson, and Leonard Woolley were among the directors, and T. E. Lawrence took part in the early campaigns. A joint Turkish and Italian project began new excavations in 2011. The excavated gateways, city walls, and monumental public spaces show several phases of building and rebuilding.

In the Late Bronze Age, the city was the seat of a royal representative of the [[hittites-and-hethites|Hittite Empire]] who oversaw its interests in Syria. After that empire fell, Carchemish remained the capital of a local dynasty associated with Hittite traditions. The Iron Age city is known for its Luwian inscriptions, written in a script of pictorial signs, and its sculpted orthostats, or upright stone slabs set along the base of walls. The inscriptions and reliefs record royal titles, public works, and the figures honored by the rulers [@anep, figs. 37, 200–201, 461, 500, 618].

Assyrian records say that [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] defeated Pisiri, the king of Carchemish. The city became an Assyrian province in 717 BCE. Later it was again a center of conflict when Egypt and Babylonia fought for control along the Euphrates. Woolley linked the destruction by fire of an excavated building known as House D to the battle of 605 BCE. Egyptian-style objects and seal impressions were found inside. The link between the building's destruction and the battle remains an interpretation, while the Babylonian written account establishes the battle independently [@cos2, 2.118A, 2.118E] [@abc, 99].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 10:9 places Carchemish alongside other cities in a speech that presents Assyria's conquests as grounds for confidence. 2 Chr 35:20 describes Pharaoh Neco heading toward Carchemish when [[josiah|Josiah]] goes out to meet him. Jer 46:2–12 places a later Egyptian defeat at Carchemish, dated to the fourth year of Jehoiakim. The [[nebuchadnezzar-chronicle|Babylonian Chronicle]] dates the [[battle-of-carchemish|battle]] to 605 BCE. The recurring place name reflects the importance of the Euphrates crossing across distinct episodes. In 2 Kgs 24:7 the Euphrates marks the northern limit of a region formerly under Egyptian control and now held by Babylon. Carchemish thus helps locate the wider political shift described by Kings.
