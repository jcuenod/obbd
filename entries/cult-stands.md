## Overview

Cult stands were supports or stand-shaped objects used in worship. In the Late Bronze and Iron Ages, artisans around the eastern Mediterranean made examples in bronze and pottery. Some bronze stands from Cyprus could be moved on wheels and held vessels. In the Levant, decorated clay stands had openings and were sometimes shaped like small buildings. The Cypriot objects offer a comparison for the wheeled basin stands described in Solomon's temple. The pottery finds give evidence for other kinds of ritual furniture, including some whose use and imagery remain debated.

## Archaeological Evidence

Bronze stands from Late Bronze and early Iron Age Cyprus include four-sided frames with openwork decoration, meaning designs cast or cut with openings between their elements. Some stood on wheels and supported a vessel at the top. Their panels include animals and figures set within frames. These features provide a close comparison for the combination of wheels, panels, and basins in Kings. The Cypriot evidence establishes that such supports were part of the region's bronze-working tradition. [@anep]

Clay stands have been found at Taanach, Pella, Tel Rehov, and Yavneh. Some have openings like windows or doors, and some are decorated with animals, trees, or figures. The better-known [[taanach-cult-stand|Taanach cult stand]] arranges its decoration in horizontal bands. Readings that associate the female figure and other features with Asherah or that take an empty space to represent YHWH remain debated. The drawings and figures carry no inscriptions identifying particular gods. [@neaehl, 4:1431–1432, 5:2071–2073] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 150–160]

The uses of these ceramic objects may have varied. Some forms could support a bowl or other item, whereas others look like models of shrines. Findspots in rooms used for worship or among deposits of ritual objects support a religious association in particular cases. Archaeologists use “cult stand” as a broad label for several forms whose precise functions are not always recoverable. [@neaehl, 4:1431–1432, 5:2071–2073]

## In the Bible

According to 1 Kgs 7:13–14, a craftsman named Hiram came from Tyre to work in bronze for Solomon. He was a different figure from the king of Tyre of the same name. The account describes ten bronze stands with four wheels each, framed panels, and figures of lions, oxen, and [[cherubim]] (1 Kgs 7:27–37). Rare technical words make the details of their construction difficult to reconstruct. The similarities to the Cypriot stands suggest a shared regional repertoire, consistent with the account's association with Tyrian craftsmanship. [@hurowitz-exalted-house, 224–259]

In 1 Kgs 7:38–39 the ten stands support basins placed around the temple. The larger [[bronze-sea|bronze sea]] is a separate furnishing. 2 Chr 4:6 assigns the basins a role in washing materials for burnt offerings. Kings describes later changes to the stands under Ahaz (2 Kgs 16:17) and their destruction or removal after the Babylonian conquest (2 Kgs 25:13, 16). The surviving objects therefore provide comparisons for furnishings known in Jerusalem through literary descriptions.
