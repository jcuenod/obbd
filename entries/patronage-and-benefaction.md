## Overview

Patronage, benefaction, and gift exchange helped structure relationships in Jewish and Greco-Roman communities. Individuals sought protection and access through people with resources. Cities and associations honored donors who paid for buildings, festivals, or other common needs. Gifts could also express friendship and partnership. These practices give context for the requests, titles, and exchanges described in the Gospels and Paul's letters. The expectations attached to a gift depended on the people involved and the setting in which it was given.

## Jewish and Greco-Roman Evidence

A patron could provide protection, introductions, or material support over time. A civic benefactor could give to a city or smaller community and receive public honors in return. A gift between friends might also invite a response, even without a formal relationship. Seneca's *On Benefits* reflects Roman moral debate about such obligations. It distinguishes the benefit from the thing handed over and emphasizes the giver's intention [@seneca-benefits, 1.5–6].

In Berenike in Cyrenaica, a Judean corporate body honored Decimus Valerius Dionysios for paying to improve a building. Its decree calls for recurring public honors and records his exemption from certain civic obligations. The gift and the honors were both visible to the wider community. The [[berenike-judean-benefactor-decree]] is one example of the practices also documented by [[greco-roman-associations|associations]] [@agrw-305].

## In the Bible

The elders in Luke 7:1–5 ask Jesus to help a centurion and cite his support for their synagogue. They act as intermediaries and present his gift as a reason to grant their request. Luke 14:12–14 takes a different approach to reciprocal obligation, instructing hosts to invite people who cannot repay them. Luke 22:25–27 connects the title “benefactors” with rulers and contrasts royal status with service among the disciples.

In Rom 16:1–2 Paul asks the Roman believers to assist Phoebe and describes her as a *prostatis* of many, including himself. The word can convey protection, assistance, or patronage. The text leaves her precise legal and social position open. In 1 Cor 9:1–18 Paul defends his right to receive support while explaining why he has chosen to forgo it. In Phil 4:10–20 he acknowledges a gift in language of shared participation, giving, and receiving.

## Interpretive Significance

Public honors, personal protection, and gifts among friends created different kinds of obligations. Locating an exchange within those practices can clarify the expectations that a biblical text uses or challenges. The particular terms and circumstances of each exchange remain important, especially when inferring a patron's formal status or the ties created by a gift.
