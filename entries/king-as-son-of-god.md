## Overview

Biblical texts sometimes portray the Davidic king as God’s son. The language expresses a special relationship involving authority, protection and responsibility. It has a broader ancient Near Eastern setting in Egyptian accounts of divine royal birth and Mesopotamian claims of divine parentage or nursing. Several Hebrew texts relate sonship to the king’s appointment or to God’s promises to David’s dynasty. Later Jewish texts apply such language in new settings, and the New Testament uses it in its presentation of Jesus.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian kings used the title Son of Re. Temple scenes at Deir el-Bahari and Luxor present Amun as the divine father of the ruler and depict the making or birth of the royal child. These images express the legitimacy of the pharaoh within the Egyptian divine order. Mesopotamian royal texts could also describe gods as parents and goddesses as nurses of kings. Assyrian portrayals of divine maternal care associate protection and successful rule with the deity’s continuing support. [@anep] [@cos2] [@saa9]

Royal sonship also has a West Semitic setting. The Ugaritic poem about [[kirta-epic|Kirta]] gives the king a filial relation to El and asks how a figure so closely connected with the god can die. Such evidence shows a range of uses for family language, from claims about a king’s birth to poetic descriptions of divine favor. Everyday legal practices of [[adoption]] provide another context for expressions of filial status. [@parker-unp] [@roth-law]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Ps 2:7 the king recites a divine decree declaring him a son on that day. This is often read as royal installation expressed in terms analogous to adoption. The promise in 2 Sam 7:14 associates sonship with the Davidic covenant and allows for discipline if the king does wrong. 1 Chr 17:13 reiterates the relationship within its own account of the promise. Ps 89:26–27 (MT 89:27–28) gives the king the language of a son addressing a father and depicts God making him the firstborn, superior to other kings. [@abd] [@ddd]

Several royal texts remain difficult. The ancient Greek version of Ps 110:3 explicitly recalls begetting before the morning star, while the Hebrew wording is obscure. Isa 9:6 (MT 9:5) presents royal titles whose relationship to Egyptian titulary has been debated. How the address in Ps 45:6 (MT 45:7) applies language for God to the royal figure is also disputed. Arguments about any one of these passages depend on wording and context as well as on ancient parallels. [@abd] [@ddd]

## Interpretive Significance

The [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] include 4Q174, which interprets the promise to David in relation to a future figure. Another text, 4Q246, speaks of a figure called son of God and son of the Most High. The identity and role of that figure are debated. Mark 1:11 uses filial language in the heavenly address to Jesus at his baptism, and Heb 1:5 applies both the psalm and the promise concerning David’s heir to him. These applications show how royal texts continued to shape the language of authority and expectation after the monarchy. [@abd]
