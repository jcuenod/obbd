## Overview

Fasting, or deliberately going without food, appears in the Hebrew Bible and early Jewish and Christian literature as a way to mark grief, seek help, or prepare for an encounter with God. Some fasts also restrict drink, and many accompany prayer or acts such as wearing sackcloth. Biblical examples range from a single person's response to illness to a whole community's appeal in a crisis. Later traditions specify regular days and additional forms of abstinence. The range of evidence helps explain why the same practice can signify distress, petition, or communal identity in different passages.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Greek historian Herodotus describes Egyptians fasting in preparation for a sacrifice and mourning during the rite (Histories 2.40). In a different episode, Persian troops extend their mourning for a dead commander to their horses and pack animals by cutting their manes (Histories 9.24). The latter is a comparison for involving animals in a human rite. Herodotus does not describe the animals as fasting. Mesopotamian lists of favorable and unfavorable days also record restrictions on particular foods [@livingstone-hemerologies].

## In the Hebrew Bible

The men of Jabesh fast for seven days after burying Saul (1 Sam 31:13). David fasts while his child is ill and stops after the child's death, showing that his act was an appeal while the outcome remained open (2 Sam 12:16–23). Joel calls for a communal fast during a crisis (Joel 2:12–17). In Jonah 3:5–9, the king's decree extends abstinence and signs of distress to animals as well as humans.

A fast can accompany confession, as it does at Mizpah (1 Sam 7:6). In Dan 9:3 the prophet turns to God with prayer and fasting, while Dan 10:2–3 depicts another period of mourning and abstention from rich foods before a vision. The combination of gestures varies. Isa 58:3–7 criticizes people who fast while oppressing others and describes releasing those they mistreat as the kind of fast God chooses.

The law for the [[day-of-atonement-elimination|Day of Atonement]] tells participants to afflict themselves (Lev 16:29–31; 23:27–32). It does not list acts of abstinence. The later Mishnah interprets the requirement as excluding food and drink, bathing, use of oil on the body, wearing shoes, and sexual relations (Yoma 8:1). Zech 7:3–5 and 8:19 refer to recurring fasts connected with the experience of national disaster and restoration.

## Interpretive Significance

David A. Lambert argues that fasting in many biblical texts makes distress visible and accompanies an appeal, while an emphasis on inward repentance reflects later readings. In several narratives fasting is clearly joined to acknowledgment of wrongdoing. Its meaning in a given story still depends on the other actions and words that accompany it. The comparison with [[mourning-rites|mourning rites]] clarifies why visual signs of grief and petition can coincide.

Luke 18:12 depicts someone who fasts twice a week, and Mark 2:18–20 portrays a dispute over when Jesus' disciples should fast. The early Christian manual called the Didache prescribes days for its readers that differ from those of a group it criticizes (Didache 8:1). Matt 6:16–18 advises fasting without making it apparent to other people. Together these texts show debates about timing, audience, and the place of fasting in a community's life.
