## Overview

A footstool was part of royal seating in ancient Egypt and the Levant. Placed beneath a throne, it could display an image of the ruler's enemies so that putting one's feet on it enacted their defeat. Biblical texts apply this image to a king's enemies, to the sanctuary and ark, and to the earth beneath God's throne. The uses share a spatial idea but make different claims about rule. Archaeological evidence from Egyptian royal furniture helps explain the force of the image, while the biblical passages show how it could express worship, victory, and the reach of divine sovereignty.

## Archaeological Evidence

Egyptian royal furniture made the relationship between a seated ruler and his subjects or enemies visible. A [[tutankhamun-footstool|footstool from Tutankhamun's tomb]] bears images of bound foreign captives. Some examples use the Nine Bows, an Egyptian emblem for enemies of the realm. Comparable imagery occurs on sandals and surfaces meant to be stepped on. Placing the feet above such images made domination part of the use of the object [@anep] [@keel-symbolism].

Levantine thrones are also known from carved furniture and representations in art. Some are flanked by winged creatures. The [[cherub-throne|cherub throne]] associated with the God of Israel belongs to this wider history of images of rule. An actual footstool and the metaphor of something beneath divine feet need to be interpreted in their particular settings.

## In the Bible

2 Chr 9:18 describes a golden footstool attached to Solomon's throne. In Ps 110:1 the speaker promises that the ruler's enemies will become his footstool. The Egyptian objects offer a close comparison because they put representations of enemies in exactly that position. The psalm uses the image to express victory granted by God, while Heb 1:13 applies the same words to Christ.

The footstool can also mark the place of divine presence. Ps 99:5 and 132:7 call worshipers to bow at God's footstool. In 1 Chr 28:2 the [[ark-of-the-covenant|ark of the covenant]] and God's footstool are closely associated in David's account of the temple he intended to build. Lam 2:1 laments that God did not remember his footstool during judgment on Zion. These passages use the image to describe a place below the divine throne, either as an object of reverence or as a sign of a relationship shattered by destruction.

Isa 66:1 shifts the image to the scale of the world. The heavens are God's throne, and the earth is his footstool. The prophet then asks what house could be built for such a God, giving the image force in a passage concerned with the limits of a sanctuary. Matt 5:35 recalls the same image in a warning about oaths. The difference between local worship and cosmic rule helps explain why the footstool may refer to different things without losing its association with the space below a throne.
