## Overview

Divination is the attempt to obtain knowledge from a deity or from signs understood to carry divine meaning. In the ancient Near East it could involve inspecting the body of a sacrificial animal, studying the sky, casting lots, receiving a dream, or consulting a prophet. Mesopotamian records show how specialists used such methods in royal service. The Bible rejects several forms of divination while recognizing other ways of seeking divine guidance. These distinctions help explain why practices that all seek hidden knowledge receive different treatment across biblical texts.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Ancient writers could distinguish methods that interpret observable signs from those that receive an inspired message. The Roman writer Cicero used a version of this distinction much later. It is useful for comparing Mesopotamian, Levantine, and biblical texts, although practices could overlap. A dream, for example, might be experienced without preparation but require an expert to interpret it.

Mesopotamian scholars recorded signs in large collections of omens. Entries often state what a sign looks like and what outcome it may indicate. [[extispicy|Extispicy]], the inspection of a sacrificed animal's internal organs, could be used to address questions about royal affairs. [[astrology|Celestial divination]] used events such as eclipses as indications relevant to a king or country. Other omen collections dealt with unusual events or such things as oil on water. The number and variety of texts show how much trained labor court divination could command [@cos1, 1.120] [@horowitz-cosmic-geography, 150–169].

By contrast, a prophet could announce a message received from a god. Letters from Mari describe such messages reaching the king, and Neo-Assyrian texts preserve oracles for the royal household. Some reports and rituals show several sources of information used together. Officials could consult experts about an unusual dream or seek confirmation of a report from a prophet [@nissinen-prophets, 1–10] [@saa9, no. 1].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 18:9–14 lists practices Israel is to avoid. The Hebrew terms include *qesem* (“divination”), forms of *nāḥaš* (“interpret an omen”), *ʿônēn* (a prognosticator), and *mĕkaššēp* (“sorcerer”). A *ḥōbēr ḥeber* probably performs a charm or binding spell, while a *šōʾēl ʾôb* consults a spirit or someone who mediates contact with the dead. Several of these terms remain difficult to match to a particular ancient technique. Lev 19:26, 31 prohibits other practices described in related terms, including consultation with mediums.

In the next part of Deuteronomy, Moses promises that God will raise up a prophet the people should hear and gives criteria for assessing a prophetic claim (Deut 18:15–22). That sequence makes the source and authorization of knowledge central to the distinction. Elsewhere, Num 27:21 provides for inquiry by the priest using the [[urim-and-thummim|Urim]]. Lev 16:8 gives a ritual role to [[lots-and-cleromancy|lots]]. In 1 Sam 28:6–7 Saul receives no answer by dreams, Urim, or prophets and then seeks a woman who consults the dead. The narrative distinguishes the avenues he turns to.

## Interpretive Significance

Ezek 21:21 (MT 21:26) portrays the king of Babylon shaking arrows, consulting *teraphim*, or household images, and inspecting a liver before deciding where to advance. The passage brings familiar practices into a prophetic depiction of events. Gen 44:5, 15 describes Joseph as using a cup for divination within a story in which he conceals his identity and puts his brothers to the test. The episode does not specify the exact method, such as whether the cup held a liquid to be observed.

The status of a method could therefore depend on who sought the answer, what authority the text gave the source, and how the scene served the narrative. The [[prophecy-in-the-ane|prophetic message]] and the interpreted omen belonged to a wider field of human approaches to divine knowledge. Biblical writers draw boundaries within that field according to the claims about divine authority they are making.
