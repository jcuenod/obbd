## Overview

Phoenicians is the conventional name for the inhabitants of Iron Age coastal cities such as Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, and Arwad and for people who established related communities around the Mediterranean. These cities shared features of language, writing, crafts, and religion while remaining politically distinct. Their trade linked the Levant with Cyprus, North Africa, and much of the western Mediterranean. Biblical texts portray their rulers and craftspeople as partners and sometimes rivals, and prophetic accounts of Tyre use its wealth and reach to explore the limits of political and commercial power.

## Sources

Phoenician inscriptions include royal and dedicatory texts from [[byblos|Byblos]], the Phoenician portion of the Karatepe bilingual, and the inscription of Kilamuwa at Zincirli. Some were produced away from the Phoenician coast. The language belongs to the Canaanite branch of Northwest Semitic and is closely related to Hebrew. Its alphabetic script became an important source for the Greek alphabet, in which some letter signs were adapted to represent vowels. The inscriptions are also essential evidence for distinguishing Phoenician, Hebrew, and Aramaic writing traditions.

“Phoenician” comes from Greek usage. Inscriptions and other accounts more often identify people by their city or kingdom. Much later, Augustine reports an association between a North African Punic-speaking population and the name Canaanite. His testimony concerns a late western setting. Evidence for individual city affiliations is stronger than evidence for a uniform self-designation across the Iron Age world.

## History

Coastal cities used different strategies to maintain influence between larger territorial powers. [[tyre|Tyre]] and [[sidon|Sidon]] could cooperate or compete, and empires demanded tribute or intervened in their affairs. [[phoenician-seafaring|Maritime networks]] linked eastern ports and new western settlements. Dates for settlement at Kition, Carthage, Sardinia, and Iberia vary with the kind of evidence used. Radiocarbon samples and pottery often date activities at a site more securely than they date a formal foundation.

Phoenician artisans produced metalwork, carved ivories, textiles, and other goods that moved between courts. Religious traditions also varied by city. [[melqart|Melqart]] is especially associated with Tyre, Eshmun with Sidon, and Astarte with several centers. The term Baal can function as a title, meaning “lord,” so its occurrence need not name the same local god in every text. Western sites known as tophets contain urns with cremated remains. Their interpretation and the role of sacrifice remain debated and cannot automatically be transferred to every eastern city.

## In the Bible

First Kings 5:1–12 depicts cooperation between Solomon and Hiram of Tyre for timber and labor, and 9:26–28 portrays cooperation at sea. The craftsman named Hiram in 1 Kgs 7:13–14 supplies another connection with Tyre and must be distinguished from the king. The stories make coastal skill and long-distance supply part of the explanation for royal construction. First Kings 16:31–33 connects a royal marriage involving Jezebel with worship of Baal in Israel.

Isa 23 and Ezek 27 portray Tyre’s commercial reach within oracles of loss. Ezek 28:1–19 directs criticism at its ruler and king, using images of extraordinary wealth and pride. Joel 3:4–6 (MT 4:4–6) charges Tyre, Sidon, and the regions of Philistia with taking valuables and selling people. The passages provide evidence for how writers represented the political and commercial power of the coast. Mark 7:26 uses a Syrophoenician designation for a woman in the Tyrian region, and Acts 11:19 names Phoenicia as a region reached by people traveling from Judea. These are later uses of geographical and cultural terms under Rome.
