## Overview

A ziggurat was a stepped temple tower in Mesopotamia. Kings built and renewed these monuments from the late third millennium BCE through the Neo-Babylonian period. Their terraces, stairways and high sanctuaries made them conspicuous features of urban sacred precincts. Archaeology preserves their lower structures more often than their summits. The temple towers provide a setting for the building materials and height of the tower in Genesis 11. Their relationship to other biblical images of heavenward ascent remains a matter of interpretation.

## Archaeological Evidence

Raised temples preceded the monumental ziggurats of the Third Dynasty of Ur. At [[ur|Ur]], the surviving tower begun under Ur-Nammu about 2100 BCE had a mud-brick core, baked-brick facing and monumental stairs that meet at the first terrace. The stairways gave the tower a prominent approach within the sacred precinct. The complex belonged to Nanna, the city's moon god. Its upper stages and summit sanctuary are reconstructed from limited remains and comparative evidence. The exposed structure also incorporates later building and modern reconstruction. [@anep, figs. 746–748] [@abd, 1:564–566, 6:374–376]

Other cities had towers of different dimensions and plans. Royal inscriptions name them in connection with gods and sanctuaries. Nebuchadnezzar II restored the ziggurat of Nabû at Borsippa, and [[etemenanki|Etemenanki]] belonged to Marduk's precinct at Babylon. Building accounts and surveys of temples can supply evidence for upper stages where the masonry no longer survives. Mud-brick cores made large volumes of building possible, while outer layers of fired brick and bitumen could protect them from weather. [@cos2, 2.122B] [@george-tower-of-babel, 75–85]

The evidence places ziggurats in temple complexes and associates them with cult and royal building. Their elevation and names sometimes evoke the relation of earth and heaven. Claims that they were designed as royal tombs or astronomical observatories lack secure evidence. Practical uses of particular terraces and access to the upper sanctuaries are less well documented than the fabric itself. [@horowitz-cosmic-geography, 287–289] [@abd, 1:564–566, 6:374–376]

## In the Bible

Gen 11:1–9 locates its tower in Shinar and describes brick and bitumen construction. Height approaching heaven and the location in southern Mesopotamia make ziggurats an apt [[ziggurat-and-babel|comparison for Babel]]. Gen 28:12 pictures a *sullām* reaching from earth to heaven. Its meaning may be stairway or ramp, and comparison with a ziggurat's access is possible. Gen 28:17 calls the location God's house and heaven's gate. The wording has invited comparison with the traditional Babylonian explanation of the name *Bāb-ili*, “gate of the god.” [@abd, 1:561–562] [@george-tower-of-babel, 75–80]

Ezek 43:13–17 describes an altar in tiers. William F. Albright and others compared its obscure terms for parts of the structure, including *ḥêq hāʾāreṣ* and *harʾēl*, with Mesopotamian temple-tower terminology. The vocabulary and derivations remain disputed. Isa 14:13 imagines ascent to the mountain of assembly, using a wider ancient association of height with divine rule. [@abd] [@horowitz-cosmic-geography]
