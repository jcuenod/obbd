## Overview

The Victory Stele of Naram-Sin shows an Akkadian king ascending a mountain above defeated enemies. A horned crown associates him with divine status, while celestial signs appear above him. The stele was carved in the late third millennium BCE and later taken to Susa by an Elamite king, who added an inscription. Its history illustrates how royal monuments could assert victory more than once. Its imagery also provides an early comparison for biblical descriptions of royal ascent, divine claims, and enemies beneath a ruler's feet.

## Description and Provenance

The pink limestone stele was found by the French archaeological mission at [[susa|Susa]] in 1898 and is now in the Louvre (SB 4). Its original inscription celebrates an Akkadian victory over the Lullubi of the Zagros. A later inscription by the Elamite ruler Shutruk-Nahhunte records taking the stone to Elam as booty. The later text gives the monument a new political setting while preserving the picture commissioned for Naram-Sin.

## Contents

Naram-Sin appears larger than other figures on the stele. He climbs the slope at the head of his soldiers, above people shown falling, pleading, or lying defeated. He wears a horned crown, an attribute usually associated with divinity in Mesopotamian art. Celestial signs above the scene locate the victory within a wider divine order. Royal inscriptions from his reign also use a sign before his name that identifies him as divine.

The design directs attention to the king's ascent and the different positions of victors and defeated peoples. Its scale, placement, and attributes all express a claim about the king's exceptional standing. The Elamite seizure later made the object itself a sign of changed fortunes.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 14:13–14 pictures a ruler aspiring to climb the heights and be like the Most High. Ezek 28:2 condemns the ruler of Tyre for claiming divinity, and Ezek 28:14–16 describes exaltation and expulsion in imagery associated with God's holy mountain. These passages criticize claims to elevation and divine status. The stele shows how some ancient kings could express related claims positively in official art.

Josh 10:24 places the feet of victors on the necks of defeated kings. Ps 110:1 uses enemies as a [[footstool|footstool]], and Ps 18:37–42 (MT 18:38–43) describes a royal speaker pursuing and crushing foes. The defeated figures under the ascending king on the stele give a visual comparison for the language of subjection. Comparable imagery appears in other regions and periods.

## Scholarly Issues

Later Mesopotamian literature could remember Naram-Sin differently. The Curse of Agade portrays him offending the gods and connects the city's disaster with his conduct. Such a portrayal is a literary judgment on a king, whereas the stele represents the king's victory. The differences between the Akkadian object, its later appropriation, and biblical poetry help show the range of meanings given to royal power.
