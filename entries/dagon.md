## Overview

Dagon is the biblical form of the name Dagan, an important god of the middle Euphrates region and inland Syria during the Bronze Age. Texts record his worship at several cities and name him as Baal's father in literature from Ugarit. In the Bible, Dagon is associated with Philistine temples, especially at Ashdod and Gaza. The earlier Syrian evidence helps explain the long history behind this name. It also provides a better basis for understanding the deity than the later interpretation of Dagon as a god with the body of a fish.

## Sources and Iconography

Dagan appears in third-millennium BCE records from Ebla and Mari and in later evidence from Mari, Terqa, Tuttul, and Emar. He was a prominent god in the middle Euphrates region, where rulers acknowledged him and communities supported offerings and sanctuaries. Documents associated with the [[mari-letters|Mari archives]] and the [[emar-texts|Emar texts]] reflect different local traditions. The sources show his standing more clearly than they define one exclusive function. [@ddd] [@cos1]

At Late Bronze Age [[ugarit|Ugarit]], Baal is called son of Dagan in literary texts, though Dagan has little direct role in their action. Two inscribed stelae name Dagan in connection with offerings. One of the temples on the acropolis has often been assigned to him on archaeological and inscriptional grounds, though the identification involves inference from the finds. Securely identified portraits of the god are rare. [@ktu3] [@parker-unp]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Judg 16:23 depicts the rulers of the [[philistines|Philistines]] gathering to sacrifice to Dagon and crediting him with capturing Samson. In 1 Sam 5:1–5 they place the captured [[ark-of-the-covenant|ark]] in Dagon's temple at [[ashdod|Ashdod]]. The statue falls before it and is found broken at the threshold. The story explains a practice of avoiding the threshold and presents the two objects as representing competing claims to divine power.

In 1 Chr 10:10, the Philistines display Saul's head in Dagon's temple, but the town is unnamed. The later narrative in 1 Macc 10:83–84 reports the burning of a temple of Dagon at Azotus, the Greek form of Ashdod. These passages preserve associations of Dagon with major coastal settlements. They offer little evidence about his exact standing across all Philistine communities or about the form of his image. [@abd] [@ddd]

## Scholarly Issues

The god's name has been connected with a West Semitic word for grain and, alternatively, with a root associated with cloudy or rainy weather. Philo of Byblos associated Dagon with grain and the plow. That testimony is much later than the Bronze Age sources and does not settle the original meaning. The association with Hebrew *dāg* (“fish”) has little support in the early evidence. Assyrian [[apkallu-reliefs|fish-cloaked protective figures]] belong to a distinct tradition of guardian figures. [@ddd]

The presence of a god with an old West Semitic name in biblical accounts of Philistine worship is consistent with the adoption or retention of local traditions in the southern coastal plain. The surviving evidence gives too little detail to date that process or reconstruct a direct line of transmission from a particular Syrian sanctuary. [@ddd]
