## Overview

Ashdod was an ancient city on the southern coastal plain of the Levant and one of the principal cities of the Philistines in the Iron Age. Its inland mound, Tel Ashdod, should be distinguished from the coastal site of Ashdod-Yam. Excavations found pottery linked to Aegean traditions, and fragments of an Assyrian monument record the city's place in the empire of Sargon II. The Bible knows it as the city where the Philistines took the ark, as a target of Assyrian conquest, and, under the Greek name Azotus, as a stop on Philip's journey.

## Location and Identification

Tel Ashdod lies inland from the Mediterranean, south of the modern city. Ashdod-Yam, meaning “Ashdod by the Sea,” had its own settlement and fortifications. Assyrian texts name both places in their account of a single campaign. The coastal settlement grew in importance over time, and in later periods there were inland and maritime sites associated with the name Azotus [@neaehl, 1:93–102].

## Archaeological History

Excavations directed by Moshe Dothan found early Iron Age layers with locally made pottery in shapes and painted styles related to those of the Aegean. Archaeologists call one of the early styles Mycenaean IIIC after related vessels in the Aegean. A later layer yielded the clay figure nicknamed Ashdoda, a seated woman whose body and chair are joined. The objects help situate Ashdod within wider patterns of settlement and exchange among the [[philistines|Philistines]], although the figurine's identity remains open [@neaehl, 1:93–102] [@mazar-archaeology, 300–318].

Assyrian accounts describe how Yamani took power in Ashdod and fled as Sargon II's forces approached. The texts report the capture of Ashdod and Ashdod-Yam and the installation of an Assyrian governor. Fragments of a basalt monument erected by Sargon at Tel Ashdod add local evidence for the event and its commemoration. The campaign is generally dated to 711 BCE, with 712 BCE also proposed from another Assyrian account [@cos2, 2.118A, 2.118E, 2.118J].

Ashdod continued to designate a community or district in the Persian period. Its exact administrative status then is less clear than its earlier status under Assyria. In the Hellenistic period Ashdod-Yam had substantial construction, and ancient texts continue to name Azotus through the Roman period. The name can require care when a text gives no detail distinguishing the port from the inland town.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 13:3 counts the Ashdodites among the five Philistine lordships. In 1 Sam 5:1–7, the captured ark is brought into the temple of [[dagon|Dagon]] in Ashdod, where the god's image falls before it. A later narrative likewise describes a temple of Dagon at Azotus and attributes its burning to Jonathan (1 Macc 10:84). No building at Tel Ashdod has been securely identified as Dagon's temple, and the accounts describe events separated by centuries.

Isa 20:1 dates its episode to the year when the commander sent by Sargon took Ashdod. The [[sargon-ii-inscriptions|Assyrian records of Sargon]] independently place the conquest within his reign. Neh 13:23–24, by contrast, uses Ashdod to identify families and a way of speaking in the Persian period, without specifying the language further. Acts 8:40 places Philip at Azotus before he continues northward to Caesarea.
