## Overview

The *Satire of the Trades*, also called the *Instruction of Khety*, is an Egyptian composition about a father taking his son to learn to write. It probably originated in the Middle Kingdom and is best preserved in New Kingdom copies. Khety gives exaggerated accounts of the hardships of manual occupations to commend the scribal profession. It offers a comparison for Ben Sira's contrast between artisans and scribes, especially where both works connect writing with the opportunity to attain a valued form of expertise.

## Manuscripts and Date

Papyrus Sallier II preserves the fullest copy, and Papyrus Anastasi VII and a number of other witnesses preserve parts of the composition. Both papyri are now in the British Museum. The numerous copies on ostraca, fragments of pottery or stone used for writing, show that scribes repeatedly copied the text in the New Kingdom. The story is commonly assigned to the Middle Kingdom, but the date of the surviving manuscripts is later. Scribal omissions, varying readings and corrupt passages sometimes make the wording difficult to recover. [@ael, 1:184–192] [@cos1, 1.48] [@anet, 432–434]

## Contents

Khety son of Duauf addresses his son Pepy while taking him to the Residence to enter a school for writing. The father catalogs occupations and describes bodily discomfort, danger, exhausting work and subjection to others. A metalworker labors in the heat, a potter is filthy from clay, and the work of a fisherman involves danger on the water. The cumulative list praises the status and relative independence associated with [[scribes-and-scribal-education|scribal work]]. Khety also offers instruction on proper conduct, so the attraction of the occupation is paired with advice on how to carry oneself. [@ael, 1:184–192] [@cos1, 1.48]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Sir 38:24–34 explains why farmers, artisans, smiths and potters have little time for a scribe's pursuit of wisdom. These occupations demand attention and skill. Ben Sira adds that artisans sustain the city, even though they are unlikely to sit in judgment or become known for instruction. Sir 39:1–11 presents the scribe as someone who studies Torah and inherited wisdom, serves among rulers and prays for understanding. The contrast gives scribal study public and religious purposes while acknowledging the dependence of public life on the trades. [@abd] [@ael, 1:184–192]

## Scholarly Issues

The traditional title *Satire* reflects the exaggeration of the occupational sketches. Interpreters differ over the balance of humor and exhortation, and repeated copying documents the work's later pedagogical value more directly than its original purpose. The Egyptian and Jewish compositions share a literary way of valuing scribal work through comparison with other occupations. Contacts between Egypt and Judaea make knowledge of Egyptian tradition possible, while the surviving similarities also fit a widely shared scribal outlook. Direct knowledge of this particular composition by Ben Sira remains debated. [@ael, 1:184–192] [@cos1, 1.48] [@abd]
