## Overview

The Nebuchadnezzar Chronicle is a Babylonian clay tablet recording campaigns from the last year of Nabopolassar into the early reign of Nebuchadnezzar II. It describes the victory at Carchemish, expeditions to the Levant, and the capture of the city it calls the city of Judah. By dating that capture to the second day of Adar in the king's seventh regnal year, the tablet gives historians a precise date in March 597 BCE for Jerusalem's surrender. The record provides a close comparison with Kings, Chronicles, and Jeremiah.

## Description and Provenance

The tablet is held by the British Museum (BM 21946). D. J. Wiseman published it in 1956, and it appears as Chronicle 5 in A. K. Grayson's edition. Like other [[babylonian-chronicles|Babylonian chronicles]], it organizes episodes by year and gives particular dates for some events. It is incomplete, and its period of coverage ends in the king's early years. The date at which the surviving copy was written cannot be fixed securely.

## Contents

In Nabopolassar's twenty-first year, the future [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] defeats the Egyptian army at [[battle-of-carchemish|Carchemish]] in 605 BCE. He returns to Babylon when his father dies and takes the throne. The tablet then records further campaigns in the western territories, including the destruction of Ashkelon in Kislev in 604 BCE. An engagement with Egypt in 601 BCE results in heavy losses and is followed by a period when the Babylonians rebuild their military resources.

In the seventh regnal year, the king marches west and besieges the city of Judah. On 2 Adar he captures the city and its king, sets a king of his choice in place, and takes substantial tribute to Babylon. The entry refers to the two rulers by position. The biblical accounts supply the names Jehoiachin and Zedekiah.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 24:10–17 describes [[jehoiachin|Jehoiachin's]] surrender and deportation and the appointment of his uncle as king, with the name Zedekiah. 2 Chr 36:10 also describes the transfer of the king and temple valuables to Babylon. The tablet and biblical accounts agree about the major change of ruler. 2 Kgs 24:12 dates the capture to the eighth year, whereas Jer 52:28 records a deportation in the seventh year. A common explanation is that the records count the accession year differently.

Jer 46:2 places the Egyptian defeat at Carchemish during Jehoiakim's fourth year. Jer 47:5–7 describes suffering in connection with Ashkelon, a city whose destruction the chronicle records. The clash of 601 BCE provides a possible historical setting for Jehoiakim's change of allegiance (2 Kgs 24:1). This last connection is inferred from the order of the campaigns rather than stated by the chronicle.

## Scholarly Issues

Dan 1:1–2 describes a siege of Jerusalem in Jehoiakim's third year. The chronicle mentions western operations in the period around 605 BCE without recording a siege of Jerusalem then. Its entry for 597 BCE is much more specific. The broken and selective record leaves room for events it does not describe, but provides no independent confirmation of an earlier siege. Its surviving text also ends before Jerusalem's later destruction in 587/586 BCE.
