## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern kings sometimes called themselves shepherds because their office combined care, authority, and responsibility for others. Mesopotamian royal texts use the title, and Egyptian royal imagery uses the shepherd’s crook. Biblical texts apply the image to David, to other rulers, and to YHWH. They also criticize leaders who use the flock for their own benefit. This background helps readers see why a shepherd can convey political authority, practical care, and accountability in the same passage.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Sumerian *sipa* and Akkadian *rēʾû*, both meaning "shepherd," became standard royal epithets, but they appear in the earliest royal inscriptions less often than is usually assumed. Old Sumerian examples are rare, and the royal inscriptions of the Sargonic kings of Akkad do not use the image at all [@westenholz-good-shepherd, 283–86]. [[gudea|Gudea]] of Lagash, in the late third millennium BCE, is the first ruler known to call himself a shepherd. His [[statue-of-gudea|Statue B]] names him the "shepherd chosen in the heart of Ningirsu" and says that the god chose him as "legitimate shepherd (*sipa zi*) in the land" (ii 8; iii 6–9) [@rime3-1, 31–32]. After Gudea the epithet became routine. Shulgi of Ur is the "faithful shepherd of Sumer," and Lipit-Ishtar of Isin the "humble shepherd of Nippur" [@westenholz-good-shepherd, 287–89].

The [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] use the title in both the prologue and the epilogue. The prologue begins its list of the king's deeds with "Hammurabi, the shepherd, called by Enlil, am I" (i 50ff.) and later calls him "shepherd of the people" (iv 45) [@roth-law] [@westenholz-good-shepherd, 291]. The epilogue develops the image. Hammurabi says he has not neglected "the black-headed (people)" whose shepherding Marduk entrusted to him, and that he made the people "lie down in pastures." He calls himself "the shepherd who provides well-being (*rēʾûm mušallimum*), whose staff is straight" (xlvii 9–21, 35–58). The straight staff stands for just rule, and the epilogue binds the king's shepherding to the justice the laws claim to establish [@westenholz-good-shepherd, 291–92].

First-millennium kings kept the title. Sargon II calls himself "shepherd of Assyria," and Nebuchadnezzar II "legitimate shepherd" (*rēʾû kīnu*). Assyrian texts also turn the shepherd's equipment toward conquest. An inscription of Sennacherib describes "the just staff that extends the realm, the merciless crook for the destruction of enemies." Gods were called shepherds too. Sumerian hymns call Enlil the "faithful shepherd," and in *Enuma Elish* the gods decree that Marduk will "shepherd the black-headed people" (VI 107). A bilingual proverb sums up the metaphor: "a people without a king (are) as sheep without a shepherd" [@westenholz-good-shepherd, 293–95].

In Egypt the *heqa* sceptre, a shepherd's crook, belonged to royal regalia from the beginnings of kingship, and the crook sign writes the word *ḥqꜣ*, "ruler" [@wilkinson-early-dynastic]. The Instruction for Merikare applies herding language to the creator's care for humanity: "Well tended is mankind—god's cattle" [@ael, 1:106].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 3:1 introduces Moses keeping a flock before his commission. In Num 27:17 Moses asks for a leader whose appointment will keep Israel from becoming like sheep without a shepherd. First Kings 22:17 uses the scattered flock to picture a leader’s loss. First Samuel 16:11 introduces David in his work with sheep. Second Samuel 5:2 makes shepherding Israel part of David’s royal commission, and 7:7–8 links the image of leadership with his selection from the pasture. Ps 78:70–72 similarly moves from his service with animals to his care for the people.

Jacob already speaks of "the God who has shepherded me" (Gen 48:15). Ps 23 presents YHWH providing direction, protection, and food, and the "rod" of 23:4 (*šēbeṭ*) is also the word for a royal sceptre (Ps 45:6, MT 45:7). Ps 80:1 addresses God as Israel’s shepherd. Isa 40:11 uses the image for gathering and carrying vulnerable animals. Isa 44:28 calls Cyrus God’s shepherd in connection with rebuilding. The range of applications makes sense in light of both practical herding and royal rhetoric. Divine shepherding can express rule as well as intimate care.

Ezek 34 accuses leaders of feeding themselves at the flock’s expense. It portrays God intervening and promises a shepherd associated with David. Jer 23:1–6 likewise connects failed shepherds with a future Davidic ruler. Zech 11:4–17 stages loyalty, abandonment, and conflict through the duties of a shepherd. Mic 5:4 pictures a ruler shepherding with divine strength. John 10:1–18 makes care, recognition of the shepherd’s voice, and willingness to die for the sheep central to its presentation of Jesus.

## Interpretive Significance

The comparison with royal language helps explain why a familiar occupation can carry claims about government. Protecting the flock, keeping it together, and accounting for losses become standards for assessing a ruler. Biblical texts use those standards both to praise good leadership and to judge rulers for exploiting the people entrusted to them. Westenholz observes that Mesopotamian royal usage stresses feeding and leading the flock, while guarding it is seldom explicit. The prophets, by contrast, fault Israel's shepherds for letting the sheep become prey (Ezek 34:8), and John 10:11 makes the shepherd's death for the sheep the defining mark of the good shepherd [@westenholz-good-shepherd, 292, 296–97].
