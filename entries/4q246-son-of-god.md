## Overview

4Q246 is a fragmentary Aramaic apocalypse from Qumran Cave 4. It depicts conflict among kingdoms, refers to a figure called “Son of God” and “Son of the Most High,” and describes an enduring kingdom associated with the people of God. The titles offer a close comparison with language used in Luke's account of the announcement to Mary. The scroll provides no surviving name for the figure, whose identity and role in the sequence of kingdoms remain disputed.

## Description and Provenance

Two columns survive in part, with particularly substantial losses in the first. The short text is often called the Aramaic Apocalypse or the Son of God Text. Neither title is preserved as an ancient heading. The manuscript belongs to the Aramaic writings among the [[dead-sea-scrolls]] [@djd22, 4Q246].

## Contents

The fragment moves through political conflict and rule toward lasting dominion. In the second column the titles “Son of God” and “Son of the Most High” occur close together. The people of God then appear in the account of an enduring kingdom. Damage to the text makes it hard to establish how each figure relates to the next figure or group in the vision.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Dan 7:13–14, 27 describes the transfer of lasting rule and assigns the kingdom to the holy ones of the Most High. That sequence supplies a close literary comparison with 4Q246 and its shifting dominions. Luke 1:32–35 applies titles of divine sonship to Jesus and associates him with enduring kingship. The resemblance shows that the language circulated in Jewish settings, with the identity of the figure in the fragment remaining a separate question.

## Scholarly Issues

Interpretations include a hostile human ruler claiming divine status, a favorable royal or messianic figure, and a heavenly representative of a kingdom or people. The relationship of the titled figure to the people of God is also important for readings that give the kingdom a collective focus. Michael Segal proposes that Ps 82:6–8 helps explain a heavenly representative in the vision. That allusion remains disputed. The titles alone cannot decide among these readings [@segal-4q246].
