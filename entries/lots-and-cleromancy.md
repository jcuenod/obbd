## Overview

Casting lots was a way to make decisions and distribute responsibilities in ancient Israel and its neighbors. A lot could assign land, select an official, identify a suspected offender, or determine a ritual role. An inscribed Assyrian cube records the ancient word behind the *pûr* of Esther and the name Purim. Biblical texts sometimes treat such decisions as governed by God. This helps explain why apparently random choices could be binding or carry religious meaning, even when the procedure itself is described only briefly.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

A ninth-century BCE inscribed clay cube from Assyria calls itself the *pūru* (“lot”) of Yaḫalu, a prominent official of Shalmaneser III. Its inscription anticipates his term as eponym, the official whose name would designate a year. The object therefore links casting a lot with an important administrative appointment. The surviving [[assyrian-eponym-list|Assyrian lists of eponyms]] give a separate record of officeholders and do not show that every year was assigned through the same mechanism [@cos2] [@rima3].

## In the Hebrew Bible

The common Hebrew word for a lot is *gôrāl*. Num 26:55–56 provides for distribution of land by lot, and Josh 18:6–10 depicts the procedure before YHWH at Shiloh. In Josh 7:14–18 a process of selection narrows the inquiry to Achan, and in 1 Sam 10:20–21 successive selections identify Saul. Jonah 1:7 presents sailors casting lots to discover why they are in danger. Ps 22:18 (MT 22:19) depicts a more ordinary division of clothing.

Some procedures concern ritual service. Lev 16:8–10 assigns one goat to YHWH and one to Azazel by lot. 1 Chr 24:5, 31 depicts duties allocated by lot, and Luke 1:9 presents Zechariah selected to burn incense. In Acts 1:26 the lot falls to Matthias as a replacement among the apostles. The choices can be pictured as impartial because they occur through a procedure outside the preference of an individual participant.

In 1 Sam 14:41–42 an inquiry falls first on Saul and Jonathan and then on Jonathan. An ancient Greek form of the passage contains a fuller prayer involving the [[urim-and-thummim|Urim and Thummim]]. That reading gives a point of contact between lots and a priestly means of inquiry, whose physical mechanism is unknown. The text does not describe how the objects were manipulated.

## Interpretive Significance

Prov 16:33 assigns the lot's decision to YHWH, showing why casting lots could be treated as a legitimate way of discerning an outcome. Esth 3:7 glosses *pûr* as a lot when Haman seeks a day for his plan, and Esth 9:24–26 relates the word to the name Purim. The Assyrian cube establishes an older Akkadian parallel for the term. The different settings leave the object and precise procedure in Esther uncertain.
