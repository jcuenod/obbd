## Overview

Jezebel was the Phoenician wife of Ahab of Israel and a royal mother during the ninth century BCE. Kings portrays her as an influential patron of worship and an active participant in court politics. Independent sources help explain her Phoenician connections, while the claim that an inscribed seal belonged to her remains unproven. Her story brings together ancient practices of dynastic marriage, administrative sealing and royal spectacle. The biblical depiction also reflects the writers’ judgment of the Omride court and its sponsorship of Baal.

## Extrabiblical Sources

1 Kgs 16:31 identifies Jezebel as the daughter of Ethbaal, king of the Sidonians. He is commonly identified with Ittobaal of [[tyre|Tyre]], known through the historian Menander as quoted by Josephus. “Sidonians” could serve as a broad designation for [[phoenicians|Phoenicians]], so the wording need not place the royal house in Sidon specifically. A marriage of this kind could formalize relations between ruling houses with complementary economic and political interests. [@abd] [@neaehl]

A seal first published by Nahman Avigad in 1964 bears the surviving letters *yzbl*. Marjo Korpel has proposed restoring a missing initial letter to match Jezebel’s name and has read the imagery as appropriate to a Phoenician queen. The seal lacks an excavated context, title and patronymic. Its damaged portion, date and imagery are insufficient to establish an attribution. Other scholars have emphasized that individual motifs circulated widely among elite [[seals-and-bullae|seals]]. [@avigad-sass-wsss] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig]

## Biblical Portrayal

In 1 Kgs 18:19 Elijah refers to prophets who eat at Jezebel’s table. The language depicts royal patronage of religion. The identity of the [[baal|Baal]] presented in the account is unsettled. The Tyrian cult of [[melqart|Melqart]], Baal of Heaven and a less specific use of the title have all been considered. In 1 Kgs 21:8, Jezebel uses Ahab’s name and seal to authorize letters to local officials in the Naboth episode. The narrative assumes familiarity with the authority conveyed by a royal seal. [@ddd] [@abd]

In 2 Kgs 9:30–37, Jezebel paints her eyes, adorns her head and confronts Jehu from a window. The scene has been compared with the woman-at-the-window motif found on Phoenician-style ivories, including examples from [[samaria-ivories|Samaria]]. The motif provides a visual comparison, but the figures on the ivories do not portray the episode. The word translated queen in 2 Kgs 10:13, *gĕbîrâ*, can designate a senior royal woman. Jezebel is often understood to be the figure in view, though she is unnamed there. [@anep] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig] [@abd]

## Historical Assessment

Kings links Jezebel’s foreign origin, religious sponsorship and political force to its critique of Ahab’s rule. Its judgments reflect the literary interests of the histories centered on covenant loyalty and legitimate worship. The account still helps show how a royal wife and mother might exercise power through court resources and dynastic relationships. Rev 2:20 later applies Jezebel’s name to a woman it condemns for claiming prophetic authority, extending the biblical image to a different setting. [@abd]
