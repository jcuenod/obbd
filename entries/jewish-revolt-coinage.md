## Overview

Jewish authorities issued distinctive coins during revolts against Rome in 66–70 CE and 132–135 CE. The first issue included silver shekels and bronze coins dated by years of the revolt. The second, associated with Simon bar Kosiba, used existing coins as the metal for new designs. The issues use old Hebrew letter forms, names for Israel and Jerusalem, and imagery connected with the temple and its festivals. They provide contemporary evidence for the public claims and aspirations of the two revolts.

## Archaeological Evidence

During the [[first-jewish-revolt|First Jewish Revolt]], silver shekels and half-shekels were issued over five years. Full shekels show a cup and bear the legend “Shekel of Israel” with a date. The other face shows a stem with three fruits commonly identified as pomegranates, with a legend naming Jerusalem as holy. Fifth-year coins are particularly scarce. Jerusalem is the probable mint, while the precise role of temple administrators in issuing the coins remains uncertain. Small bronze coins of years two and three carry a jar and vine leaf and proclaim freedom for Zion. Larger fourth-year bronzes use festival imagery and proclaim Zion’s redemption. [@abd, 1:1083–1089] [@neaehl]

The rebels led by Simon bar Kosiba, later known as Bar Kokhba, issued coins in 132–135 CE. They struck new designs on existing coins, including Roman denarii and provincial tetradrachms. Traces of earlier designs sometimes remain visible. Legends name Simon and Eleazar the priest or proclaim Israel’s redemption and Jerusalem’s freedom. The design on some large silver pieces shows a temple front with a central object that has been interpreted as the ark or a table. Other designs use plant bundles, trumpets, lyres or jars. [@abd, 1:1083–1089] [@neaehl]

Both rebellions used the [[paleo-hebrew-script|paleo-Hebrew script]], an older form of Hebrew writing also used on Hasmonean coins. Such lettering recalled earlier Jewish authority in a period when the square script predominated for many purposes. The Roman [[judaea-capta-coins|Judaea Capta series]] answered the first revolt with its own imagery of rule and defeat. Forgeries circulate on the modern market, so excavated finds and firmly documented specimens carry special evidential weight. [@abd, 1:1083–1089] [@neaehl]

## In the Bible

Exod 30:13–16 specifies a half-shekel contribution by weight. Later temple payments help explain why a half-shekel coin would have been useful, and revolt silver may have replaced the [[tyrian-shekel|Tyrian shekel]] for temple dues. The specific purpose of the issues is debated. The description of holy Jerusalem in Isa 52:1 provides a biblical comparison for the silver legends. The plant designs on some bronzes and later silver pieces include a *lulav*, the festive palm bundle, and an *etrog*, a citron. They illustrate the festival custom associated with Lev 23:40. Num 10:2 offers a context for the use of trumpets in Israelite worship and assembly. [@abd, 1:1083–1089]

The star above the temple on some Bar Kokhba issues has been linked with the star from Jacob in Num 24:17 and with the rebel leader’s later name, meaning “son of the star.” The symbol’s precise meaning at the time of issue remains debated. In 1 Macc 15:6, the grant of authority to mint coins gives an earlier example of how issuing money signaled political status. [@abd, 1:1083–1089]
