## Overview

El is both a West Semitic word for “god” and the name of a senior deity. In Late Bronze Age texts from Ugarit, on the Syrian coast, El appears as an aged father and king who presides over other gods and approves their plans. His titles emphasize creation, kinship and authority. Hebrew biblical texts also use El as a name or title for Israel's God as well as a general word for a deity. Scholars debate when and how the characteristics associated with El came to be associated with Yahweh.

## Sources and Iconography

The Ugaritic poems call El “Bull El,” “Kindly El the Compassionate,” “father of humanity,” “creator of creatures” and “king.” These expressions indicate his rank and role among the gods, though they occur in different settings. The texts locate his dwelling at the source of the rivers, near the springs of the two deeps. In the Baal Cycle other deities travel there to consult him, and his approval matters for Baal's plans. [@ktu3, 1.4 IV–V, 1.6 I] [@parker-unp, 83, 91–92, 115–116, 126–129, 153]

One image often associated with El is a small [[statue-of-el|enthroned deity from Ugarit]], which shows a bearded male figure. The association is an interpretation based on the appearance and posture of the representation. The Ugaritic narratives provide firmer evidence for El's seniority and relationship to the [[divine-council|assembly of the gods]]. [@keel-uehlinger-ggig] [@ddd, 274–280]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Hebrew, *ʾēl* can mean “god” without identifying a particular deity. It also occurs in personal names, including Israel. In Gen 14:18–22, Melchizedek blesses Abram in the name of El Elyon, “God Most High,” whom Abram invokes in connection with Yahweh. The narrative connects the divine titles in its present form. The earlier history of [[elyon|Elyon]] remains debated. [@ddd, 274–280]

Exod 6:2–3 distinguishes the name Yahweh from the title El Shaddai by which God appeared to the ancestors. Biblical writers use El language for Yahweh without portraying a rivalry between Yahweh and El like the rivalry with [[baal|Baal]] in some narratives. Num 23:22 compares the strength of God with that of a wild ox. A possible echo of “Bull El” has been proposed, but a shared use of bovine power as an image is also possible.

## Scholarly Issues

Frank Moore Cross argued for an early, close connection between Yahweh and El within the religious world of early Israel. Mark S. Smith and other scholars have described a process in which identities or attributes originally associated with distinct gods came together. Earlier, Albrecht Alt proposed that the ancestral traditions preserve a form of worship organized around the god of a particular family, involving names and titles of El. The contrast in Exod 6:2–3 has figured in such historical reconstructions.

The biblical texts demonstrate the use of El language for Yahweh. Reconstructing the [[yhwh-origins|origins of that association]] requires interpretation of later literary sources and limited evidence for the earlier period. The proposals explain some of the same evidence in different ways, and no single reconstruction is certain. [@ddd, 274–280] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig]
