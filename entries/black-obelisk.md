## Overview

The Black Obelisk is a carved limestone monument erected at Nimrud in northern Iraq near the end of the reign of Shalmaneser III, king of Assyria, in the ninth century BCE. Its text records military campaigns, and its reliefs present tribute from several regions. One caption identifies tribute from a ruler usually understood as Jehu of Israel. The payment dates to 841 BCE, during an Assyrian campaign against Hazael of Damascus. The scene gives a rare Assyrian view of Israel's place in regional politics at the time of Jehu's rise to power.

## Description and Provenance

Austen Henry Layard found the obelisk at [[nimrud|Nimrud]], ancient Kalhu, in 1846. It is now in the British Museum. The four sides carry an account of the campaigns of [[shalmaneser-iii|Shalmaneser III]] and carved scenes arranged in five horizontal bands. Each band shows a delegation or its offerings and has an identifying caption. The inscription records events through the king's thirty-first regnal year, near the end of the 820s BCE. The monument therefore commemorates some events that took place years before it was made. [@rima3, A.0.102.14, A.0.102.88] [@cos2, 2.113F]

## Contents

The second band depicts a figure prostrate before the Assyrian king and others bringing goods. The caption identifies the tribute as coming from a ruler whose name is normally read as [[jehu|Jehu]] and associates him with [[omri|Omri]]. Metals and vessels appear in the tribute list. Another version of the king's annals associates the payment with his eighteenth year, 841 BCE, when he fought [[hazael|Hazael]] of Damascus. The [[kurkh-monoliths|Kurkh Monoliths]] record an earlier campaign in which Israel and Damascus fought in the same coalition against Shalmaneser. [@rima3, A.0.102.14, A.0.102.88] [@cos2, 2.113F]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The biblical account of Jehu's coup and reign in 2 Kgs 9:1–10:36 does not describe this payment. It identifies him as the son of Jehoshaphat, son of Nimshi (2 Kgs 9:2), and depicts his overthrow of Omri's descendants. The Assyrian expression “son of Omri” fits the practice of identifying Israel by the name of its former ruling house. It is generally understood as a political designation. The two accounts therefore illuminate distinct aspects of the same period, including how Assyria continued to classify a kingdom after its rule changed. [@rima3, A.0.102.14, A.0.102.88] [@abd, 3:563–565]

In 2 Kgs 10:32–33, Hazael takes land from Israel east of the Jordan. Shalmaneser's inscriptions show that the ruler of Damascus was also contending with Assyria. Jehu may have seen [[tribute-and-vassalage|tribute]] as a way to manage that balance of power, but the obelisk records payment and leaves his motives unstated. The Assyrian dating establishes a fixed point for the chronology of Jehu's reign.

## Scholarly Issues

The figure bowing before Shalmaneser could represent Jehu himself or someone sent to present his tribute. The caption identifies the source of the goods, and the scene uses conventional signs of [[prostration-and-obeisance|submission]] that do not distinguish the ruler's body from his representative with certainty. P. Kyle McCarter proposed that the name on the obelisk refers to Joram instead. Most scholars retain the identification with Jehu, while the precise identity of the individual in the relief remains open. [@rima3, A.0.102.14, A.0.102.88]
