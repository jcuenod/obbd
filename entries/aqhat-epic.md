## Overview

The *Tale of Aqhat* is a Late Bronze Age poem from Ugarit on the Syrian coast. It tells how Danel, a man remembered for administering justice, receives a son named Aqhat after appealing to the gods. Aqhat refuses to give his bow to the goddess Anat and is killed. The tablets preserve much of the family's grief but leave the ending uncertain. The poem offers important comparisons for biblical descriptions of righteous judgment, family obligations and the Danel whom Ezekiel lists with Noah and Job.

## Manuscripts and Date

The poem survives on three broken clay tablets from Ugarit. They belong to the Late Bronze Age and use [[ugaritic-texts|Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform]], a script that writes letters with wedge-shaped marks. Damage to the tablets leaves gaps within scenes and interrupts the concluding episode. The surviving copies therefore preserve a narrative that can be followed through Aqhat's death but without a secure account of its outcome. [@ktu3, 1.17–1.19] [@parker-unp, 49–80]

## Contents

Danel lacks a son and ministers before the gods for seven days. Baal intercedes with El, who grants the request for a child. In the poem's list of duties, a son looks after his father and performs actions concerned with the family and the dead. One difficult expression, *skn ilib*, is often understood as a standing stone belonging to an ancestral god or divine ancestor. Its precise meaning and the rite involved remain disputed. [@parker-unp, 51–56] [@cos1, 1.103]

A divine craftsman brings a bow that comes into Aqhat's possession. [[anat|Anat]] wants it and offers riches and then life without death. Aqhat replies that mortals die and refuses. Anat arranges an attack in which he is killed, and the poem recounts the grief of his family and the failure of rain. Aqhat's sister Pughat sets out against the person responsible for his death, but her story breaks off before the outcome is clear. [@parker-unp, 58–78]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Danel sits by the gate and hears the cases of widows and orphans. The episode illustrates a broader ideal in which rulers and judges protect people without powerful family advocates. Isa 1:17 similarly links the pursuit of justice with defending the orphan and pleading for the widow. The comparison concerns an ideal of [[widow-orphan-sojourner|protection of vulnerable people]] shared across the region.

Ezek 14:14, 20 names Danel with Noah and Job as figures whose righteousness would save only themselves. Ezek 28:3 uses Danel as a measure of wisdom. Many scholars identify Ezekiel's figure with the traditional wise and just man also known from Ugarit. The unusual spelling of the name in Ezekiel and its grouping with Noah and Job support that identification, although others understand the name to refer to Daniel, the figure associated with the biblical book. Ezekiel could have known the figure through a tradition that circulated independently of the surviving poem. [@abd] [@parker-unp, 50]

## Scholarly Issues

Some scholars interpret Danel's seven-day rite as [[dream-incubation|dream incubation]], the deliberate seeking of a divine message while sleeping in a sacred place. The damaged text leaves the ritual's exact form open. Danel's concern for descendants and Aqhat's refusal of immortality provide two ways the poem explores the limits of human life. The later drought is part of the story, but it gives limited grounds for interpreting the whole work as an allegory about fertility. [@parker-unp, 49–53, 60–63, 68–70]
