## Overview

Qos, also written Qaus, was a principal deity of the Iron Age kingdom of Edom. His name appears in those of Edomite kings recorded by Assyria and in a blessing from a site in the southern Levant. Later evidence shows continued veneration among people identified as Idumeans and in Nabataean settings. The Hebrew Bible gives no explicit account of worship directed to Qos, although a personal name in lists of temple servants probably contains his name. His relation to traditions about the Lord in Seir remains speculative.

## Sources and Iconography

Assyrian inscriptions record the Edomite kings Qaus-malaka under Tiglath-pileser III and Qaus-gabri under Esarhaddon. Their names contain the divine name Qos. Such names are evidence for the deity’s importance to people who used them, including members of the royal house. A potsherd letter from Horvat ʿUza invokes Qos in a greeting or blessing. An inscription from Horvat Qitmit also appears to mention him, though readings of fragmentary material require caution. The finds place the name in use west of Edom’s main territory. [@rinap1, 122] [@rinap4, no. 1] [@ddd, 674–677]

The name continues in later evidence. A Nabataean inscription from Khirbet et-Tannur associates the sanctuary with the deity. Josephus reports that the ancestors of the Herodian-period Idumean Costobarus held a priesthood for a deity he calls Koze, generally identified with Qos. Divine images from these settings rarely have inscriptions identifying them directly. Discussions of Qos’s visual appearance therefore depend on interpretation of context and symbols. [@kai] [@ddd, 674–677] [@abd]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ezra 2:53 and Neh 7:55 name Barkos in parallel lists of temple servants. The name is commonly interpreted as “son of Qos,” though the lists supply no information about the bearer’s worship. 2 Chr 25:14–15 accuses Amaziah of worshiping the gods of the people of Seir after a victory. The passage names no deity, so a connection with Qos remains an inference. [@abd] [@ddd, 674–677]

Deut 33:2 and Judg 5:4 portray the Lord appearing from locations in the south, including Seir, which is associated with Edom. These texts are relevant to proposals about the [[yhwh-origins|southern origins of worship of the Lord]]. They provide no direct account of a relationship with Qos. The explicit evidence for Qos comes chiefly from inscriptions and names. [@ddd, 674–677] [@abd, 4:19–21, 6:1011–1013]

## Scholarly Issues

The relationship of Qos to neighboring deities is difficult to reconstruct. Scholars have connected the name with a word for a bow and have proposed a role involving storms or warfare, but the surviving evidence gives limited access to his myths or attributes. The use of the name in personal names and short blessings is more securely attested. Several religious traditions coexisted in the southern Levant, so the use of a divine name in a letter need not establish an entire community’s origin, language or political allegiance. [@ddd, 674–677] [@neaehl]
