## Overview

Ai is the town east of Bethel that Israel attacks in the book of Joshua. Most archaeologists identify it with et-Tell, a mound in the central highlands north of Jerusalem. Excavations found the remains of a large Early Bronze Age city and a much later village from the beginning of the Iron Age. The mound has yielded no occupied Late Bronze Age city corresponding to the usual historical settings proposed for Joshua. This gap has shaped both the interpretation of the narrative and efforts to identify Ai elsewhere.

## Location and Identification

Josh 7:2 places Ai beside Beth-aven, east of [[bethel|Bethel]]. Gen 12:8 likewise sets Abram's campsite between Bethel to the west and Ai to the east. The identification of Bethel with modern Beitin makes et-Tell an attractive candidate. The modern Arabic name denotes a mound, and the Hebrew name *hāʿay* means “the ruin.” These names fit the conspicuous remains at et-Tell. The biblical directions and the visibility of the ruins explain the prevailing identification, whose geographical argument depends in part on the location of Bethel. [@neaehl] [@abd]

## Archaeological History

Judith Marquet-Krause excavated at et-Tell in the 1930s, and Joseph Callaway directed renewed work in the 1960s and early 1970s. They documented a fortified Early Bronze Age city with substantial public and defensive architecture. Its final destruction is often dated to c. 2400 BCE, though dates within the Early Bronze Age vary with the chronology used. The city was followed by a long gap in settlement. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

A small, unfortified village then occupied part of the mound in the early Iron Age. The village is important in its own right as evidence for rural life in the highlands during the period discussed under the [[emergence-of-israel|emergence of Israel]]. Excavations did not identify a city at the mound between the Early Bronze ruins and the later village. In the usual chronologies of the conquest, the Late Bronze Age falls within that gap. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Josh 7:2–5, a small Israelite force is driven away from Ai. The next attack involves an ambush, the capture and burning of the city, and the death of its king (Josh 8:1–29). The account says the town became a heap that endured to the narrator's day (Josh 8:28). Many scholars read it as an origin story for the conspicuous ruins, a reading often associated with Albrecht Alt and Martin Noth. The story also belongs to a wider body of [[conquest-accounts-and-war-rhetoric|conquest accounts]] concerned with defeat, obedience, and divine aid. [@abd]

The identification and the historical interpretation are separate questions. If the site is et-Tell, its known occupation history provides no Late Bronze city to match the account. Bryant Wood and other excavators proposed Khirbet el-Maqatir on the basis of a fortified site they date to the Late Bronze period and a conquest chronology around 1400 BCE. This remains a minority identification. Other proposals, such as Khirbet Nisya or nearby Khirbet Khaiyan, have gained limited acceptance, and an identification with either would need stronger evidence from the relevant period. [@neaehl]
