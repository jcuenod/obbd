## Overview

Petra in southern Jordan was a major Nabataean city whose famous rock-cut monuments date mainly to the late Hellenistic and Roman periods. The biblical stronghold Sela, whose name means “rock,” belongs to the earlier setting of Judah and Edom. More than one site has been proposed for it. Excavations document an Edomite settlement on a summit at Petra, and another stronghold near Tafila preserves a relief associated with Nabonidus. The evidence confirms Edomite activity in the region but leaves the biblical site uncertain.

## Location and Identification

Petra lies in the mountains east of Wadi Arabah, near modern Wadi Musa. Its basin is reached through narrow routes in sandstone and is surrounded by steep heights. Umm el-Biyara, a plateau above Petra, is one candidate for Sela. Another is as-Silaʿ near Tafila farther north, a prominent fortified height whose modern name is close to the Hebrew one. Both suit a description involving a rocky stronghold. No surviving inscription securely identifies either with the site in Kings.

## Archaeological History

Crystal Bennett’s excavations at Umm el-Biyara uncovered an Edomite settlement from the Iron Age, including a seal impression naming Qaus-gabri, king of Edom. This attests the presence of a royal administrative connection or object at the site. It cannot identify it as the capital. [[bozrah|Bozrah]], usually identified with Busayra, is the principal known administrative center of Iron Age Edom. The rock relief at as-Silaʿ depicts a king and is associated with [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]], whose activities took him into the region in the sixth century BCE. It establishes the location’s importance at that time without recording the earlier biblical encounter.

The best-known monuments within Petra are [[nabataeans|Nabataean]] or later. Rock-cut tombs, religious sites, channels, and cisterns document a city able to capture and store water in a dry setting. The elaborate architecture of the late first century BCE and first century CE belongs to a political and commercial center quite different from the Edomite settlement at Umm el-Biyara. Petra subsequently came under Roman rule.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Second Kings 14:7 reports that Amaziah defeated Edom and captured Sela, giving it another name. Second Chronicles 25:11–12 describes the killing of captives at a rocky height. The two accounts explain why a steep stronghold is sought, without giving directions sufficient to identify one. The Hebrew *selaʿ* means “rock” and can function as a place name or a common noun. Isa 16:1 and Isa 42:11 have geographical uses that require separate assessment. Jer 49:16 and Obad 3 use the image of secure dwellings in rock clefts for Edom. Such descriptions suit several locations in the region.

Second Corinthians 11:32 mentions the official of King Aretas in connection with Paul’s escape from Damascus. Aretas IV was a Nabataean king, and Petra helps explain the kingdom behind that reference. The passage concerns Damascus. It gives no account of Paul visiting Petra or of the precise arrangement through which the official exercised authority.
