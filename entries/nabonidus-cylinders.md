## Overview

The Nabonidus cylinders are inscribed clay objects from sixth-century BCE Babylonia that record temple restorations by [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]], the last king of Babylon before the Persian conquest. Copies found at [[ur|Ur]] describe the repair of a stepped temple tower and end with a prayer naming [[belshazzar|Belshazzar]] as the king's eldest son. The inscription known as the Sippar Cylinder recounts the rebuilding of the temple of the moon god at [[harran|Harran]]. The texts provide independent evidence for Belshazzar, who appears as king in Daniel, and later historical background for two cities on Abram's route in Genesis.

## Description and Provenance

The cylinders are foundation deposits, objects placed within a building during construction or repair to commemorate the work. Their texts are in Akkadian, the language of Babylonian royal inscriptions, written in cuneiform, a script made with wedge-shaped marks in clay. The Ur inscription is arranged in two columns; the Sippar inscription is longer and occupies three. Some copies of the Ur text are only about 9.5 cm long [@cos2].

J. E. Taylor recovered copies of the Ur text while excavating the [[ziggurat|ziggurat]], or stepped temple tower, in the 1850s. Hormuzd Rassam's excavations at Sippar yielded copies and fragments of the longer inscription, which is also known from finds at Babylon. The British Museum in London holds examples of both texts. The Sippar Cylinder should not be confused with the [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]], a separate inscription issued in Cyrus's name after his conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE.

## Contents

At Ur, Nabonidus says he consulted inscriptions of the earlier kings Ur-Nammu and Shulgi and repaired the tower on their foundations. He asks [[sin-moon-god|Sin]], the moon god, to grant him long life and asks that Belshazzar, “my firstborn son,” revere the god and enjoy a full life (col. II, lines 23–26). The prayer identifies Belshazzar by name and parentage, but assigns him no royal title [@cos2, 313–314].

In the Sippar text, Nabonidus reports that Marduk, Babylon's patron god, appeared alongside Sin in a dream and told him, “Build Ehulhul” (col. I, lines 15–20). Ehulhul was Sin's temple at Harran. The text then presents Cyrus's defeat and capture of Astyages, king of the Medes, as removing the obstacle to the rebuilding. In a prayer for the restored temple, Nabonidus calls Sin king of the gods, while the dream credits Marduk with commissioning the work [@cos2, 310–313].

The same inscription reports that Nabonidus excavated the foundations of Ebabbar, the temple of the sun god Shamash at Sippar. He claims to have found a deposit of the much earlier Akkadian king Naram-Sin where Nebuchadnezzar II had looked without success. Nabonidus says he returned the older inscription to its place alongside his own and asks a future restorer to treat his inscription in the same way [@cos2, 310–313].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The Ur find gave nineteenth-century scholars evidence that Belshazzar was a historical figure. Dan 5:1–2 depicts him holding a banquet as king, while Dan 7:1 and 8:1 date visions to his first and third years. The cylinder confirms his identity but records neither the banquet nor those years of rule. A different source, the [[verse-account-of-nabonidus|Verse Account of Nabonidus]], says Nabonidus entrusted royal authority to his eldest son before leaving for Arabia [@anet].

Gen 11:31–32 describes Terah's household leaving Ur of the Chaldeans and settling in Harran on the way to Canaan. The inscriptions show that the southern Mesopotamian city of Ur and Harran were both centers of Sin's worship in Nabonidus's time. That is background for the places named in Genesis, not evidence for the journey or for the family's religious practices.

## Scholarly Issues

Dan 5:2, 11, 18 and 22 uses father–son language for Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar, whereas the Ur prayer explicitly identifies Nabonidus as Belshazzar's father. One interpretation takes Daniel's language as a way of marking royal succession rather than literal descent; another takes it as a later narrative tradition linking Belshazzar to the better-known Nebuchadnezzar. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the relative standing of the dynastic-language and later-tradition readings in current Daniel scholarship. --> The cylinder establishes the historical parentage but cannot determine why Daniel frames the relationship as it does.
