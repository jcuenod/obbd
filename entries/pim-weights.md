## Overview

Pim weights are small stone measures marked with the Hebrew letters for *pim*, a unit used in the late Judahite monarchy. Surviving examples weigh about 7.6 grams, or roughly two-thirds of a Judahite shekel, with variation among specimens. They belong to a larger system of labeled stone weights used to measure goods and silver. Their identification helped explain a difficult term in 1 Samuel, where a pim is charged for work on tools. The objects also provide context for biblical laws about honest weighing.

## Archaeological Evidence

Judahite weights are often rounded stones with a relatively flat base. Markings can identify units or numbers. Some use signs derived from Egyptian hieratic, a cursive form of Egyptian writing. Others give alphabetic names, including *pim*, *beqaʿ*, and *nesef*. These labeled stones show that customary measures could be recorded consistently enough to circulate across sites. They also show that a local system could adopt signs from a neighboring scribal tradition.

A Judahite shekel is commonly reckoned at about 11.3 grams from groups of weights. A pim averages roughly 7.6 grams in the usual reconstruction, while the beqa was a half shekel. Individual pieces vary in weight. Wear, manufacture, and different standards can contribute to the variation, and discrepancies alone do not establish fraudulent intent. Market objects with no secure archaeological context require additional scrutiny. Excavated objects provide a firmer basis for identifying the forms and inscriptions of the system.

The shekel in this setting is a unit of mass. As a measure for silver it could establish an amount to transfer by weighing pieces rather than by counting coins. A balance and a recognizable set of stones made that calculation possible. The physical objects clarify these practices independently of the way a narrative or legal text describes them.

## In the Bible

First Samuel 13:21 gives a *pim* as the charge in a passage about obtaining work on agricultural implements. The word was difficult to explain before inscribed weights were recognized in the early twentieth century. The weights made it possible to read the word as a unit of payment. They do not resolve every detail of the verse, including the underlying ordering and description of all the implements. Nor do late Judahite stones independently date the events narrated in Samuel.

Exod 38:26 identifies the *beqaʿ* as half a shekel in the sanctuary account, and Gen 24:22 uses the same measure for a gold ornament. In Gen 23:16 Abraham weighs out silver in a land transaction. Ezek 45:12 describes proportional units for a regulated system. These passages show biblical writers accounting for values by weight, in settings that need not follow one identical local standard.

Lev 19:35–36 and Deut 25:13–16 call for just balances and weights. Prov 11:1 condemns a false balance, and Amos 8:5 and Mic 6:11 portray manipulation of measures as part of economic wrongdoing. The surviving objects make these concerns concrete. A unit’s stated name could be checked against its actual mass, especially if buyers and sellers had access to common standards.
