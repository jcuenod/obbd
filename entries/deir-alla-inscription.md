## Overview

The Deir ʿAlla Inscription consists of fragments of an Iron Age text written in ink on wall plaster in the eastern Jordan Valley. The best-preserved part names Balaam son of Beor and portrays him as a seer who receives a disturbing message from the gods. The inscription is often dated around 800 BCE, although its precise date and the classification of its language remain debated. It shows that a tradition about a seer of this name circulated outside the biblical account in Numbers and gives a rare regional example of written visions and divine judgment.

## Description and Provenance

Excavators at Tell Deir ʿAlla found the plaster fragments in 1967 among debris from a collapsed wall. The text had been written in black and red ink, and its words survive in uneven groups of fragments. Scholars have proposed several identifications for the ancient town, including Succoth and Penuel, but none is secure. The archaeological context and the shapes of the letters place the text broadly around the transition from the ninth to the eighth century BCE in a common dating. Some scholars have argued for a later date. [@cos2] [@nissinen-prophets]

## Contents

The better-preserved section, often called Combination I, introduces Balaam son of Beor as a seer of the gods. The gods come to him at night. He rises in distress and reports an impending change involving darkness, decreed in a [[divine-council|divine assembly]]. Images of darkness and disruption present the effects of the decree. Precise readings of some words and the sequence of certain lines differ with the reconstruction. [@cos2] [@nissinen-prophets]

The group called Combination II is harder to read. Damage and uncertainty about how the pieces fit affect the meaning of even fairly short passages. Its relation to the first group remains open. Proposals connecting it with consulting the dead or sacrificing children depend on disputed readings and should be treated as possibilities.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Num 22:5 names Balaam son of Beor, the same personal name and father's name found on the plaster. In Num 22:8–13 and 19–20 the deity communicates with Balaam during the night. The inscription gives independent evidence for a regional tradition about such a figure. It cannot determine how the surviving narratives developed or establish that the events of either account took place. The different portrayals are evidence for the uses to which the figure was put. [@nissinen-prophets]

Balaam speaks of seeing a vision of Shaddai in Num 24:4, 16. The plaster text uses a similar-looking expression for a group of gods, and scholars have discussed a linguistic relationship. The resemblance leaves the exact relationship and original meaning open. The darkness announced in the plaster text can also be compared with Amos 5:18–20, though a specific literary connection is debated. Both use darkness to make divine judgment vivid. [@ddd]

## Scholarly Issues

The text has features shared with [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]] and features closer to Canaanite languages such as Hebrew. Scholars have classified it as a local form of Aramaic, as a Canaanite dialect, or as a local language that does not fit either category neatly. The classification matters for mapping the languages spoken east of the Jordan in the Iron Age. The script and vocabulary can supply evidence for language contact without identifying a particular ethnic group as the authors. [@cos2]
