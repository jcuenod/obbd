## Overview

The Laws of Hammurabi are a Babylonian collection of legal provisions inscribed on a stone monument in the eighteenth century BCE. The text covers matters such as theft, injury, property and family relationships. Its opening and closing present Hammurabi as a king charged by the gods with establishing justice. The monument was discovered at Susa in what is now Iran. Its provisions offer some of the closest comparisons for laws in Exodus, and scholars debate whether biblical scribes used this particular collection directly.

## Description and Provenance

A French expedition found the monument in pieces at [[susa|Susa]] in 1901–1902. The Louvre catalogue describes the stone as basalt. The relief above the text shows [[hammurabi|Hammurabi]] standing before a seated deity generally identified as [[shamash|Shamash]], the sun god associated with justice. The monument was taken from Babylonia to Elam, probably as war booty in the twelfth century BCE. The attribution of its removal to Shutruk-Nahhunte or his successor remains uncertain. [@roth-law] [@anep]

The laws also survive in fragments of other monuments and on clay tablets. Scribes copied the text long after Hammurabi's lifetime. Those copies sometimes preserve portions missing from the most complete monument. Their distribution shows that the work could circulate independently of the original display and remain part of scribal study. [@roth-law] [@cos2]

## Contents

The prologue introduces Hammurabi's commission, and the epilogue celebrates his justice and warns future rulers against altering the monument. The provisions between them usually use a case and consequence, a form known as casuistic law. The numbering by which roughly 282 provisions are distinguished is modern. Consequences for injuries can depend on the legal status of the parties, expressed through Akkadian terms including *awīlum*, a person of full standing, *muškēnum*, a person of a different free status whose exact standing is debated, and *wardum*, an enslaved person. [@roth-law]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The rules concerning a dangerous animal whose owner had warning can be compared with the [[goring-ox|goring ox]] in Exod 21:28–32. The biblical and Babylonian cases distinguish prior notice from an unforeseen attack, and the consequences differ. Proportional bodily penalties also offer comparisons with [[lex-talionis|the rule of equivalent retaliation]] in Exod 21:23–25. Some Babylonian provisions impose punishment on a child for harm caused by a parent, whereas Deut 24:16 expressly prohibits executing children for their parents' acts. [@roth-law]

The collection gives the king a prominent part in proclaiming justice, under a commission from the gods. Exodus presents its laws as Yahweh's speech to Israel. These frames help explain why similar forms and legal problems can appear in writings that depict lawgiving in different ways.

## Scholarly Issues

Court documents rarely cite the collection. Some interpreters emphasize scribal learning or the royal presentation of justice, while others allow that the legal reasoning in the text could help guide decisions. The wider questions about practice belong to [[ane-law-collections|the ancient law collections]]. [@roth-law]

David P. Wright argues that the Covenant Code of Exodus reworked the Laws of Hammurabi in the first millennium BCE. Other scholars explain parallels through a shared tradition of legal cases and their transmission among scribes. The differences between the two works are part of the debate, as are the sequences in which related cases appear. The manuscript history shows why direct use was possible, while it cannot establish that such use took place.
