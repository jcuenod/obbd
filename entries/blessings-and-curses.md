## Overview

Blessings and curses announce benefits or harms tied to people's conduct. Ancient Near Eastern rulers, scribes, and priests used them in treaties, royal inscriptions, and other formal texts. A curse could call on gods to enforce an oath or protect a monument, often with a vivid picture of the consequences of breaking its terms. Biblical covenant texts and prophetic speeches use related forms. Their similarities help explain how images of failed harvests, barren fields, and houses their builders never occupy convey the consequences of disobedience and injustice.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Treaties could name divine witnesses and call on them to bring disaster if an oath was broken. The [[sefire-treaties|Sefire treaties]] from Syria include curses enacted by handling objects. An act performed before witnesses gave concrete form to what was invoked against a treaty breaker. The words and actions were part of establishing the obligation, as well as statements about a feared future [@kai, no. 222 A] [@cos2, 2.82].

A futility curse describes an activity that fails to achieve its expected result. The [[tell-fekheriye-statue|Tell Fekheriye inscription]] wishes that someone may sow without reaping. It and a Sefire text also envisage a lamb remaining hungry despite being suckled by many ewes. Their different uses of similar formulas show that a curse could circulate beyond any single treaty or monument [@kai, no. 222 A] [@cos2, 2.82].

Other inscriptions use curses to protect legal and property claims. The ending of the [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] calls for punishment of a future ruler who disregards or alters the monument's provisions. Babylonian land-grant monuments invoke divine punishment against people who disturb property arrangements. Levantine tomb inscriptions, including those of Ahiram and Eshmunazar, threaten people who intrude. The common form makes divine protection of the claim explicit, whether the subject is land, burial, or a royal record [@roth-law, 133–140] [@kai, nos. 1, 14].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Lev 26:3–45 and Deut 28:1–68 set blessings and punishments alongside obligations. The blessings include agricultural abundance and security. The curses imagine scarcity, defeat, and exile. In Deut 27:11–26, the people are told to give spoken assent after each curse. Josh 8:30–35 places a public reading of blessings and curses at Mount Ebal.

Deut 28:23 compares the sky to bronze and the earth to iron. Lev 26:19 uses the same metals in reverse order. The [[esarhaddon-succession-treaty|succession treaty of Esarhaddon]] contains a close comparison that combines metals and the withholding of rain. The two biblical passages use the image within their own depictions of failed harvests and scarcity [@saa2, 51].

Prophets adapt the form to particular charges. Hos 4:10 warns of eating without being satisfied. Amos 5:11 tells people who exploit the poor that they will build houses without living in them and plant vineyards without drinking the wine. These sayings use familiar activities to show how judgment takes away the expected benefits of wealth and labor.

## Interpretive Significance

Some passages in Deuteronomy have close correspondences in image and sequence with Assyrian curses. Many scholars understand these correspondences as evidence for an Assyrian influence on the writers. They differ on whether a particular tablet was used directly or whether the writers knew the forms from a broader practice of making oaths. In prophetic oracles, a shared formula can also be explained by the circulation of sayings and images across different kinds of speech.
