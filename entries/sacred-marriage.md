## Overview

Sacred marriage is a modern term for ancient accounts and rites that portray a union involving a deity. Sumerian royal songs describe a king joining the goddess Inanna, while first-millennium Mesopotamian records document ceremonies involving divine images. The evidence spans different periods and kinds of text. Biblical prophets also use marriage to portray the relationship between YHWH and his people. These images invite comparison with ancient representations of divine marriage, while their emphasis on loyalty and restoration belongs to a distinct covenantal setting.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Sumerian hymn commonly called *Iddin-Dagan A* celebrates Inanna and portrays the king sharing her bed during a festival. The surrounding verses describe music, offerings, a procession and the goddess's favor. Related [[sumerian-love-songs|Sumerian poems]] use the voices or imagery of Inanna and Dumuzi. Their royal and erotic language links intimacy, legitimacy and the flourishing of the country. Scholars disagree on whether performers enacted sexual union, whether statues or other ceremonial objects embodied it, or whether the language belongs primarily to poetry. [@cos1, 1.169, 1.173] [@ddd]

Seventh-century BCE Assyrian correspondence gives firmer evidence for a rite in which Nabû and Tašmetu enter a bedchamber. In this setting the gods were represented by their cult images. Processions and offerings formed part of the occasion. Love lyrics associated with the pair give a literary comparison to the ritual correspondence. The first-millennium evidence establishes one form of divine wedding and leaves the exact performance behind earlier royal hymns open. [@cos1, 1.128] [@abd]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Hos 2:14–23 (MT 2:16–25) portrays YHWH wooing and betrothing Israel anew. The change from the designation based on *baʿal*, which can mean husband or master, to another word for husband accompanies the removal of the names of the Baals. Jer 2:2 recalls Israel's early devotion as a bride in the wilderness. Ezek 16:8 portrays a covenant made with Jerusalem through the gestures and obligations of marriage. Isa 62:4–5 gives restored Zion and its land names of delight and marriage. Rev 21:2, 9 depicts the New Jerusalem as the Lamb's bride, continuing the use of bridal language for a city and its people. [@abd] [@ddd]

Ezek 8:14 reports women mourning [[tammuz|Tammuz]], the Mesopotamian Dumuzi. It is evidence for a biblical tradition linking Jerusalem with a lament for that god. The scene concerns his mourning and gives no account of a wedding celebration. [@ddd] [@cos1, 1.118]

## Interpretive Significance

Some interpreters have read Song 1–8 as preserving lyrics for a cultic union. This remains a minority view. The poetic exchanges can be read alongside Mesopotamian and [[egyptian-love-poetry|Egyptian love poetry]] as works about desire and intimacy. Shared diction and images leave the occasion of performance open. Prophetic depictions of a divine marriage draw readily on family relations, marriage obligations and covenant in their immediate contexts. Older reconstructions that tied these texts to a widespread fertility cult and institutional [[cultic-prostitution|sacred prostitution]] lack secure evidence. [@abd] [@cos1, 1.169]
