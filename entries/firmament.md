## Overview

The Hebrew word often translated “firmament” or “expanse” names the sky made by God in the first creation account of Genesis. It separates waters above from waters below and is associated with the places of the heavenly lights. Other biblical writers use the word for the visible sky or for a shining surface beneath God's throne. Ancient Mesopotamian descriptions of heavens made of stone and a sky that restrains waters offer comparisons. The chief interpretive question is how physically the biblical text pictures the sky and what its language can establish about its form.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the Babylonian creation poem [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]], Marduk makes part of the body of the defeated sea deity Tiamat into a covering. He sets guards and establishes a restraint so that her waters do not escape. The scene provides an ancient example of a sky that holds back water. Its narrative setting is divine combat and victory [@lambert-bcm].

Some first-millennium BCE Mesopotamian texts describe several heavens made from different kinds of stone. These texts also assign the visible stars and the dwelling places of gods to particular levels. They provide comparisons for ancient descriptions in which heaven has material properties. Individual texts give different accounts of the number and arrangement of cosmic regions [@horowitz-cosmic-geography].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 1:6–8, God makes a *rāqîaʿ* to separate the waters, and calls it sky. Gen 1:14–17 places the lights in it, and Gen 1:20 describes birds flying in relation to its face. Gen 7:11 describes openings in the heavens releasing water during the flood. These images belong to the wider biblical language of [[waters-above-and-below|waters above and below]].

The root of the word also occurs in a verb for beating or spreading something out. In Exod 39:3 the verb describes hammering sheets of gold. Job 37:18 uses the image of a hard cast mirror for the spread of the skies. In Ezek 1:22–26, a gleaming surface is stretched above the living beings, with a throne above it. The pavement beneath the God of Israel in Exod 24:10 and the firmament in [[ezekiel-throne-chariot|Ezekiel's vision]] both add material imagery to a description of the divine realm.

## Interpretive Significance

The ancient Greek and Latin translations use words that suggest something firm or fixed. Many scholars understand Genesis as picturing a solid barrier holding back upper waters, particularly because of its role in separating the waters and the surrounding ancient imagery. Some interpreters favor the term “expanse” and argue that the Hebrew word does not define the material of the sky. The text gives the expanse a clear role in the creation account while describing few physical particulars.

The term is one part of a varied biblical [[cosmology|picture of the world]]. The popular Flammarion engraving, which appeared in a French work on the atmosphere published in 1888, belongs to modern reception. Ancient accounts and images give the appropriate basis for comparing the ways ancient people imagined heaven.
