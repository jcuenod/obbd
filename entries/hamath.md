## Overview

Hamath was an ancient city on the Orontes River at modern Hama in central Syria. In the Iron Age it was the center of a kingdom whose rulers built monuments and joined other Syrian and Levantine powers against Assyria. Luwian and Aramaic inscriptions document different phases of its royal history, and Assyrian records describe the defeat of its revolt in 720 BCE. The Bible names Hamath as a foreign kingdom and refers to Lebo-hamath in descriptions of Israel's northern extent. That expression may refer to a different place on the approach to the kingdom.

## Location and Identification

Modern Hama occupies a site on the Orontes with an ancient citadel mound. The river valley offered routes between southern and northern Syria. Both the city and its territory could be called Hamath in ancient sources. Lebo-hamath, a name or expression used in biblical boundary descriptions, has often been understood as an entrance to the territory of Hamath. It is also commonly associated with Lebweh in the northern Beqaa Valley. The phrase need not identify the capital itself. [@abd, 3:33–37]

## Archaeological History

The Danish expedition led by Harald Ingholt excavated the citadel from 1931 to 1938. It uncovered remains of large Iron Age buildings and monumental sculpture. Inscriptions from Hama name the rulers Urhilina and Uratarhunda, often known also by other renderings of their names. They are written in Luwian using a script of pictorial signs and document royal building projects. They belong to a period when traditions derived from the Hittite states of Anatolia continued in parts of Syria after the fall of the empire. [@cos2, 2.35] [@anep]

Shalmaneser III identifies the Hamathite ruler Irhuleni as a leading member of the coalition that opposed Assyria at the [[battle-of-qarqar|battle of Qarqar]] in 853 BCE. A later [[zakkur-stele|Aramaic inscription of Zakkur]] names him as king of Hamath and Luʿash and describes a siege at Hazrach. In 720 BCE, [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] suppressed a revolt led by a ruler of Hamath whom his records call Iaubiʾdi or Ilubiʾdi. Whether the first form contains a name connected with YHWH is debated. The variant spellings by themselves cannot resolve the question. [@rima3, A.0.102.2 ii 78–102] [@kai, no. 202] [@cos2, 2.113A, 2.35]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 2 Sam 8:9–10, the king of Hamath sends his son to congratulate David after David defeats a rival. The scene portrays Hamath as an independent power with interests in conflicts to its south. Hamath is also among the places named in accounts or prophetic reflections on Assyrian expansion, including 2 Kgs 18:34 and Isa 10:9. In 2 Kgs 17:24, 30 the narrative names people from Hamath among those settled in Samaria, where it associates them with the god Ashima. The Assyrian sources establish widespread resettlement without documenting that community's entire experience. [@cos2, 2.113A, 2.35] [@abd, 3:33–37]

Num 34:8 uses Lebo-hamath in describing a northern boundary, and 1 Kgs 8:65 uses it to define the range of people gathered at Solomon's dedication. The expression can function as a familiar geographical limit in texts of different genres. Its use does not show that Israel controlled the city of Hamath itself in every period represented. [@abd, 3:33–37]
