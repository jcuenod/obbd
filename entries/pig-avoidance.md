## Overview

Leviticus and Deuteronomy prohibit eating pigs because they have divided hooves but do not chew the cud. Excavated animal bones show that pork consumption varied across the southern Levant long before and during the periods in which these laws took shape. Pig remains are scarce at many early highland settlements and common at some coastal cities, with important exceptions on both sides. The archaeological pattern helps frame questions about diet and identity, while the biblical laws and later narratives give more direct evidence for meanings attached to avoiding pork.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Zooarchaeology studies animal remains from archaeological sites. Analysts identify bones, estimate how many specimens came from each kind of animal, and compare samples from particular deposits. Pig bones are rare at many Iron Age I highland sites. Some early [[philistines|Philistine]] cities have higher proportions, but some rural settlements within Philistia have few. Pig use increased in parts of the northern kingdom in the Iron Age II. The pattern depends on the location and period sampled and the nature of the deposit.

A common interpretation treats avoidance as one possible sign of a social boundary. Regional comparisons also point to limits on pig rearing set by access to water and fodder, mobility, urban or rural markets, and customary preferences. Pig consumption and ritual treatment varied in other parts of the ancient Near East. In Mesopotamia and Egypt, use of pigs could coexist with restrictions or unfavorable associations in particular settings. The Iron Age distributions alone cannot establish the reason an individual community ate or avoided them.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Lev 11 and Deut 14:3–21 place the pig within wider dietary classifications. Lev 11:7 and Deut 14:8 give the relevant features and regulate eating or touching the carcass. They do not identify a disease or offer a theory of health. Isa 65:4 and 66:3, 17 associate pig flesh with practices the authors reject. The prophetic settings give the act a broader ritual significance than a list of ingredients might suggest.

In 1 Macc 1:47 the king’s measures include sacrifice of swine and other prohibited animals. Second Maccabees 6:18–31 depicts Eleazar choosing death over eating pork or pretending to do so. The episode makes public obedience central. Mark 5:11–13 includes a herd of pigs in the setting of an exorcism, showing animals whose presence is intelligible in a region of mixed practices. Acts 10:9–16 uses a vision of animals and language of purity whose implications develop through the account of Peter meeting Cornelius.

## Interpretive Significance

Mary Douglas drew attention to how the laws classify animals. Marvin Harris emphasized the ecological and economic conditions in which raising pigs could be advantageous or costly. Jacob Milgrom drew out ethical concerns within the larger system of permitted and prohibited foods. These approaches address different questions. Neither the distribution of bones nor a single rationale explains the meaning of every biblical text. The often-repeated claim that the rule was instituted to prevent trichinosis lacks ancient textual support and does not account for the wider scheme of classification.
