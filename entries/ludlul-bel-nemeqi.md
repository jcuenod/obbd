## Overview

*Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*, “I will praise the lord of wisdom,” is an Akkadian poem about a man who falls from a position of standing into sickness and isolation. It probably took shape in Babylonia during the later second millennium BCE and survives in later copies. The speaker describes divine anger that he cannot understand and praises Marduk for restoring him. The poem is a major comparison for Job's experience of suffering and for the movement from distress to thanksgiving in biblical psalms.

## Manuscripts and Date

The poem is often associated with the Kassite period, in part because its protagonist Šubši-mešrê-Šakkan has a name attested in that setting. Its absolute date is uncertain. First-millennium copies and ancient commentary show that it became part of learned scribal culture. Traditional editions arranged it in four tablets, and later study has proposed a fifth. The differences concern how the surviving text, fragments, and commentary are assigned to the original series [@lambert-bwl].

## Contents

The speaker begins by praising [[marduk|Marduk]], whose anger and favor are beyond human calculation. He then recalls the desertion of divine and human supporters, his loss of standing, and his physical suffering. He has taken part in religious observance and cannot determine what offense caused his situation. The poem asks how a human can know what is acceptable to a god. Dreams accompany the change of his fortunes, and restoration gives rise to public praise [@lambert-bwl] [@cos1].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Job 19:13–22 describes abandonment by relatives, friends, and servants. Job 7:3–5, 13–14 pairs nights of distress with physical affliction and frightening dreams. Job 42:10–17 concludes with restoration after suffering. These motifs make comparison with the Babylonian speaker appropriate. The poem also gives a setting for Zophar's question about divine knowledge in Job 11:7–8 and for Eliphaz's thought that a god can wound and heal in Job 5:17–18. In Job those sayings belong to voices within a debate.

Other biblical parallels draw attention to the poem's form. Ps 38:2–11 (MT 38:3–12) presents illness and isolation in relation to divine anger. Ps 22:6–8, 14–17 (MT 22:7–9, 15–18) gives poetic form to mockery and bodily distress. Ps 30 gives thanks for rescue from near death. The movement from crisis to public praise makes hymns of thanksgiving a comparison in addition to the narrative of Job.

## Scholarly Issues

The label “Babylonian Job” points to a similarity in subject. The works develop it in different ways. Job stages dialogues about justice and includes direct challenges to God. *Ludlul* places its sufferer's uncertainty inside a sustained first-person account that praises Marduk as rescuer. Other works, including the [[babylonian-theodicy|Babylonian Theodicy]], develop the issue through dialogue. The evidence shows a wider ancient discussion of suffering and divine justice without establishing that Job used this specific poem.
