## Overview

The Nile sustained farming, settlement, and transport in ancient Egypt. Before modern dams regulated its flow, the annual flood brought water and fertile sediment to fields along the valley and in the Delta. Egyptian literature celebrates the inundation as a force that feeds the country. The Bible makes the Nile a setting for events in the Joseph and Exodus narratives and uses its flood and failure as images of judgment. Its waters also help explain the contrast Deuteronomy draws between farming in Egypt and Canaan.

## Location and Identification

The Nile runs north through Egypt and divides into branches in the Delta before reaching the Mediterranean. The Hebrew name *yĕʾōr* is generally derived from Egyptian *itrw*, “river.” It can refer to the Nile or to a channel of its system. The name Shihor, used in Isa 23:3 and Jer 2:18, is also associated with Egyptian waters and may designate a branch or channel near the eastern Delta. Num 34:5 uses a different expression, the Brook of Egypt, most often identified with Wadi el-ʿArish.

## Archaeological History

Settlements and cultivated fields followed the narrow stretch of land made productive by the river and broadened in the Delta. Communities could guide or retain floodwater through earthworks and channels. A rising flood could supply the water needed for cultivation, while unusually low waters could leave large areas unproductive. The river also carried people and goods between places that would otherwise have been separated by difficult overland journeys.

Egyptian hymns personify the inundation as Hapi and credit it with sustaining crops, animals, and people. The [[famine-stele|Famine Stele]] tells of a succession of low floods and the scarcity that resulted. Although the inscription is much later than the age in which it sets its story, it illustrates the cultural association between insufficient flooding and famine. Herodotus's questions about the cause of the flood also show that it attracted attention outside Egypt (Histories 2.19–34).

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The cows coming out of the Nile in Pharaoh's dream evoke a landscape of water, pasture, and food production (Gen 41:1–4). The same river becomes a place of death when Pharaoh orders Hebrew boys thrown into it (Exod 1:22). In the first plague, the river turns to blood, fish die, and drinking water becomes hard to obtain (Exod 7:14–25). The narrative makes the source of Egyptian subsistence the occasion of its disruption.

Deut 11:10–11 contrasts irrigation in Egypt with the dependence of Canaan's landscape on rainfall. Isa 19:5–10 imagines the river and its branches drying up, bringing distress to fishers and those who work flax. Jer 46:7–8 likens Egypt's advance to the river in flood. Ezek 29:3–10 pictures Pharaoh as a great creature among the channels and challenges his claim to have made the Nile. Amos 8:8 and 9:5 invoke its rising and sinking to portray the movement of the land under judgment.

Gen 2:13 says the Gihon flows around Cush, a name often associated with regions to Egypt's south. This has encouraged comparison with the Nile. The river belongs to the larger geography of the [[garden-of-god|garden of Eden]], and the identification remains uncertain.
