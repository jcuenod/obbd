## Overview

Sennacherib was king of Assyria from 705 to 681 BCE. He succeeded Sargon II, made Nineveh his capital, and claimed victories across the empire. His campaign against Judah in 701 BCE is known from Assyrian inscriptions, archaeology, and biblical accounts. He later destroyed Babylon and was assassinated before Esarhaddon secured the throne. The different records make it possible to compare the king’s presentation of imperial power with biblical speeches that put similar claims in the mouths of him and his officials.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[sennacherib-annals|royal annals]] describe campaigns and building works. Several copies recount fighting in the Levant and the confinement of Hezekiah within Jerusalem while celebrating tribute and territorial gains. Reliefs from the Southwest Palace at [[nineveh|Nineveh]] show the capture of Lachish. Other inscriptions record the construction of the palace, city defenses, watercourses, and gardens. Together they document a ruler making his capital a center of administration and royal display.

Sennacherib tried repeatedly to secure control over Babylonia. After prolonged conflict his forces took and destroyed Babylon in 689 BCE. An account of this destruction proclaims the extent of the devastation in the king’s voice. Babylonian records give an independent chronological context for the reign and for Sennacherib’s death. A chronicle dates his murder to 681 BCE and attributes it to his son. Succession was contested before [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] assumed control.

## Biblical Portrayal

The accounts in 2 Kgs 18:13–19:37, Isa 36–37, and 2 Chr 32:1–23 place Sennacherib within the story of an Assyrian assault on Judah. The official addressing Jerusalem asks why its God should be able to save it when the gods of other peoples have failed (2 Kgs 18:33–35). In 2 Kgs 19:23–24 the king’s confidence is expressed through imagery of chariots, mountains, cedars, and water. These are recognizable elements of imperial accounts of campaign and mastery. The biblical narrative reuses them as words spoken by a ruler whose claims will be challenged.

Second Kings 19:37 names two sons in the attack on Sennacherib and gives Nineveh as the setting of his death. It connects the notice with his return from Judah in the preceding verse. The [[sennacherib-campaign-701|campaign]] and the death are separated by about twenty years in the external chronology. The narrative compresses that interval and makes the death part of its portrayal of the limits of Assyrian power.

## Historical Assessment

The Assyrian and biblical texts agree that Sennacherib’s forces attacked Judah and that Jerusalem remained in the hands of its ruler. They describe the campaign’s outcome differently. The Assyrian record focuses on conquest and submission, while Kings and Isaiah attach the city’s survival to divine deliverance. The Egyptian story in Herodotus (*Histories* 2.141) also describes an Assyrian threat overcome in an unexpected way. Its setting and details differ, and its value as evidence for events around Jerusalem is limited.

Descriptions of Sennacherib’s murder agree in associating it with his family. Differences in the number and names of participants cannot all be resolved. The combination of independent evidence for the campaign, the king’s building, and his death makes it possible to set the biblical narrative alongside a much wider political career.
