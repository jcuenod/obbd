## Overview

A damaged clay tablet preserves part of a seventh-century BCE agreement imposed by the Assyrian king Esarhaddon on Baal, ruler of Tyre. Its terms concern trade, shipping and Assyrian oversight of an important Phoenician city. The curses call on several gods worshipped in the coastal region as well as gods of Assyria. The agreement provides a political and economic setting for reading Ezekiel's portrayal of Tyre as a maritime trading center, and shows how an empire could regulate the movement of people and goods at sea.

## Manuscripts and Date

The Akkadian treaty survives in fragments and is normally dated within the reign of [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]]. Some place it after the Assyrian destruction of Sidon in 677 or 676 BCE, when control of the coast was being reorganized. The text lacks a preserved date that establishes the suggestion. The agreement is the principal surviving original treaty text between Assyria and a Phoenician city. [@saa2] [@rinap4]

## Contents

The terms mention ports or trading regions, including Akko, Dor and the Philistine coast. Byblos and the Lebanon also appear in the damaged route and access provisions. If a Tyrian ship wrecks within the specified jurisdiction, its cargo belongs to Esarhaddon, while the people on board are to be spared and returned. Such provisions suggest that Assyria had an interest in protecting maritime traffic while asserting authority over its material proceeds. [@saa2]

The fragmentary text also provides for the participation of an Assyrian representative in decisions affecting [[tyre|Tyre]]. At the end, divine sanctions invoke deities including Bethel, Anat-Bethel, Baal of Heaven, Baal of Malage, Baal of Zaphon, [[melqart|Melqart]], Eshmun and Astarte. The list shows the range of divine names available for enforcing obligations to the Assyrian king. [@saa2] [@ddd]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ezek 27:12–25 presents a wide trading network and draws on Tyre's maritime wealth. Ezek 27:25–36 turns the ship image into a lament for the city's downfall. The treaty gives concrete evidence of the commercial activities and risks that underlie such images. Its shipwreck clause treats the wreck as an administrative problem, while Ezekiel uses shipwreck to portray the ruin of a trading city.

Jer 48:13 compares Moab's disappointment in its god to Israel's disappointment in Bethel. The treaty shows that Bethel could be a divine name in the region. Interpreters differ over whether the biblical verse refers to the god Bethel or to the sanctuary and worship at Bethel. The treaty makes the divine-name reading possible without deciding how Jeremiah uses the word. [@saa2] [@ddd]

## Scholarly Issues

The treaty does not give a complete picture of Tyre's political and commercial position. Damaged lines leave the exact extent of some rights and duties uncertain. Later Assyrian sources report a siege of Baal of Tyre under Ashurbanipal, showing that the earlier arrangement did not prevent another conflict. The preserved agreement is evidence for negotiations and demands at a particular stage in Assyria's relationship with the city. [@saa2] [@anet]
