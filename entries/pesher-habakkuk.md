## Overview

The Habakkuk Pesher is a Hebrew commentary from Qumran Cave 1 on the first two chapters of Habakkuk. It quotes the prophet in sequence and explains his words in relation to the author’s community, its leaders, and its enemies. The surviving manuscript is usually dated by its handwriting to the late first century BCE. Its interpretation of the Kittim and its references to the Teacher of Righteousness offer evidence for how one Jewish group read prophecy. They also provide a comparison for later biblical writers who applied prophetic words to their own circumstances.

## Description and Provenance

The manuscript now known as 1QpHab was among the first [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] recovered from Cave 1 near Qumran in 1947. The leather scroll survives in thirteen columns, with damaged areas. Most of its writing uses the Jewish square script. The four-letter divine name, YHWH, is written in older [[paleo-hebrew-script|Hebrew letter forms]]. The distinction shows how scribes could mark a sacred name visually within a text written in another script.

## Contents

A *pesher*, or interpretation, ordinarily quotes a passage and follows it with an explanation. Here the quoted text moves from Habakkuk 1 into Habakkuk 2. The explanations link the prophet’s words with the Teacher of Righteousness, the Wicked Priest, the Man of the Lie, and other figures and groups. The titles tell readers how the commentary judges these people. They do not usually supply personal names.

The Kittim are interpreted as a conquering power with military practices that fit Rome. The description of offerings to their standards (1QpHab 6:3–5) is one reason many specialists identify them as Romans. That reading places the interpretation in a later historical setting than the Chaldeans named in Hab 1:6. The date of the surviving copy and the dates of episodes it interprets are separate matters.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Hab 2:2–3 tells of a vision written for an appointed time. The pesher treats such timing as a mystery disclosed to the Teacher, through whom the community understands the prophet’s words. Its application of Hab 2:4 links the righteous with fidelity in relation to the Teacher and the community. Its reading of Hab 2:15 turns the prophet’s woe into an accusation against the Wicked Priest in an episode connected with the Day of Atonement. These applications show how the commentary makes a prophetic passage speak to a community’s contested history.

Rom 1:17 and Heb 10:37–38 also make use of Habakkuk, with distinct emphases on faith, endurance, and fulfillment. Acts 2:16–21 applies a passage from Joel to an event taking place in the narrative. These texts exemplify different ways of reading prophecy as pertinent to more recent events. The Qumran evidence helps give historical context to this manner of reading without establishing direct contact among the authors.

## Scholarly Issues

Jonathan and Simon of the Hasmonean family have both been proposed as the Wicked Priest. Other interpreters have considered later rulers, or have argued that the title covers multiple priests in material gathered across time. The titles and events rarely provide enough external anchors to choose among reconstructions. The commentary ends after material from Habakkuk 2. Its failure to discuss chapter 3 does not establish that its author lacked that chapter, since the scope of the original commentary is itself part of the question.
