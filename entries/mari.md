## Overview

Mari was a city on the middle Euphrates in what is now eastern Syria. Founded in the early third millennium BCE, it remained an important center until [[hammurabi|Hammurabi of Babylon]] destroyed its palace in the eighteenth century BCE. Excavators recovered about 20,000 clay tablets from the palace, including letters to its last king, Zimri-Lim. The records show how rulers received prophetic messages, dealt with tribal groups, and made treaties. They offer early examples of practices familiar from the Hebrew Bible without establishing a direct link between Mari and the biblical narratives.

## Location and Identification

The ruins of Mari lie at Tell Hariri, near Syria's border with Iraq. The city stood on a terrace on the right bank of the Euphrates, along a route connecting Syria with southern Mesopotamia. Inscriptions found during the first excavation campaign, including finds from the temple of Ishtar, the goddess of love and war, identified the site as ancient Mari.

## Archaeological History

French excavations began in 1933 under André Parrot. He uncovered the great royal palace used by Zimri-Lim as well as temples and earlier buildings. Jean-Claude Margueron succeeded him and clarified the layout and development of the third-millennium city. Pascal Butterlin later directed work in the city center and east of the palace. The site was badly damaged and looted during the Syrian civil war.

Mari was rebuilt between its early phases and its final period of prominence under rulers identified as [[amorites|Amorites]], a name used for groups with roots west of southern Mesopotamia. The great palace was already standing when Zimri-Lim came to power. Its courtyards, painted rooms, and storage areas give a picture of royal ceremony and administration, while the [[mari-letters|palace archive]] documents decisions and relationships beyond the city walls. Hammurabi took Mari around 1761 BCE and destroyed it around 1759 BCE, using the middle chronology, a conventional way of assigning calendar dates to the reigns of Babylonian kings. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the conversion of Hammurabi's conquest and destruction year names to middle-chronology dates -->

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The palace letters are among the most important sources for comparing Israelite prophecy with [[prophecy-in-the-ane|prophecy elsewhere in the ancient Near East]]. Religious specialists included the *āpilum* ("answerer," a speaker for a deity) and the *muḫḫûm* (a religious specialist associated with ecstatic behavior). People without named prophetic offices also reported divine messages, some received in [[dreams-and-dream-interpretation|dreams]]. In one letter, Adad, the storm god of Aleppo, claims to have restored Zimri-Lim to his father's throne. The message resembles Nathan's address to David in 2 Sam 7:1–17 in linking kingship to divine support, though Nathan announces an enduring dynasty rather than a restoration [@nissinen-prophets].

Mari officials did not necessarily accept a message merely because someone claimed divine authority. Some letters send the speaker's hair and a piece of clothing, apparently so that specialists could seek confirmation by interpreting signs [@nissinen-prophets]. The biblical texts address the same problem but use different criteria: Deut 13:1–5 (MT 13:2–6) rejects any appeal to other gods, even if a sign comes true, while Jer 28 makes fulfillment a test of a prophecy of peace. Neither passage describes the Mari procedure.

Some Mari letters record the killing of a donkey to seal an agreement [@cos3]. This is comparable to the use of animals in the [[covenant-cutting-ceremony|covenant ceremony]] in Gen 15:9–10, 17, though Genesis depicts a smoking furnace and a flaming torch passing between pieces, not two parties killing a donkey. The Mari name *Binu-Yamina* ("sons of the right" or "sons of the south") also resembles Benjamin (Gen 35:18), but the resemblance does not identify the groups. Older scholarship associated with William F. Albright used such comparisons to support the historical setting of the biblical ancestors. The parallels illustrate shared types of names and practices; they do not date or authenticate the stories.

The palace letters also name [[hazor|Hazor]] and its king [@cos3]. They attest that Hazor was a royal center connected to Mari's world in the eighteenth century BCE. That evidence helps explain the prominence of the city in Josh 11:10, but it does not independently attest the conquest described there.
