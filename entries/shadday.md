## Overview

Shadday is a divine name or title in the Hebrew Bible, appearing both alone and in the phrase El Shadday. Genesis associates it with the patriarchs, Exodus makes it part of an account of the revelation of God’s name, and Job uses it frequently in poetry. Ancient translations helped establish the traditional rendering “Almighty.” The Hebrew term’s derivation remains uncertain. The Deir ʿAlla inscription may preserve a related term for a group of divine beings, giving an important comparison without settling the original sense.

## Sources and Iconography

The eighth-century BCE [[deir-alla-inscription|Deir ʿAlla inscription]] from the Jordan Valley refers to divine figures in the story of Balaam. One designation is spelled *šdyn*. Some scholars take it to be related to Shadday and understand it in connection with a divine assembly. The inscription’s spelling, language, and damaged condition leave room for debate about the exact relationship. It does not identify one of the figures as the God named Shadday in the biblical narratives. No securely identified image establishes this title’s meaning.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 17:1 introduces the name El Shadday in an appearance to Abram. Gen 28:3, 35:11, and 48:3 associate it with blessing, offspring, and the promises to the patriarchs. In Exod 6:3 God contrasts appearing to the ancestors as El Shadday with being known by the name YHWH. The passage gives a theological account of revelation and has an important place in analysis of the Priestly narrative. It cannot by itself establish the chronological distribution of divine titles across early Israel.

Gen 49:25 places Shadday near the term for breasts in a blessing, creating a possible verbal association. In Num 24:4, 16 Balaam speaks of a vision of Shadday. Ruth 1:20–21 uses the title in Naomi’s account of suffering, and Job 6:4 is one of many poetic examples in Job. Ps 91:1 links it with protection, while Isa 13:6 associates it with destruction in an oracle. Ezek 10:5 uses it in describing an overwhelming sound. Personal names in Num 1:5–12, including Ammishaddai and Zurishaddai, contain a similar element. The date of a name’s use is difficult to infer from its appearance in a list.

## Scholarly Issues

One widespread proposal compares Akkadian *šadû*, “mountain,” and understands Shadday in relation to a mountain or high place. Other explanations appeal to Hebrew *śādeh*, “field,” or *šad*, “breast.” The fertility language in parts of Genesis can explain an association within a text without proving an etymology. Some scholars see the title as preserving older language, while others emphasize that later writers could deliberately use archaic-sounding diction. Both are possible ways for a term to occur in a relatively late work.

Greek renderings vary by book. The Greek of Job often uses *pantokratōr*, a term expressing universal power, and Latin *omnipotens* reinforced the interpretation behind English “Almighty.” A rabbinic explanation interprets the name through the idea of sufficiency. These renderings are important for the title’s reception. The Deir ʿAlla comparison and the biblical contexts leave the original derivation open.
