## Overview

Rhetorical education in the Greco-Roman world trained speakers to arrange arguments, address particular audiences, and make effective use of questions, examples, and appeals. Such techniques also circulated outside formal schooling in public speech, letters, and moral instruction. They offer background for the objections Paul raises in Romans and for the different ways Acts presents speeches before officials. Recognizing a familiar technique helps readers follow an argument, while leaving a speaker's educational history open.

## Greco-Roman Evidence

Quintilian's late first-century work on the education of an orator explains how an opening can prepare listeners to hear a case. He also distinguishes questions that seek information from those used for emphasis, challenge, or anticipation of an objection. His examples include answering a question posed by the speaker. The handbook systematizes practices that could be learned or recognized through ordinary exposure to public speech [@quintilian-orator, 4.1.1–7] [@quintilian-orator, 9.2.6–17].

Persuasion also served moral instruction. A writer could rehearse an objection to expose its weakness or give an example that made a pattern of conduct recognizable. Conversations, letters, and public argument offered overlapping settings for these techniques. See [[letter-writing-conventions]] and [[stoics-epicureans-and-moral-philosophy]] for related evidence.

## In the Bible

In Rom 3:1–9 Paul sets out questions and gives responses as he moves the argument forward. The form lets readers consider an objection before hearing how Paul meets it. In 1 Cor 1:17–2:5 he calls attention to the kind of speech he used and where he wants his audience to locate the force of the proclamation. The letter still makes deliberate use of persuasive language. Second Corinthians 10:10–11 reports a criticism contrasting Paul's letters with his public presence and speech.

In Acts 24:1–9 Tertullus begins by praising Felix and then presents the accusations against Paul. Such an opening can be read alongside ancient descriptions of how speakers tried to gain a hearing from a judge. The speech reaches readers through Acts' narrative, which gives it a particular role within the scene.

## Interpretive Significance

Formal rhetoric supplies language for describing a move within a speech or letter. The comparison is strongest when it clarifies what that move accomplishes for the audience. The presence of questions, an opening appeal, or a moral example does not by itself establish formal enrollment in a school or use of a specific handbook.
