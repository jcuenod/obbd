## Overview

Byblos, called Gebal in the Hebrew Bible, was an ancient port on the coast of what is now Lebanon. Its temples, burials, and inscriptions reflect long ties with Egypt and an important place in Phoenician trade and writing. Bronze Age letters describe the city under pressure from its neighbors, while royal inscriptions show its rulers presenting themselves in their own language. Biblical texts mention the territory of Gebal and associate its people with preparing materials for Solomon's temple and maintaining the ships of Tyre.

## Location and Identification

Ancient Byblos lies at modern Jbeil, north of Beirut. It occupied a coastal site with access to shipping and to the mountains inland. The local name Gebal appears in Semitic sources. The Greek name Byblos was also associated with the word for papyrus, though this linguistic link cannot establish the scale of a papyrus trade at the port [@abd].

## Archaeological History

Egyptian objects and inscriptions show links with Byblos from the third millennium BCE. The city was well placed to send timber, including cedar, to Egypt. Its ancient sanctuaries include the temple of Baalat Gebal, the “Lady of Byblos,” and the Bronze Age Temple of the Obelisks, named for its many upright stones. Bronze Age inscriptions from the city also preserve a script conventionally called Byblos syllabic, whose signs have not been securely deciphered [@anep] [@abd].

Rib-Hadda of Byblos is the most prolific correspondent in the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]]. He repeatedly appeals to Egypt for help against enemies and reports political instability among neighboring towns. He also refers to the Apiru, a designation used for people on the margins of settled political communities. His letters present a ruler's efforts to win support, with language shaped by that aim [@moran-amarna].

In the Egyptian [[report-of-wenamun|story of Wenamun]], an envoy seeks cedar from Zakar-Baal of Byblos, who presses him for payment. The episode illustrates how an Egyptian writer imagined relations when Egyptian leverage on the coast was weaker. Iron Age records of rulers such as Yehimilk, Abibaal, Elibaal, and Shipitbaal, together with the [[ahiram-sarcophagus|Ahiram sarcophagus inscription]], are important evidence for Phoenician alphabetic writing. The later inscription of Yehawmilk also records devotion to the Lady of Byblos [@ael] [@kai]. These monuments show the continued importance of the goddess in royal self-presentation, as well as changes in the script over time.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 13:5 includes the land of the Gebalites among territories still to be taken. In 1 Kgs 5:18 (MT 5:32), Gebalites help the builders of Solomon and Hiram prepare timber and stone. Trade with Egypt and the timber sought by Wenamun provide a wider setting for the association of Byblos with such work. Ezek 27:9 pictures Tyre as a ship whose seams are maintained by skilled men from Gebal, another use of the city's reputation in a literary text.
