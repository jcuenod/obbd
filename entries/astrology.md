## Overview

Celestial divination was the interpretation of events in the sky as signs about affairs on earth. In ancient Mesopotamia, court scholars treated eclipses, planetary appearances, and other phenomena as possible messages about kings and countries. Much later, Babylonian astrologers also developed horoscopes concerned with individual births. The distinction helps explain the biblical descriptions of court specialists and prophecies about heavenly signs. It also supplies context for the eastern magi in Matthew, who understand a star as an indication of a royal birth.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian scholars collected celestial omens, or statements connecting signs with possible outcomes. The long series called [[enuma-anu-enlil|Enuma Anu Enlil]] includes signs involving the moon, sun, weather, and planets. An entry could link an eclipse or the timing of the moon's appearance to an outcome such as a change in a king's fortunes. The lists set out what particular signs could mean. Reports and letters from the seventh century BCE show how scholars working for Assyrian kings applied, compared, and sometimes questioned the written judgments [@horowitz-cosmic-geography].

Astronomical observation and the interpretation of signs often went together. Babylonian horoscopes, however, introduced a different focus by recording celestial positions for an individual's birth. The earliest surviving examples date to the late fifth century BCE. The zodiac, a division of the sun's apparent path into twelve signs, provided a framework for locating the moon and planets. The development of horoscopes did not end the use of older omens concerning a ruler or state.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 47:13 challenges Babylon's experts in observing and interpreting the sky to save the city from what is coming. Jer 10:2 tells its audience to resist the fear other nations attach to celestial signs. Both texts assume that heavenly phenomena were interpreted as warnings, and both place limits on the authority of those readings.

In Dan 2:2, 27 and 4:7 (MT 4:4), court specialists are summoned to interpret dreams or questioned about their ability to do so. The Chaldeans appear among several groups of experts. The name also designated a people, but Greek and Roman authors could use it for experts in astrology. Daniel's grouping can be compared with that professional usage, especially in the Hellenistic period, without treating every court specialist as an astrologer or dating the book from the word alone.

## Interpretive Significance

Gen 1:14–18 gives the lights in the sky a role in marking signs and times and providing light for the earth. The sun and moon are described by their functions, without their ordinary names. Some interpreters understand this wording as a deliberate response to the worship of heavenly bodies. Others take it as part of the account's concern with assigning functions within the created order. The text itself does not name a particular system of celestial [[divination|divination, or interpretation of divine signs]].

The Greek term *magoi* (“magi”) in Matt 2:1–12 could evoke Persian religious specialists or, in wider Greco-Roman usage, eastern experts in interpreting signs. The narrative leaves the visitors' homeland unspecified beyond the east. Their reading of the star concerns the birth of the king of the Jews, and a later dream warns them against returning to Herod. These details give the star a role within the story's account of recognition and guidance.
