## Overview

The lion hunt reliefs are carved gypsum wall panels made for the North Palace of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh in the seventh century BCE. They show attendants releasing lions and the king confronting them in a controlled setting. The panels give a powerful visual account of royal strength and the ruler's claim to protect his people. They provide a comparison for biblical poetry in which kings appear as lions, shepherds defend their flocks, and Nineveh itself becomes a lions' den overtaken by judgment.

## Description and Provenance

The panels once lined rooms in the North Palace at [[nineveh|Nineveh]]. Hormuzd Rassam excavated important parts of the palace in the nineteenth century, and major sections of the hunt reliefs are now in the British Museum. The carvings are dated to the mid-seventh century BCE, in the reign of [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]]. They were part of a palace program that made the activities and authority of the king visible to those admitted to its rooms [@anep, fig. 626].

## Contents

The sequences depict the release of captive lions, attendants and bystanders, and the king's encounters with the animals. Lions are shown at different stages of movement, injury, and death. The scene including a wounded lioness belongs to the same larger representation of a royal hunt. The individual treatment of the animals gives power to the scene while the king and his attendants maintain control of the event.

The use of captive lions shows that these scenes include staged hunting. Other Assyrian evidence likewise presents fighting lions as a royal achievement. The palace context helps explain how an elite performance could communicate a wider ideal. Animal danger stands for threats against which the king claims to protect the land [@keel-symbolism, 89–94, 281–283].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ezek 19:1–9 likens Judah's princes to lion cubs. Pits, nets, and a cage give the poem images of capture and removal. Assyrian art confirms that lions could be held and released from cages, while the biblical poem uses capture to depict the fall of rulers. Nah 2:11–13 (MT 2:12–14) imagines Nineveh as a lions' den, once filled with prey and due to be destroyed. In its Assyrian setting the image plausibly turns the language of royal lion power against the imperial capital.

The king who protects can also be imagined as a shepherd. David recalls defending his flock against lions and bears in 1 Sam 17:34–37. Amos 3:12 uses the remains snatched from a lion's mouth to express the small scale of survival. Judg 14:5–6 portrays Samson killing a lion, a sign of extraordinary strength. These biblical examples draw on real pastoral danger and on its literary potential [@king-stager-life].

## Scholarly Issues

Dan 6:16–24 (MT 6:17–25) describes lions kept in a pit under royal authority. Assyrian reliefs show that royal establishments could keep captive lions, although they are earlier than the setting of Daniel and give no evidence for its particular event. The palace scenes likewise give a setting for the royal and political force of biblical [[lion-imagery|lion imagery]] without requiring a direct literary use of the panels.
