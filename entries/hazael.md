## Overview

Hazael was king of Aram-Damascus from about 842 BCE into the closing years of the ninth century. The annals of Shalmaneser III record Assyrian campaigns against him. Aramaic inscriptions on objects attest his royal name, and many scholars attribute the Tel Dan Stele to him. The books of Kings depict his rise and his campaigns against Israel, Philistine Gath, and Judah. The sources together establish his importance in the southern Levant, while leaving open the circumstances of his accession and the details of particular campaigns.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The annals of [[shalmaneser-iii|Shalmaneser III]] report fighting against Hazael in 841 BCE. In an account of the change of ruler, the Assyrian king describes Hazael as a man with no recognized dynastic standing. At Mount Saniru the account claims victory over Hazael’s forces. Hazael withdrew into [[damascus|Damascus]], which the Assyrians failed to capture. The same campaign is associated with receipt of tribute from [[jehu|Jehu]] of Israel. Later Assyrian fighting against Hazael again damaged his territory without ending his rule.

The [[tel-dan-stele|Tel Dan Stele]] describes an Aramean king’s victories over Israel and the House of David. Hazael is the leading candidate for the speaker, whose name is lost. The inscription speaks of the speaker’s father in a royal context. If Hazael composed it, that language has to be considered alongside the Assyrian dismissal of his ancestry. The term father could claim political succession, or the sources could offer incompatible versions of his pedigree. The fragmentary state of the text keeps the identification conditional.

Aramaic inscriptions on horse equipment found at Samos and Eretria name Hazael and credit Hadad with giving booty to him. An ivory fragment from Arslan Tash also names Hazael. These objects show that items associated with his court traveled widely, sometimes reaching sanctuaries far from Aram. Their findspots cannot indicate that he campaigned in Greece or directly controlled the place where each item was ultimately deposited.

## Biblical Portrayal

In 1 Kgs 19:15–17 Elijah receives the commission to anoint Hazael over Aram. In 2 Kgs 8:7–15 Elisha meets Hazael during Ben-hadad’s illness and foresees the harm he will do. Hazael succeeds after Ben-hadad dies. Fighting at Ramoth-gilead in 2 Kgs 8:28–29 brings him into conflict with Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah. In 2 Kgs 10:32–33 he takes territory east of the Jordan, and 2 Kgs 13:3, 22 portrays his domination continuing under Jehoahaz. Amos 1:3–4 condemns Damascus for violence in Gilead and names Hazael’s house as a target of judgment.

Second Kings 12:17–18 (MT 12:18–19) reports the fall of [[gath|Gath]] and says that King Joash diverted Hazael from Jerusalem with valuables from the temple and palace. Excavators at Tell eṣ-Ṣafi, widely identified with Gath, document a substantial late ninth-century destruction and features interpreted as siege works. Their chronology makes the correlation strong. The finds do not include a statement identifying the attackers.

## Historical Assessment

Assyrian records independently establish Hazael’s kingship and military power. Kings interprets political reversals through prophecy and divine judgment, while the Mesopotamian annals celebrate the Assyrian king’s victories. The Tel Dan inscription, if attributable to Hazael, adds another partisan account. Its claims to have killed royal enemies are difficult to reconcile in detail with Kings’ account of Jehu’s revolt. Royal claims may take credit for outcomes achieved through indirect action, but the surviving sources do not resolve who killed the two kings.
