## Overview

Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread are linked observances in biblical accounts of Israel’s departure from Egypt. Exodus describes households eating a slaughtered animal and marking doors with its blood. Deuteronomy directs the sacrifice to a central sanctuary, and later texts describe communal observance in Jerusalem. A damaged letter from a Jewish community in Persian-period Egypt mentions regulations for unleavened bread. These witnesses show changes in how the festivals were observed and remembered. The earlier origin of each practice remains a matter of historical reconstruction.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Rituals in several ancient Near Eastern settings placed protective substances or objects at thresholds. Mesopotamian examples include figurines near entrances and procedures involving substances applied to parts of a doorway. They illustrate how a building’s boundary could be treated as a point of danger. The [[apotropaic-doorposts-and-thresholds|protective rite]] in Exodus uses that familiar space to identify the households to be spared. The surviving parallels do not establish a particular foreign origin for the Passover rite.

The [[elephantine-passover-letter|Elephantine letter]] from 419 BCE gives instructions to a Jewish military community in Egypt. The surviving and restored lines concern the period for eating unleavened bread and avoiding leaven. The name Passover itself is restored in damaged text in the usual edition and cannot be read directly on the fragment. References interpreted as Passover occur on some ostraca from the community. How the observance related to the biblical festival and the authority behind the letter are separate questions.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 12:1–14, 21–23 depicts the household’s animal, meal, and blood on the door frame as protection from the destructive event in Egypt. Exod 12:43–49 addresses who may participate and under what conditions. Exod 12:15–20 establishes a seven-day period of unleavened bread, and Exod 34:25 combines attention to leaven with instructions about the Passover sacrifice. The arrangements join ritual practice to the remembered deliverance of Israel.

Deut 16:1–8 brings the sacrifice to the sanctuary chosen by YHWH and combines it with the observance of unleavened bread. The verb translated “boil” or “cook” in Deut 16:7 can appear to differ from the requirement in Exod 12:9 that the animal be roasted. Second Chronicles 35:13 applies that verb to cooking the Passover with fire, while placing other offerings in pots. Many readers see this as an interpretation that joins the language of Deuteronomy to the procedure of Exodus. The account in 2 Kgs 23:21–23 associates a major observance with [[josiah|Josiah]] and the covenant book. Chronicles also depicts an earlier observance in the reign of Hezekiah, with accommodation for participants unable to come in the first month (2 Chr 30:1–27).

Num 9:1–14 provides for a second-month observance for those unable to keep the first. Josh 5:10–12 sets the festival at Gilgal alongside eating produce of the land. Ezra 6:19–22 links Passover and Unleavened Bread with the restored temple and community. The different settings are part of the biblical presentation of the festival, though reconstructing a continuous institutional sequence from them is difficult.

## Interpretive Significance

Many scholars infer that Passover and Unleavened Bread began as distinct rites that were eventually combined. A family sacrifice for protection has sometimes been linked to pastoral life, and Unleavened Bread to spring harvest. Such comparisons suggest possible earlier forms but no surviving description independently attests either original festival. The Hebrew root *psḥ* has been interpreted through ideas of passing over, protecting, and limping. The use in the narrative centers on the deliverance of the houses, while derivation of the festival’s name remains debated.
