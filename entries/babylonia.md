## Overview

The Neo-Babylonian Empire was the kingdom based at Babylon that replaced Assyria as the dominant power in much of Mesopotamia and the Levant between 626 and 539 BCE. Its kings included Nabopolassar, Nebuchadnezzar II, and Nabonidus. Its armies captured Jerusalem, ended Judah's monarchy, and deported groups of Judeans. The empire's chronicles record several events also reported in the Bible, while its administrative tablets document life after deportation. The Bible's use of “Chaldeans” for the Babylonians reflects the complicated history of peoples and political power in southern Mesopotamia.

## Sources

The [[babylonian-chronicles|Babylonian chronicles]] date battles, changes of ruler, and campaigns by the years of each king's reign. Their coverage is uneven. Most surviving Neo-Babylonian royal inscriptions celebrate construction and the kings' support for the gods, so chronicles are particularly valuable for political events. The continuing production of contracts, accounts, and other records across the empire documents details seldom found in royal accounts [@abc, 8–28] [@cos2, 2.121–2.123] [@cos1, 1.137].

The oil ration tablets of the royal palace name Jehoiachin and members of his family. The [[al-yahudu-tablets|Āl-Yāhūdu tablets]] give a view of families living in rural Babylonia under obligations to the authorities. Written under Persian rule about a century after the deportations, the Murašû business archive records people with Judean names involved in land and credit transactions around Nippur. The three sets of records illuminate different circumstances and groups within the [[babylonian-exile|Judean exile]].

## History

From the ninth century BCE, Assyrian records name Chaldean groups in southern Babylonia. Some controlled territories from which they competed for rule of [[babylon|Babylon]]. Among them was [[merodach-baladan|Merodach-baladan]], a king of Babylon who resisted Assyria. The label “Chaldean” is often applied to the later Neo-Babylonian dynasty, but the origins of its founder Nabopolassar and his relationship to the earlier Chaldean groups remain debated.

Nabopolassar took power in Babylon in 626 BCE. The Babylonians and Medes captured Nineveh in 612 BCE and continued campaigning against the remnants of the Assyrian state. Nabopolassar's son [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] defeated an Egyptian army at Carchemish in 605 BCE, establishing Babylonian supremacy in much of the west [@abc, 88, 94, 99].

The chronicle for Nebuchadnezzar's early years records his capture of the city of Judah on the second day of the month Adar in his seventh year, in March 597 BCE. The event corresponds to the surrender of Jerusalem and removal of King Jehoiachin in Kings. Another siege ended in Jerusalem's destruction in 587 or 586 BCE. Babylonian rulers used deportation as the Assyrians had done, but the evidence for Judah does not show a program of importing displaced outsiders on the scale known for Assyrian Samaria. The empire ended with [[fall-of-babylon-539|Cyrus's capture of Babylon]] from [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] in 539 BCE [@glassner-chronicles, no. 24, 226–229].

## In the Bible

The Babylonian embassy to Hezekiah in 2 Kgs 20:12–19 belongs to the period before Nabopolassar's dynasty. Later passages recount Judah's subjection to Nebuchadnezzar, rebellion, and defeat (2 Kgs 24:1–17; 25:1–21). Jer 27:1–15 urges submission to the king of Babylon. Hab 1:6–11 uses the name Chaldeans for the invading power.

Ezekiel situates his vision among deportees by the Chebar (Ezek 1:1–3), generally identified with a Babylonian canal attested in texts from Nippur. Daniel uses the royal court as the setting for stories of Judeans serving foreign kings. In that setting, “Chaldeans” can also designate a group of court specialists who interpret dreams or signs (Dan 1–5). The scope of the term depends on the passage.
