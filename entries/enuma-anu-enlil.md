## Overview

*Enuma Anu Enlil* is a Mesopotamian collection of omens read from the sky. Its traditions reach back into the second millennium BCE, and many surviving copies come from the Assyrian royal center of Nineveh in the seventh century BCE. The series links events such as eclipses, unusual weather and the movements of planets with predictions about rulers and their lands. It provides a historical setting for biblical references to specialists who read signs in the heavens, especially the challenge to Babylon's experts in Isaiah.

## Manuscripts and Date

The Akkadian title comes from the series' opening words, referring to the gods Anu and Enlil. Its sources preserve earlier material, and scholars generally place much of its organization in the later second millennium BCE. First-millennium copies from the [[library-of-ashurbanipal|scholarly collections at Nineveh]] and elsewhere transmit versions with some differences in tablet divisions and readings. The full series comprised about seventy tablets, though many parts survive only in fragments.

One tablet records the appearances and disappearances of Venus in a sequence associated with the Old Babylonian ruler Ammi-saduqa. The surviving copies are later. The tablet is therefore evidence for the transmission of earlier observations or records through later scribal practice. Its use for dating the king's reign has been debated. [@abd]

## Contents

Groups of tablets treat phenomena relating to the moon, the sun, weather and planets or stars. Many entries join an observed or imagined sign to an expected consequence. A sign might be interpreted as affecting a king, a harvest or a country. The series provides examples and rules for [[astrology|celestial divination]] as a branch of learned practice. [@horowitz-cosmic-geography]

Reports to Neo-Assyrian kings quote entries alongside observations about current events. An eclipse judged dangerous for a king could prompt protective rituals. In one form of response, a substitute was placed in a royal role to take on the threatened fate. This practice shows that a prediction could guide a decision about ritual action rather than simply announce an unavoidable event.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 47:13 calls on Babylon's observers of the heavens to save the city in the face of disaster. Jeremiah similarly warns people against being dismayed by signs in the heavens (Jer 10:2). The Babylonian series and Assyrian reports help clarify why such specialists could be associated with the safety of a ruler or land. In Dan 2:2, 10, specialists are summoned to explain a royal dream, showing a broader range of court expertise. [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

The contingencies in *Enuma Anu Enlil* generally concern public affairs. Individual horoscopes, which use celestial positions at a person's birth, appear in surviving Babylonian texts from the late fifth century BCE onward. The distinction matters when reading biblical references to signs. The proposed relation between the darkened sun in Amos 8:9 and formal eclipse omens is disputed, since the verse can work as prophetic imagery without invoking a technical series.
