## Overview

Stone vessels and stepped pools are two kinds of archaeological evidence for Jewish purity practices around the time of the New Testament. People used vessels carved from chalk and pools suited to bodily immersion. Finds from Judea and Galilee show how these practices reached different places in the late Second Temple period. Biblical laws provide an older background for washing and the treatment of vessels. The archaeological evidence helps explain the stone jars in John and illustrates why later rabbinic rules need to be distinguished from earlier installations.

## Archaeological Evidence

Stone cups, bowls, and large jars appear in late Second Temple contexts. Workshops have been identified near Jerusalem and at sites in Galilee, including ʿEinot Amitai near Reina. People made some large vessels on a lathe. The distribution suggests that this craft served Jewish communities, and concentrations of finished vessels or production waste provide different evidence for local use and manufacture [@neaehl].

A *miqveh* is a pool for ritual immersion, with the plural *miqvaʾot*. The earliest securely identified examples appear around the late second or early first century BCE. Many have steps leading into a plastered basin, and some have features associated with water collection or movement. The structures vary. Later rabbinic specifications for volume and connection to a reservoir cannot be assigned to each early pool without examining its construction and context.

## In the Bible

Lev 11:32–33 directs that some vessels be washed or destroyed after contact with impurity, depending on the material. The passage does not list stone in the same way as earthenware. Later Jewish law treats stone vessels as resistant to ritual impurity. Their archaeological distribution gives evidence for the use of such vessels well before those later written formulations. Lev 15:13, 16, 18 describes washing after particular conditions, while the text itself does not prescribe the design of a later stepped pool.

John 2:6 explicitly identifies six stone jars with Jewish purification. The quantities given fit the scale of known large stone vessels, even though the narrative names no specific workshop type. Mark 7:1–5 refers to washing hands and utensils. Matt 23:25–26 uses cleaning cups and dishes as an image in an argument about conduct. John 3:25 likewise presupposes discussion of purification. The sources together illustrate the familiarity of these practices without making all Jewish groups identical in their interpretation.
