## Overview

The Āl-Yāhūdu tablets are clay records concerning descendants of Judean deportees and their neighbors in rural Babylonia. They date from 572 to 477 BCE and take their name from a settlement whose name means “Judahtown.” The documents record land, taxes or service obligations, debts, and other transactions from the Babylonian and Persian periods. They give a view of household life during and after the exile that complements the biblical emphasis on Jerusalem and on return. Their undocumented discovery limits what can be established about the original archive and its location.

## Manuscripts and Date

The documents are written in Babylonian cuneiform, a script impressed in clay with a wedge-shaped stylus. They concern Āl-Yāhūdu, Bīt-Našar, Bīt-Abī-râm, and other settlements generally placed in the countryside around Nippur. The known texts span the reigns of Nebuchadnezzar II to Xerxes I, across the Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE. Their dates come from the records themselves, which identify transactions by the reigning king and year. Evidence for their regional setting comes from the place names and the relationships between people and transactions.

The tablets surfaced on the antiquities market, and many entered the collection of David Sofer. Some were displayed at the Bible Lands Museum in Jerusalem in 2015. Without controlled excavation, the precise place of discovery and the original grouping of the documents are uncertain. Their market history also raises questions about the loss of archaeological context and the legal and ethical circumstances of their circulation. Those issues affect what the collection can establish about the settlements as a whole.

## Contents

Contracts and receipts record work on agricultural land, loans, leases, and obligations to administrators. Many households participated in arrangements under which land was assigned in return for taxes or service. Families could hold or cultivate land over several generations, and some individuals acted as intermediaries or creditors. The documents mainly record people who entered formal transactions. They give only indirect evidence for farming households whose activities left fewer written records.

Many people have names that incorporate a form of YHWH, the name of Judah's god. These forms include an initial *Yāhû-* and endings such as *-yāma* and *-yāḫu*. Babylonian names also occur, including alongside Judean names in the same families. The names reveal the persistence of naming traditions within a Babylonian legal and economic setting. They tell less about what families believed or how they worshiped. For a later comparison in the same region, see the [[murashu-archive|Murašû archive]].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Jer 29:4–7 urges deportees to build houses, plant gardens, and establish families in Babylonia. The tablets document later generations living in the kind of settled community envisioned in the letter. Ezek 1:1–3 and 3:15 place Judeans beside the Chebar waterway, and Ps 137:1 evokes the waterways of Babylonia in a lament for Zion. Rural settlements and irrigation networks help situate these passages. No tablet in the collection securely identifies Ezekiel's community at Tel-abib.

Ezra 2:1–2 introduces a list of people who returned to Judah. The documents from Āl-Yāhūdu and nearby communities continue well into the Persian period, showing that Judean life in Babylonia continued while returns were possible. The [[jehoiachin-ration-tablets|Jehoiachin ration tablets]] give a complementary view of Judeans connected to the royal court. Together the collections expand the geographical and social setting of the [[babylonian-exile|Babylonian exile]].

## Scholarly Issues

The name “Judahtown” and the distribution of Judean personal names support the interpretation that Babylonian authorities settled deportees in groups. The documents themselves mostly begin after that settlement was under way. Historians infer the link to [[deportation]] from the names, royal chronology, and broader evidence for forced resettlement. The surviving records give a detailed view of some communities while leaving the experiences of other deportees and their descendants less visible.
