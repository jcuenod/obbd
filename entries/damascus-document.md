## Overview

The Damascus Document is a Jewish work from the late Second Temple period that combines an account of a community's origins with rules for its members. Ancient copies survive among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and medieval copies come from the Cairo Genizah. The work explains how its community understood scripture, organized its settlements, and observed the Sabbath. Its regulations give a comparison for biblical commands and for disputes over Sabbath observance in the Gospels. Its references to “Damascus” and to a coming anointed figure have generated different readings of the community's history and expectations.

## Manuscripts and Date

Scholars first knew the text from two medieval manuscript witnesses preserved in the Cairo Genizah, a synagogue collection of retired texts. Solomon Schechter published them in 1910. The witnesses are often called CD-A and CD-B. The early form of the work is also represented by eight fragmentary copies from Cave 4 at [[qumran|Qumran]] and one each from Caves 5 and 6. Thus the work belongs among the [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] as well as among texts copied in medieval Egypt. [@abd, 2:8–10]

The ancient manuscripts preserve material missing from the Genizah copies, including material near the beginning, laws concerning skin disease, and provisions for discipline and expulsion. The witnesses represent a complex transmission history. Modern editions distinguish the columns of the Cairo manuscripts from the separately numbered fragments and lines of the Qumran copies. The date of the work's first composition and the dates of its revisions are less certain than the period when copies were in circulation.

## Contents

The section called the Admonition retells Israel's history as a series of failures and acts of divine mercy. Its account of the Teacher of Righteousness places an authoritative teacher within that history. Periods of 390 years and twenty years serve the work's interpretation of the past. Their precise starting points and historical application are debated. The document refers to a covenant in the land of Damascus, a name that has been understood as an actual place of exile or a symbolic designation.

The laws cover membership, oath-taking, purity, the [[sabbath|Sabbath]], and the responsibilities of community officials. They envision people living in camps with wives and children. The [[community-rule|Community Rule]] has different organizational emphases, and the relationship between the communities reflected in the two works remains debated. Some scholars compare the camps with Josephus's account of Essenes who married. That comparison alone cannot establish the identity of the camps.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The Admonition reads Amos 5:26–27 together with other scriptural images to explain the community's present and future. The star and scepter of Num 24:17 supply figures for its own interpretation. The citation in Acts 7:42–43 uses the Amos passage differently and names Babylon where the Hebrew form has Damascus. The examples show how different Jewish readers could bring the same prophetic text to bear on different accounts of exile and worship.

The Sabbath code in the Cairo Document, columns 10–11, includes a rule beginning the observance while the sun is above the horizon. It restricts carrying, a concern also found in Jer 17:21–22, and forbids lifting an animal from a pit on the Sabbath. Matt 12:11–12 and Luke 14:5 invoke rescue from a pit or well in arguments about Sabbath practice. The Damascus Document's rule on rescuing a person is difficult and mentions implements such as a ladder or rope. A related Qumran text, 4Q265, appears to permit use of a garment, though the text is fragmentary. These sources illustrate variation in the application of Sabbath rules.

## Scholarly Issues

Several passages in the document anticipate the anointed one of Aaron and Israel. This expression has been read as a reference to one figure with significance for the priesthood and people, or to more than one expected leader. The passage in the Community Rule about anointed figures provides a comparison without resolving the grammar of the Damascus passages. Differences in the texts also caution against combining their accounts of [[messianic-expectations|expected leaders]] into a single scheme.
