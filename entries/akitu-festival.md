## Overview

The *akītu* was a Mesopotamian festival known from several cities and periods. Its best-documented form is the first-millennium BCE celebration at Babylon in Nisannu, the first month of the Babylonian year. It included the movement of divine statues, ritual acts involving the king, and the recitation of the story of Marduk's rise to supremacy. Biblical texts about Babylonian images, royal investiture, and YHWH's kingship have been compared with elements of the festival. The comparisons range from an identifiable Babylonian setting in Isaiah to a debated reconstruction of an Israelite ceremony.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Festivals called *akītu* are attested in Mesopotamia as early as the third millennium BCE. They differed by city and season, and some fell in autumn rather than spring. The spring festival at [[babylon|Babylon]] centered on [[marduk|Marduk]], the city's patron god, and included [[nabu|Nabu]], whose statue came from nearby Borsippa. Processions moved [[cult-images|divine statues]] between sacred locations, including a building known as the *akītu* house. The detailed instructions for Babylon survive mainly in much later tablets from the Hellenistic period, so their sequence should be used cautiously for earlier centuries. [@cohen-cultic-calendars] [@anet]

The fourth day's ritual included the recitation of [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]], the epic that tells how Marduk attained kingship over the gods. In the fifth day's instructions, a priest removes the Babylonian king's insignia. The king rehearses his fidelity to Marduk and Babylon, receives reassurance, and has his insignia returned. The priest also strikes him on the cheek in a prescribed part of the ceremony. The ritual presents the king's standing as dependent on divine favor. [@anet] [@lambert-bcm]

The [[nabonidus-chronicle|Nabonidus Chronicle]] offers earlier evidence for the king's importance to the celebration. In several years when Nabonidus stayed at Tema in Arabia, it reports that Nabu did not come to Babylon, Marduk did not go out, and the festival did not take place. Regular temple offerings continued. The distinction shows that suspending the procession did not suspend all worship. [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 46:1–2 pictures Bel and Nebo, that is, Marduk and Nabu, stooping while animals carry their images as burdens. A Babylonian setting makes the reversal effective. Statues normally associated with the ceremonial movement of powerful gods appear unable to save even themselves. Isa 45:1 presents YHWH as grasping the right hand of Cyrus. Some compare the Babylonian king's act of taking Bel by the hand, though the divine and human roles are reversed and the allusion remains debated.

## Interpretive Significance

Sigmund Mowinckel proposed that ancient Israel celebrated YHWH's kingship at an annual autumn enthronement festival. Ps 47:5–8 (MT 47:6–9, using the Hebrew verse numbering) depicts God's ascent and reign, and Ps 93:1 uses a Hebrew phrase that can be understood as “YHWH reigns” or “YHWH has become king.” The latter translation can suggest an accession, while the former expresses continuing kingship. The psalms and Zech 14:16–17, which envisages the nations worshiping the King at Booths, have been read in support of such a festival. They establish the importance of divine kingship in Israel's worship, while the exact rites and their relationship to Babylon remain debated. [@keel-symbolism]

The so-called Marduk Ordeal, an Assyrian text that interprets actions and objects used in worship, was once taken as evidence that Marduk died and rose during the festival. Its wording and genre do not establish a resurrection ritual. The securely documented rites provide a sounder basis for comparisons with biblical images of procession and the inauguration of rule.
