## Overview

In the diplomatic language of the ancient Near East, love could describe the faithfulness owed to a king or ally. Letters from Late Bronze Age Canaan and Assyrian loyalty oaths from the first millennium BCE show that such love could be expected and expressed through action. This background helps explain why Deuteronomy commands Israel to love God and connects love with service and obedience. Biblical texts also speak of God's love for Israel in terms of choice, attachment, and family relationships. The political and familial uses together show the range of meanings carried by covenant language.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]], rulers use expressions of love when discussing their relations with other rulers. Such words could affirm cooperation and loyalty to the Egyptian king. The writer's actions, such as carrying out requests or supporting an ally, could show what the declaration meant. The political setting made love a matter of conduct as well as a description of a relationship [@moran-amarna].

The [[esarhaddon-succession-treaty|succession treaty of Esarhaddon]] binds its oath takers to love Ashurbanipal as they love themselves. Their obligation includes protecting the designated successor and opposing efforts to remove him. The surrounding terms define the loyalty expected of those who swear. This is one clear example of a king requiring love in the formal language of a treaty [@saa2, 39].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 6:5 commands love for YHWH with heart, life, and strength. Other passages describe such love through service and obedience (Deut 10:12; 11:1, 13, 22). Deut 13:3 (MT 13:4) treats an invitation to serve other gods as a test of love for YHWH. Together these passages present attachment to YHWH as something visible in choices and commitments.

Deut 7:7–9 also speaks of a love that YHWH shows toward Israel in choosing the people and keeping faith with them. Hos 11:1 gives that love a familial picture by calling Israel a child loved by God. The relation of the Hosea passage to covenant language is debated. The parent and child image can suggest loyalty and responsibility, and it also depicts an attachment suited to a family.

The description of Hiram's enduring love for David occurs in an account of friendly relations between royal houses (1 Kgs 5:1; MT 5:15). Jonathan's love for David and his covenant with him join personal commitment and political implications (1 Sam 18:1–3). The claim that Israel and Judah loved David in 1 Sam 18:16 has been interpreted as political allegiance. Others emphasize David's popularity in a context that describes his success as a leader.

## Interpretive Significance

William L. Moran argued that the [[covenant-and-treaty-form|treaty background]] explains why Deuteronomy can command love. Jacqueline Lapsley and Bill Arnold have emphasized reasons to take the emotional and relational aspects of the language seriously alongside conduct. Biblical love can be expressed as a pattern of action that grows out of attachment, identity, and obligation.

The corresponding Hebrew term for hate, *śānēʾ*, can sometimes describe repudiation or rejection. Mal 1:2–3 contrasts love for Jacob and hate for Esau, then points to the fate of Edom. The language expresses different relations to the groups represented by the two ancestors. The statement can be read as election and rejection without requiring every biblical use of love or hate to carry a political sense.
