## Overview

The Genesis Apocryphon is an Aramaic retelling of stories about the earliest generations in Genesis and about Abram. It survives in a single damaged scroll from Cave 1 at Qumran, catalogued as 1Q20. The author recounts familiar events from new perspectives and adds dreams, speeches, and details about motives. Several sections use the voices of the characters themselves. The surviving copy dates to around the turn of the era, and the work was probably composed earlier. It is important evidence for how Jewish writers interpreted and retold Genesis in the Second Temple period.

## Description and Provenance

The scroll was among the first [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] found at Qumran. A number of its twenty-two surviving columns are fragmentary and difficult to read. Darkening of the leather, broken surfaces, and loss of material all complicate the identification of individual letters. Photographs made under different lighting conditions and comparative study of letterforms have allowed editors to improve some readings. The main scroll is kept at the Shrine of the Book in Jerusalem.

The Israel Museum places the copy around 25 BCE–50 CE. The work may have been composed in the third or second century BCE, although the author's identity and the precise date remain uncertain. The manuscript's preservation also limits what can be said about the beginning, ending, and original length of the work.

## Contents

Lamech suspects that his wife Bitenosh has borne a child fathered by one of the Watchers. Bitenosh swears that he is the father. The episode turns the short notice of Noah's birth in Gen 5:28–29 into a domestic drama that assumes the heavenly beings' transgression in Gen 6:1–4. In more fragmentary sections Noah recounts events connected with the flood and the division of the earth among his sons, expanding on the geographic and genealogical world of Gen 10:1–32.

In the Abram section a dream anticipates the danger to him and Sarai in Egypt. An extended description praises Sarai's beauty, while Abram's prayer and laying on of hands heal Pharaoh after Sarai is returned (compare Gen 12:10–20). Abram walks the land promised to him, elaborating the command of Gen 13:14–17, and tells of the war of the kings and his encounter with Melchizedek (Gen 14:1–24). The last surviving section overlaps with the opening of Gen 15.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The text illustrates a way of interpreting inherited scripture by placing new material inside a continuous retelling. For example, Lamech's fear makes a connection between Noah's unusual birth and the story of the heavenly beings. The expansion of the episode in Egypt gives Abram a dream, Sarai a dramatic part, and Abram a role in reversing Pharaoh's illness. The healing by touch invites comparison with Luke 4:40 as another literary instance of a practice in a Jewish setting.

## Scholarly Issues

The birth of Noah has a parallel in [[first-enoch|1 Enoch]] 106–107. [[jubilees|Jubilees]] 13 also expands Abram's journey and sojourn in Egypt. Scholars debate whether the Genesis Apocryphon borrowed from either work or drew from collections of stories also available to their authors. As an extended witness to literary [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]], it can be compared with later Aramaic translations and expansions of scripture and provides evidence for a variety of the language used around the beginning of the Roman period.
