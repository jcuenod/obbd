## Overview

Tammuz is the biblical form of the name of Dumuzi, a Mesopotamian god associated with herding, the love of Inanna, and death in the underworld. Sumerian songs and narratives portray him as a lover, shepherd, and victim of underworld powers. Akkadian texts attest mourning for Tammuz, whose name also became associated with a summer month. Ezekiel sees women weeping for him at the Jerusalem temple. The scene is a rare explicit biblical reference to a named Mesopotamian deity and a particular practice of mourning.

## Sources and Iconography

The [Sumerian love songs](sumerian-love-songs.md) describe the courtship of Dumuzi and [Inanna](ishtar.md). Other compositions have darker plots. In *Inanna's Descent*, Dumuzi is surrendered as Inanna's replacement in the underworld and eventually shares the obligation with his sister Geshtinanna. *Dumuzi's Dream* depicts pursuit and capture in a different narrative setting. Akkadian literary and ritual texts attest lamentation for Tammuz. A letter from Nineveh concerning the month Duʾuzu and the ending of the Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar* show connections between the god's myth and observance. The timing and details of mourning can vary with the place and source [@cos1, 1.108, 1.169; @anet, 637–645; @cohen-cultic-calendars, 454–482].

Herding implements and plants contribute to Dumuzi's poetic imagery. In the absence of inscriptions or distinctive context, identifying individual male figures in seals as Dumuzi remains uncertain. The textual corpus gives firmer footing for his associations than most proposed images [@ddd, 829–834].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ezek 8:14 places women weeping for Tammuz at the entrance to the north gate of the temple complex. The practice belongs to a sequence of disturbing scenes Ezekiel is shown in a vision. The text names the god and identifies the act as weeping. It gives no date, words of lament, or information about the women's roles. Mesopotamian mourning traditions explain why weeping was an intelligible act of devotion to this god, but they cannot supply a full liturgy for the Jerusalem episode.

Several other passages have been compared with Tammuz rites. The quickly planted gardens of Isa 17:10–11 have been associated with plants used in Adonis rites. Neither the god nor the ritual is named. The designation “desire of women” in Dan 11:37 has been interpreted as a divine epithet among other proposals, and the lament associated with Hadad-rimmon in Zech 12:11 remains disputed. None offers evidence as direct as Ezekiel's reference.

## Scholarly Issues

Greek and Roman descriptions of mourning for Adonis, especially in connection with the eastern Mediterranean, have encouraged comparison with Tammuz. The beloved's death and ritual grief provide clear thematic points of contact. Historical continuity between individual rites requires further evidence, and the distinct myths and calendars should be retained. The language of dying and rising gods can obscure the difference between recurring release from the underworld, mourning, and a claim of permanent restoration.
