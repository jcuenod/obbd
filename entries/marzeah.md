## Overview

The *marzeaḥ* was an association known from West Semitic texts across several periods. Some sources portray its members meeting for food and wine under the patronage of a deity. Documents from Late Bronze Age Ugarit also show that such groups could manage property. Amos uses the term in a denunciation of privileged feasting, and Jeremiah uses it for a gathering in a context of mourning. The comparison helps readers understand both passages, while the question of whether the association had an inherently funerary purpose remains disputed.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The term has been related to words attested at Ebla and appears clearly in the later [[ugaritic-texts|texts from Ugarit]]. A Ugaritic narrative describes El drinking to excess at a gathering associated with the *marzeaḥ*. Legal records show groups that could hold property and had recognized heads or representatives. Taken together, these sources support an association with formal membership, shared resources, and occasions for drinking [@ktu3, 3.9] [@parker-unp, 193–196].

Later evidence includes Phoenician and Aramaic texts and examples from Nabataean and Palmyrene contexts. The institution could be associated with a deity and a particular place of meeting. The surviving records come from different cities and many centuries, so common vocabulary does not establish a uniform set of activities. An unprovenanced Moabite papyrus associated with the term has been questioned on grounds of authenticity [@kai].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Amos 6:4–7 describes people reclining on decorated beds, eating choice food, making music, and drinking wine. The passage announces an end to their *marzeaḥ*. The scene's wealth and social exclusivity fit evidence for organized feasting. The prophet's concern is the complacency of those who enjoy it while their society approaches disaster.

Jer 16:5–9 tells the prophet to abstain from entering a house of *marzeaḥ* in a passage about death, consolation, and the loss of communal celebration. In this context the term belongs near [[mourning-rites|mourning and consolation]]. The setting is important evidence that the association or meeting could have a funerary connection. It does not establish that every group using the name served a mortuary function.

## Interpretive Significance

Some scholars have linked the institution primarily with the dead or with the [[rephaim|Rephaim]]. John McLaughlin argues that feasting and religious association are better established than any universal funerary role. The broader evidence permits overlapping uses of food and wine at a feast, a religious gathering, and a commemoration. This helps explain why Amos and Jeremiah can give the same term quite different associations.

Amos 2:8 mentions wine obtained through fines drunk in the house of a god. The scene can be compared with feasting associated with a deity, though it leaves the organization unnamed. Ps 106:28 describes eating sacrifices of the dead at [[baal-peor|Baal-peor]] and was associated with the institution in later interpretation. Its direct connection with the *marzeaḥ* remains disputed.
