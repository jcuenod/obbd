## Overview

The Erastus inscription is a Latin text on paving stones at Corinth. It names Erastus and records that the paving was paid for privately in connection with his aedileship, a Roman municipal office. Romans also names an Erastus who held a city position and sent greetings to its recipients. Scholars debate whether the two references concern the same person. The identification depends especially on the inscription's date and how closely the Latin office matches the Greek title in Romans.

## Description and Provenance

The letters are cut into paving blocks near the theater of [[corinth|Corinth]]. Excavators identified and published the pieces as one inscription. The surviving arrangement of the pieces and the relationship of the inscribed blocks to the surrounding pavement are important to dating the monument. John Kent assigned it to the middle of the first century CE. Steven Friesen questions the archaeological basis for that date and argues for a later setting, probably in the second century CE.

## Contents

The inscription identifies Erastus and mentions the office of aedile. In a Roman colony an aedile was a local magistrate involved in aspects of public administration. The text records that Erastus provided the paving at his own expense in connection with the office. Such a notice made a contribution to the city part of the public record. The name alone gives little basis for connecting the donor to a literary reference.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Rom 16:23 sends greetings from Erastus, called the *oikonomos* of the city, which can be translated “treasurer” or “administrator.” The letter is often understood to have been written from Corinth. An identification with the inscription would give an example of a person associated with Paul's circle who had held a public office and could fund a civic work. The contribution of one person would still tell little by itself about the economic position of most people in the congregation. [@abd]

In 1 Cor 1:26 Paul asks readers to consider that few of them were powerful or of distinguished birth. This leaves room for individuals of different standing. Erastus also appears in Acts 19:22 and 2 Tim 4:20. It is uncertain whether either notice refers to the same person as Romans, and neither supplies the municipal title found in the inscription.

## Scholarly Issues

Gerd Theissen argued that the city official in Romans could have been an aedile. Justin Meggitt and Steven Friesen have questioned the equivalence of the Greek and Latin titles. The Greek expression might describe a different municipal post or a role that Paul describes without using its most precise title. Even if the two titles could refer to the same office, the dating of the inscription remains a separate issue. The identification is possible and debated. [@abd]
