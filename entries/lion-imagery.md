## Overview

Lions were a real danger to people and flocks in the ancient Levant and a familiar image in its art and literature. Sculptors set lions beside royal thrones and gates or showed kings defeating them. Biblical writers use the lion for God, rulers, tribes, and enemies, sometimes within the same book. The animal's strength can express danger or protection, depending on who faces it. These uses help explain why Judah can be likened to a young lion while Nineveh's fall can be described as the destruction of a lions' den.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Lions occur beside entrances and thrones in ancient Near Eastern art. They can guard a boundary, display royal authority, or identify a deity's power. A lion motif also appears on the [[seal-of-shema|seal associated with Shema]], illustrating the animal's place in royal material culture. The [[lion-hunt-of-ashurbanipal|Assyrian royal hunt reliefs]] represent another use of the animal. The controlled defeat of a dangerous beast displays the king's strength and can make a claim about his ability to maintain order [@anep, figs. 125, 184, 276, 626] [@keel-symbolism, 89–94, 281–283].

Brent Strawn's comparative study emphasizes the animal's flexibility as an image. A lion can represent the king as a formidable ruler or the threat a king defeats. Retaining both sides of the image makes it easier to understand how biblical authors apply it to different subjects.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 49:9 compares Judah to a lion's cub and then to a lion that has settled after taking prey. Num 23:24 and 24:9 use the lion for Israel's strength in Balaam's oracles. The roar or growl gives human kings a threatening voice in Prov 19:12 and 20:2. In 1 Kgs 10:19–20, lions flank Solomon's throne and its steps, evidence for the royal associations of the design within the narrative.

Divine power likewise has a lion's voice or actions. Amos 1:2 and 3:8 connect the divine voice with a roar. Hos 5:14 and 13:7–8 portray YHWH attacking his own people like a dangerous animal. Isa 31:4 likens him to a lion that refuses to be frightened from its prey by shepherds. The images vary according to the danger and the relationship of the speaker to the divine action.

Lions can also stand for nations or victims. Ezek 19:1–9 presents Judah's princes as lion cubs and uses the imagery of hunting and capture to represent their removal. Nah 2:11–13 (MT 2:12–14) presents Nineveh as a den once secure and full of prey, then threatens its destruction. Ps 22:13, 21 (MT 22:14, 22) uses a ravening lion and its mouth for the petitioner's danger. In Amos 3:12 and 1 Sam 17:34–37, the protection of flocks gives rise to a more immediate comparison with pastoral life. Exod 22:13 (MT 22:12) also assumes that predators could tear animals entrusted to someone's care.

## Interpretive Significance

The context determines whose strength the image represents and whom it threatens. Rev 5:5 applies the title Lion of the tribe of Judah to the victorious figure announced to John and draws on Judah's older imagery. Later use of the Lion of Judah as a royal emblem, including in Ethiopia, belongs to the reception of these traditions. It should be distinguished from the original setting of each biblical poem or vision.
