## Overview

Sumerian love songs include poems about the goddess Inanna and her lover Dumuzi and poems that address a reigning king in the language of desire. They describe courtship, adornment, lovemaking, and the pleasure or frustration of obtaining access to a beloved. Most surviving copies date to the Old Babylonian period, and some poems name rulers of the preceding Third Dynasty of Ur. Comparisons with the Song of Songs have focused on the voices of lovers, the mother's house, and the use of gardens and agricultural abundance as erotic imagery.

## Manuscripts and Date

The corpus survives on cuneiform tablets of uneven preservation. The individual songs have modern titles or letter designations, and their surviving copies may be considerably later than the events or rulers they evoke. A poem for Shu-Sin, a late third-millennium king of Ur, addresses the king as a desirable bridegroom. The relationship between the named king and the mythic lover depends on the particular song. Reading across the corpus brings out a common repertoire of images and settings while retaining distinct voices and scenes [@cos1, 1.169, 1.173; @anet, 637–645].

## Contents

Some songs follow Inanna and Dumuzi from negotiation through union. Family involvement can be decisive. A lover may speak of going to her mother's house or seek her mother's consent. Preparations include washing, adorning the body, and making a bed. Other lyrics use milk, fruit, gardens, and pastures to give desire a vocabulary of nourishment, scent, and plenty. Royal songs can bring the king and his well-being into the same register. The divine lovers are also known from myths and laments about Dumuzi's death. These works have separate plots and settings, even when they share characters with the love songs.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The Song of Songs gives its female lover initiative in seeking, inviting, and praising her beloved (Song 1:2–4). She imagines bringing him to her mother's house (Song 3:4; 8:1–2), a place with domestic and emotional resonance in both traditions. In Song 4:12–5:1 a garden of water, fragrance, and fruit expresses intimacy in a sustained exchange between the lovers. Royal and bridal images in Song 3:6–11 allow comparison with the prestige and ceremony associated with some Sumerian songs. Egyptian love poetry supplies additional comparanda for the voices and imagery, broadening the cultural setting for such language [@ael, 2:181–194].

The Hebrew Bible names Dumuzi in his later form, [Tammuz](tammuz.md), when Ezekiel sees women weeping for him at the Jerusalem temple (Ezek 8:14). That scene attests acquaintance with a lamenting practice. The Song contains no explicit reference to Dumuzi or to [Inanna](ishtar.md).

## Scholarly Issues

Some lyrics are often read in connection with a sacred marriage between a king and a goddess, enacted or represented to secure divine favor and abundance. The songs alone seldom establish the setting, personnel, or form of an actual performance. Similar imagery in the Song of Songs identifies literary possibilities ranging from weddings to the poetry of private desire. The imagery gives little basis for reconstructing a particular cult behind the biblical collection.
