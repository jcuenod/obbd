## Overview

Ancient writers used stone, clay, wood, pottery, metal, papyrus, and prepared animal skin. The intended use shaped the choice of a medium and tools. An inscription could be cut in stone, a cuneiform sign pressed into damp clay, and a letter written in ink on a potsherd or scroll. Different materials survive in different conditions, so excavated texts preserve an uneven sample of ancient writing. Biblical passages use the physical features of tablets, scrolls, and tools to describe acts of recording, public display, and prophetic sign-making.

## Archaeological Evidence

Clay tablets written with a stylus were common in Mesopotamia and appear at Late Bronze Age sites in the Levant, including [Ugarit](ugarit.md). The [Amarna Letters](amarna-letters.md) attest the use of cuneiform tablets for international correspondence. Assyrian writing boards of ivory and wood from Nimrud had recessed surfaces for wax and could be joined by hinges. These boards show that a writer could combine several leaves while keeping a surface that could be smoothed and reused. Such materials are less likely than stone or pottery to survive ordinary burial conditions [@moran-amarna; @abd].

In the Iron Age Levant, [ostraca](ostraca.md) carried letters, lists, and accounts in ink. Stone served for display inscriptions and commemorations, while metal could carry short inscriptions such as the silver [Ketef Hinnom amulets](ketef-hinnom-amulets.md). The impressions of papyrus fibers on the backs of some [bullae](seals-and-bullae.md) attest papyrus use even where the documents decayed. The palimpsest called Mur 17, reported from Wadi Murabbaʿat, preserves two layers of early Hebrew writing. Other proposed Iron Age Hebrew papyri lack secure excavated provenance and require individual assessment [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions; @ahituv-echoes; @avigad-sass-wsss].

Dry settings have preserved the Persian-period [Elephantine papyri](elephantine-papyri.md) and the much later [Dead Sea Scrolls](dead-sea-scrolls.md). Most surviving Judean Desert manuscripts from Qumran were written on prepared animal skin, and some were written on papyrus. The Copper Scroll is a rare example of an extended text incised on metal. These materials represent distinct archives and periods [@tad; @abd].

## In the Bible

Exod 24:12 calls for tablets of stone, and Exod 32:15–16 describes them as written on both sides. Isa 8:1 mentions a large writing surface, and Isa 30:8 pairs a tablet and a written record for later reference. Their precise materials are unspecified. Jer 17:1 speaks of an iron implement with a hard point to express the permanence of wrongdoing. Job 19:23–24 imagines his words written down and cut into rock with iron and lead.

In Jeremiah 36, Baruch writes a scroll in ink at Jeremiah's dictation and a royal official reads it before the king, who cuts and burns portions as they are read (Jer 36:2, 18, 23). The passage depicts ink, scroll, and scribal knife at work in a particular setting. Ezek 9:2 depicts a figure with a scribe's writing case, and Ezek 2:9–10 portrays a scroll written on both sides. Ezek 37:16–20 uses two inscribed pieces of wood that become one. Some compare the action with hinged writing boards, but the text does not specify hinges or wax.

The flying scroll in Zech 5:1–2 uses the shape of a familiar object at a visionary scale. Ps 40:7 refers to a book scroll without specifying material. The request for books and parchments in 2 Tim 4:13 reflects the availability of different writing objects, though the text leaves their contents and format open.
