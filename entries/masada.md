## Overview

Masada is a fortress on an isolated plateau in the Judean Desert west of the Dead Sea. In the late first century BCE, Herod the Great equipped it as a royal retreat and stronghold. Jewish rebels occupied it during the first revolt against Rome, and Roman forces captured it in 73 or 74 CE. The surviving palaces and siege works document two very different uses of the site. Fragments of biblical books and other Jewish writings also make Masada important for the history of the texts that circulated in Judea around the time of the revolt.

## Location and Identification

Steep cliffs rise from the desert to a broad, flat summit. The plateau's position and the Roman camps and ramp around it fit the description in Book 7 of *The Jewish War* by [[josephus|Josephus]], the first-century Jewish historian. He credits the first fortification to a high priest named Jonathan. This is the basis for assigning the first defenses to the Hasmoneans, the Jewish dynasty that preceded Herod, but the account does not securely identify which Jonathan he meant. [@neaehl]

## Archaeological History

[[herod-the-great|Herod]] built the Northern Palace on terraces descending from the summit and the Western Palace on the plateau. The complex also included storehouses, a bathhouse, and cisterns, or reservoirs, that held runoff. These installations supported a substantial residence in a place with no dependable water supply on the summit. Yigael Yadin led excavations in 1963–1965, and Ehud Netzer published a detailed study of the architecture. [@neaehl]

During the revolt, the occupants adapted existing buildings to new needs. One served as a synagogue, or Jewish assembly building, and is among the earliest excavated [[synagogues]] in the land. At the end of the revolt, troops under Flavius Silva built camps, a wall encircling the plateau to block escape, and an assault ramp on the western side. Together they form one of the most complete surviving Roman siege systems. The [[judaea-capta-coins|Judaea Capta coins]] provide a separate example of how Rome commemorated victory in the wider war. [@neaehl]

Josephus says that most of the occupants died by killing one another and themselves rather than surrendering. Yadin interpreted a group of ostraca, or inscribed potsherds, bearing personal names as the lots used to choose the final killers. The names are visible on the sherds, but their purpose is disputed, and the siege works cannot establish how the occupants died. The sociologist Nachman Ben-Yehuda studied how the story of the last stand became a symbol of national resolve in modern Israel. That modern use is distinct from the question of what happened when the Romans entered.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The name Masada is related to the Hebrew *mĕṣûdâ* ("stronghold"). In 1 Sam 24:22 (MT 24:23, the numbering of the traditional Hebrew text), David and his men go up to the stronghold after Saul leaves. The reference illustrates the Hebrew word, not the site's location in David's story. The notice does not name Masada.

Manuscripts from the site include portions of Leviticus, Deuteronomy, Ezekiel, and Psalms. The Ezekiel fragments preserve parts of Ezek 35:11–38:14, including the vision of the dry bones. Portions of Ps 81–85 are close to the later Masoretic text, the Hebrew form transmitted by medieval scribes. Masada also yielded part of Ben Sira in Hebrew (Sir 39:27–44:17), an early witness to the language in which the work was composed. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the exact surviving verse limits of the Ezekiel, Psalms, and Ben Sira fragments against the excavation edition -->

Fragments of the *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice*, a work describing heavenly worship, were found at Masada and among the [[dead-sea-scrolls|manuscripts]] from caves near [[qumran|Qumran]]. The finds show that the work was present at both places, but not how the Masada copy got there. Its owners and the route it took remain debated. The presence of biblical and other writings at Masada does not by itself determine how the occupants used them.
