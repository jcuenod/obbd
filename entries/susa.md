## Overview

Susa, the biblical Shushan, is in the lowlands of southwestern Iran. For much of its early history it was a major political and religious center of Elam. Later it became a royal residence of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, and Darius I built a palace there with an imposing audience hall. The site's finds include Elamite texts, Mesopotamian monuments carried to Susa, and Achaemenid inscriptions that describe imperial building projects. Nehemiah hears news from Jerusalem at the Susa citadel, Esther unfolds chiefly there, and Daniel situates a vision in Susa.

## Location and Identification

The ancient mounds adjoin modern Shush in Khuzestan, east of the Tigris plain and near routes into the Iranian uplands. The major excavated areas are conventionally called the Acropolis, the Apadana, the Royal City, and the Artisans' Town. Susa's location allowed exchange with Mesopotamia and with the peoples farther east. Names in inscriptions and the long local survival of the name support the identification with biblical Shushan. The modern shrine of Daniel reflects a later tradition associating the figure with the city [@abd, 6:242–245].

## Archaeological History

Susa has remains from the late fifth millennium BCE onward. Its material culture sometimes resembles that of neighboring Mesopotamia, and at other times shows distinct Elamite developments. Major French excavations began in the nineteenth century. The Acropolis produced the victory stele of Naram-Sin, on which Shutruk-Nahhunte records its transfer to Susa from Sippar. The Laws of Hammurabi stele was also found at Susa. Its presence fits the pattern of Elamite removal of Babylonian monuments, though its carrier and original installation are less directly attested [@roth-law, 71–73].

Darius I established a palace complex with residential courts and a columned audience hall, or *apadana*. His foundation inscription DSf describes building materials, routes of supply, and craftspeople from across the empire. Its account brings the distant provinces into the work at the king's residence. Excavated remains, including glazed-brick reliefs and great stone column elements, demonstrate some of the techniques and materials used in the complex. The buildings and their successive repairs document a residence with a history extending beyond its founder [@anet, 316–317; @anet, 316–317].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Neh 1:1 places Nehemiah in the citadel of Susa when he receives news of Jerusalem. The setting helps explain how a servant of the king could receive information through travelers and later use his access to seek an imperial commission. Esther also places its king and court at the citadel, describing banquets, costly furnishings, women's quarters, and the dispatch of decrees (Esth 1:2–6; 2:3–8; 3:15). The excavated complex gives substance to the wealth and differentiated spaces of an imperial residence. Individual architectural features in the story cannot all be matched to excavated rooms or objects.

Ezra 4:9 includes people of Susa in the list of parties to a letter sent to the king. Dan 8:2 places Daniel at Susa in Elam, by the Ulai, in a vision narrated in the reign of Belshazzar. The passage describes visionary geography. The Achaemenid prominence of Susa provides historical context for the reader's association of the city with imperial power.
