## Overview

Sakkut and Kaiwan are scholarly reconstructions of two names or expressions in Amos 5:26, a verse connecting cult images with exile. Kaiwan is commonly linked with an Akkadian name for Saturn. Sakkut has been compared with the Mesopotamian divine name Saĝkud, although the identification is less secure. Ancient readers understood both terms in different ways. The Greek translation quoted in Acts and the interpretation in the Damascus Document show how the same difficult Hebrew passage could be put to new uses.

## Sources and Iconography

Akkadian *kayamānu*, “the steady one,” is attested as a name for Saturn. It gives a plausible counterpart to the consonants of the second name, which Amos places in a phrase about a star associated with a god. Mesopotamian lexical texts attest a divine name written SAG.KUD, with syllabic forms like *Sakkud*. Some traditions associate Saĝkud with Ninurta. The comparison establishes that a similar name was used for a Mesopotamian deity, while the identification of the Hebrew expression and its association with Saturn require further argument. No cult image can be securely assigned to either figure from the wording of Amos. [@ddd, 722–723] [@abd, 5:903–905]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Amos 5:25–27 asks about sacrifice in the wilderness, refers to images the people made, and announces exile beyond Damascus. The Masoretic vocalizations *sikkût* and *kiyyûn* are often explained as using the vowel pattern of *šiqqûṣ*, “abomination,” to discredit the names. The consonants of the first term also admit a reading associated with a shelter or booth, reflected in some translations. The question of whether the people carried the images in the past or are to carry them in the future affects how the verse relates to the wilderness and exile. [@ddd, 722–723] [@abd, 5:903–905]

The [[septuagint|Greek translation]] reads a tent of Moloch and the star of a deity named Raiphan in common Greek editions. Acts 7:42–43 uses that version in Stephen's account of worship of the heavenly host. It has a form rendered Rephan in WEB and says that the people will be taken beyond Babylon. The [[dead-sea-scrolls|Damascus Document]] (CD VII 14–21) gives a different interpretation involving the king's booth and prophetic books, and links the star with an interpreter of the law. [@abd, 5:903–905] [@ddd, 700, 722–723]

## Scholarly Issues

On one reading the names preserve a reference to Assyrian-period astral worship and could have entered the passage after the fall of Samaria. Others read the verse as part of Amos's indictment in the eighth century BCE. Assyrian influence predates 722 BCE. The cults listed in 2 Kgs 17:30 illustrate the book of Kings' interest in worship introduced by settlers and give no direct attestation of these two names. [@ddd, 722–723] [@abd, 5:903–905]

The difference between Kaiwan and Greek Raiphan may reflect a mistaken reading or transmission of the first consonant. The stages of that change are uncertain. The interpretation of Sakkut as a divine name likewise remains a distinct question from identifying Kaiwan with Saturn. [@ddd, 700, 722–723]
