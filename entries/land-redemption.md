## Overview

Land tenure concerns who may hold, inherit, and transfer land. In biblical texts concerning ancient Israel and Judah, especially those set in the first millennium BCE, ancestral land is a possession that a household is expected to pass down rather than lose permanently. Redemption is the recovery of property through payment or, in some accounts, the exercise of a relative's prior right to buy. These arrangements help explain why a family field could carry obligations beyond its sale price, and why biblical law and prophecy treat dispossession as a public concern.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Some Old Babylonian sale documents have been read as preserving a family's right to recover property after a sale. <!-- VERIFY: identify an Old Babylonian sale document that expressly reserves this right to a relative rather than to the seller. --> A more specific comparison is section 39 of the [[laws-of-eshnunna|Laws of Eshnunna]], which grants an impoverished seller a right to recover a house when the buyer resells it. That rule concerns the original seller and an eventual resale, not a relative's general duty to intervene. The [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] (sections 36–41) distinguish between certain holdings tied to service obligations and property their holders can transfer. That distinction protects the provision for service rather than establishing a general ban on permanent sales. [@roth-law]

Royal grants from Late Bronze Age [[ugarit|Ugarit]] show that a king could authorize transfers of land. Babylonian [[kudurru|inscribed land-grant monuments]] likewise record particular grants or property settlements. These records show royal intervention in particular holdings, not royal supervision of every private transaction. They provide comparisons for limits on private control of land without supplying an equivalent to Israel's rules for redemption. [@cos3] [@cos2]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Lev 25:23–28 grounds the ban on permanent sale in YHWH's ownership of the land. If poverty forces a sale, the nearest relative is to buy back what was sold; a seller who later acquires sufficient means may also recover it. If neither happens, the holding returns in the Jubilee, the law's appointed year of restoration, discussed alongside [[debt-release|debt release]]. Lev 25:29–34 treats unwalled village houses like fields, but gives most houses in walled cities only a year for redemption and no Jubilee return. Houses belonging to Levites, members of the tribe of Levi, have continuing redemption rights in their cities, and the associated pastureland may not be sold.

In Num 27:1–11, Zelophehad's daughters receive their father's holding because he has no son, and the ruling sets out the order of [[inheritance-and-birthright|inheritance]] in comparable cases. Num 36:5–9 adds a marriage restriction intended to keep inherited holdings from passing between tribes. Ruth 4:1–12 presents a different question: at the town gate Boaz takes up a nearer relative's claim to Elimelech's property and marries Ruth to preserve the dead man's name on his inheritance. The narrative combines land redemption with an arrangement resembling [[levirate-marriage|levirate marriage]], in which a man marries his deceased brother's widow to continue the family line (Deut 25:5–6). Scholars dispute whether Ruth depicts an established legal combination or brings distinct customs together for the story. [@abd]

Jer 32:6–15 gives a detailed account of a family purchase. Hanamel offers Jeremiah the field at Anathoth on the basis of his right of redemption, and Jeremiah weighs out silver, seals the [[sealed-deeds|deed of purchase]], and calls witnesses. He orders both the sealed and open copies stored in an earthen jar, and the purchase signifies that land transactions will resume. Excavated [[seals-and-bullae|clay seal impressions]], called bullae, offer evidence for the practice of sealing documents in Jerusalem, not for the survival of Jeremiah's deed. [@neaehl] [@avigad-sass-wsss]

## Interpretive Significance

In 1 Kgs 21:1–19, Naboth refuses both purchase and exchange because the vineyard is ancestral property, and Elijah condemns Ahab's taking possession after Naboth's death. Mic 2:1–2 and Isa 5:8 denounce seizure and accumulation, while Ezek 46:16–18 bars the ruler from dispossessing households to provide for his sons. These passages show that loss of land was a serious concern, but they do not establish the prevalence of *latifundia*, or large private estates, in eighth-century BCE Israel and Judah. Archaeological evidence of settlement and production does not by itself establish who owned the land or how concentrated ownership was.

Some scholars see the restriction on permanent sales as preserving an early tribal custom; others understand the system in Leviticus as a later formulation of an ideal. The positions need not exclude each other, since an old expectation about family holdings could have been formalized later. A law prescribing return in the Jubilee does not by itself show how often land was actually returned. [@abd] [@king-stager-life]
