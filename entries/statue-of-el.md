## Overview

A small bronze figure from Late Bronze Age Ugarit shows a seated god. It was covered with gold foil and is commonly identified as El, the senior deity of Ugaritic literature. The figure is in the National Museum of Damascus. Its appearance gives an example of how a Levantine artist could portray divine authority through a seated posture and raised hand. These images provide context for biblical visions of an enthroned deity, even though the figure bears no inscription identifying the god.

## Description and Provenance

The object comes from Ras Shamra, ancient [[ugarit|Ugarit]], and is registered in Damascus as S 3573. Its excavation number is RS 23.394. The catalog identifies it as a gold-covered bronze figure and dates it to the Late Bronze Age. It is distinct from carved stone reliefs sometimes described as portraying the same divine figure. Comparison with another piece requires attention to its material, posture, and catalog record.

## Contents

The figure wears a beard and raises a hand, in a pose usually understood as greeting or blessing. Its seated position is associated with authority in the visual culture of the region. The description “El” draws on the form of the figure and on the wider role of [[el|El]] in local stories. Other gods can be represented with similar features. Identification by visual type therefore depends on several signals together [@anep] [@keel-symbolism].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 1 Kgs 22:19 Micaiah sees YHWH seated with the heavenly host nearby. Isa 6:1 likewise presents an exalted divine ruler on a throne, and Ps 82:1 portrays God presiding in the [[divine-council|divine assembly]]. Images of an enthroned deity help explain how a royal posture could communicate authority. The biblical scenes also have elements, such as the attendants and the acts of judgment or commissioning, that a single figure does not depict.

In Dan 7:9, age and a throne mark the Ancient of Days. A comparison with the senior role of El is possible, although the iconography gives no evidence of a direct relationship. Job 36:26 associates God with a span of years beyond human reckoning. The Ugaritic epithet *ab šnm*, often translated “father of years,” may be relevant, but its precise sense is debated [@ktu3] [@parker-unp].

## Scholarly Issues

The identification of the figure and the meaning of particular Ugaritic epithets are distinct questions. A beard and a seated posture are consistent with El's role. The artifact gives firmer evidence for an artistic type than for which god the artist represented. The comparisons with biblical imagery likewise concern portrayals of authority and age. They leave the historical relationship among the texts and images open.
