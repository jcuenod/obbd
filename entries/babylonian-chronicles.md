## Overview

The Babylonian chronicles are historical records written on clay tablets in first-millennium BCE Babylonia. Many arrange events by a king's year of reign, reporting accessions, wars, treaties, and interruptions to religious festivals. The surviving tablets cover parts of the period from the eighth century BCE into the age of the Seleucid kings. Several report events important to biblical history, including Babylon's victory at Carchemish and the first capture of Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar II. The chronicles make it possible to compare dates and perspectives across sources that often differ in purpose and detail.

## Manuscripts and Date

The chronicles survive on separate, sometimes damaged tablets. They do not all belong to one original series, and their accounts may survive in copies made substantially later than the events described. Their Akkadian text is written in cuneiform, a system of wedge-shaped signs. The two main modern editions use different numbering systems. Grayson's Chronicle 1, beginning with Nabonassar, corresponds in its principal text to Glassner 16. The tablets for the fall of Nineveh, early years of Nebuchadnezzar, and reign of Nabonidus are Grayson 3, 5, and 7, and Glassner 22, 24, and 26 [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles].

## Contents

The entry for the year typically names the reigning king and reports events concisely. Dates within a year may be given by month or by day. Texts can devote space to whether cult observances took place and to the whereabouts of a king, as in the [[nabonidus-chronicle|Nabonidus Chronicle]]. Others emphasize marches and battles, as in the [[fall-of-nineveh-chronicle|Fall of Nineveh Chronicle]], which reports the cooperation of the Babylonians and Medes in the capture of Nineveh in 612 BCE.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

One chronicle says that the Assyrian king Shalmaneser ravaged a place called Šamaraʾin, widely identified with Samaria. This gives a Babylonian witness to Shalmaneser V's role in the events surrounding the city's fall (2 Kgs 17:3–6), although Sargon II later claimed credit for conquering Samaria. The same chronicle reports that a son of Sennacherib killed him during a revolt. This agrees with Kings on the involvement of a son but differs in detail from its account of two sons taking part (2 Kgs 19:37) [@abc].

In 609 BCE, the chronicle of Nineveh's fall reports that an Egyptian army joined the surviving Assyrian forces in an attempt to retake Harran. In 2 Kgs 23:29, Neco “went up to the king of Assyria” (ESV). Some translations read the Hebrew as describing a march against Assyria, but the chronicle supports understanding the campaign as an effort to aid the Assyrians. It does not describe Josiah's death at Megiddo.

The [[nebuchadnezzar-chronicle|chronicle of Nebuchadnezzar's early years]] reports the Babylonian victory over Egypt at Carchemish, also remembered in Jer 46:2. It dates the capture of the city of Judah to March 597 BCE and says that the king of Babylon replaced its ruler, corresponding to the deportation of Jehoiachin and the installation of Zedekiah in 2 Kgs 24:10–17. The Nabonidus Chronicle records the Persian occupation of Babylon in 539 BCE under Cyrus. It provides a historical point of comparison for Dan 5:30–31 (MT 5:30–6:1), which describes a transfer of power to Darius the Mede.

## Scholarly Issues

Chronicles record Babylonian setbacks as well as victories, making them an important complement to royal inscriptions. They are selective and occasionally damaged, and their details need to be weighed alongside other sources. The tablet covering Nebuchadnezzar's early years ends well before his final siege of Jerusalem. The next surviving chronicle for the dynasty concerns Neriglissar decades later, so the collection supplies no record of Jerusalem's destruction in 587 or 586 BCE.
