## Overview

Scribes prepared records, letters and literary texts across the ancient Near East. In Israel and Judah, people called scribes appear as royal officials and as specialists in texts and instruction. Archaeological finds document writing exercises and conventions that required training. Egyptian and Mesopotamian evidence shows how such skills could be taught, while the institutional arrangements in Israel and Judah are less visible. Biblical portrayals of reading, dictation and copying help explain the kinds of work through which the biblical books themselves could be handed down.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian school tablets preserve exercises in signs, word lists, accounts, model contracts and compositions. Different settings trained scribes to handle practical records or more specialized learning. Egyptian evidence includes model letters and copies of instructions on papyri and ostraca. The [[satire-of-the-trades|*Satire of the Trades*]] depicts a father taking his son to learn to write and urging him to value the career. Its many copies show the work's use in scribal transmission. [@cos1, 1.48, 1.184–1.186] [@anet, 432–434, 475–479]

In the southern Levant, regular letter forms in Old Hebrew inscriptions and repeated use of conventions such as hieratic numerals show training in a shared repertoire. Alphabet sequences and exercises from [[izbet-sartah-abecedary|ʿIzbet Ṣarṭah]], Tel Zayit and [[kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet ʿAjrud]] illustrate practice in different settings and periods. The surviving objects rarely identify teachers or institutions. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 251–260] [@ahituv-echoes, 17–18, 249–256]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Court lists name Seraiah and Sheva in 2 Sam 8:17 and 20:25 and Elihoreph and Ahijah in 1 Kgs 4:3. In 2 Kgs 22:3–13, Shaphan brings a book from the temple and reads it to the king. Jer 36:4, 10–12, 26, 32 depicts Baruch writing by dictation, reading in a public setting and preparing a new scroll after the first is destroyed. The same story mentions a secretary's chamber. Ezek 9:2–3 depicts a figure carrying a scribe's case, with a term often connected to Egyptian writing equipment. [@abd, 5:1012–1017]

Prov 25:1 credits associates of Hezekiah with transmitting sayings. Jer 8:8 portrays scribal handling of instruction as open to abuse. Ezra 7:6, 11 presents Ezra as a skilled scribe of the law. Sir 38:24–39:11 contrasts manual occupations with the leisure, learning and public service of a scribe, recalling themes in the Egyptian instruction. [@abd, 5:1012–1017] [@ael, 1:184–192]

## Interpretive Significance

Standard writing conventions are evidence for organized transmission of skills. They allow several possible settings, including training in courts, temples or households. Scholars differ on how far to infer schools resembling those attested elsewhere. The repetitive sounds of Isa 28:9–13 have sometimes been interpreted as a parody of elementary instruction. The interpretation remains disputed. Biblical portrayals and inscriptions together attest copying, dictation and public reading. These activities help explain how texts could be collected, revised and preserved, although the evidence rarely identifies the scribes responsible for an individual biblical book. [@abd, 5:1012–1017] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]
