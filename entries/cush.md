## Overview

Cush usually refers in the Hebrew Bible to Kush, an ancient kingdom in the Nile Valley south of Egypt, centered in what is now Sudan. In the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, kings from Kush ruled Egypt as its Twenty-fifth Dynasty and confronted Assyrian expansion. Assyrian and Egyptian sources help place several biblical notices about Cush, including the revolt at Ashdod and the report that Taharqa was coming against Sennacherib. The name “Ethiopia” in some translations reflects ancient usage and generally points to the Nile region of Kush in these passages.

## Sources

Kushite rulers erected temples and inscribed monuments in Nubia and Egypt. Their texts describe building and royal successes in Egyptian forms while also showing close attachments to Nubian sanctuaries. Assyrian royal inscriptions record diplomacy and military encounters with rulers of Egypt and Kush. The two bodies of evidence bear on different parts of the kingdom's history and give different accounts of conflict. [@anet, 290–294] [@rinap3-1, no. 22 ii 37–iii 49] [@rinap4, no. 98]

## History

A royal house centered near Napata expanded north into Egypt in the eighth century BCE. Piye's victory inscription describes his defeat of Egyptian opponents and their submission. Later rulers extended Kushite authority over Egypt, and the dynasty held power during periods of conflict with Assyria. [[taharqa|Taharqa]] lost Memphis to Esarhaddon in 671 BCE, and Kushite rule in Egypt ended in the seventh century while the kingdom continued farther south. [@anet, 289–297] [@rinap4, no. 98]

The succession of Shabaka and Shebitku has been reconsidered. Sargon II's inscription at Tang-i Var names Shebitku as the Kushite ruler who extradited Yamani, a leader of the revolt at [[ashdod|Ashdod]]. This anchors Shebitku earlier than some older chronologies allowed. Frédéric Payraudeau and others have argued from Egyptian and Kushite evidence that Shebitku preceded Shabaka. The sequence affects who was ruling in 701 BCE. Taharqa's accession in Egypt is generally dated later, around 690 BCE. [@cos2, 2.118J]

## In the Bible

Isa 20:1–6 places a prophecy about Egypt and Cush at the time of Sargon's capture of Ashdod. It presents the downfall of the two powers as a warning to those who looked to them for protection from Assyria. Isa 18:1–7 associates the land beyond the rivers of Cush with envoys who travel by water. Kushite diplomacy in the era of Hezekiah provides a plausible setting, but the passage gives too little information to assign it an exact date.

In 2 Kgs 19:9 the Assyrian king hears that Tirhakah, or Taharqa, king of Cush, has come out to fight. Assyrian accounts of [[sennacherib-campaign-701|the campaign of 701 BCE]] report fighting Egyptian and Kushite forces. They do not name Taharqa as their commander. Some historians take the biblical royal title as retrospective if Taharqa participated before his accession. Others question whether the biblical story combines traditions from different times. [@rinap3-1, no. 22 ii 37–iii 49]

The name also appears in the [[table-of-nations|genealogy of nations]] (Gen 10:6–8), where its associations include peoples to the east and the figure of Nimrod. These links have led to suggestions that some occurrences involve Arabia or a Mesopotamian name. In the New Testament, Acts 8:27 places a court official under a ruler called Candace, a title associated with royal women in the kingdom centered on Meroë, which succeeded Napata as Kush's leading center. The biblical name thus extends across a long history and should be interpreted in its immediate setting. [@abd, 4:109–111]
