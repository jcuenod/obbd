## Overview

Tell Tayinat is a large mound in the Amuq plain of southern Türkiye. It is identified with Kunulua, capital of the Iron Age kingdom of Patina, and with the Assyrian provincial center established after conquest in 738 BCE. Excavations have exposed palaces and temples, including a sanctuary that contained a copy of Esarhaddon’s succession treaty. The finds illuminate biblical descriptions of temple architecture and the circulation of imperial oaths. They also provide context for the northern city named in Isaiah and Amos.

## Location and Identification

The mound lies near the Orontes River in the plain east of Antakya. Its location and written finds support identification with Kunulua, known as Kullania or Kunalia in Assyrian contexts. The kingdom surrounding it was called Patina or Unqi. Isa 10:9 and Amos 6:2 name cities conventionally identified with this center. Calneh in Gen 10:10 occurs in Shinar and presents a separate textual and geographical problem.

## Archaeological History

The University of Chicago’s Syrian-Hittite Expedition excavated in the 1930s, exposing palaces and the temple called Building II. Later work by the Tayinat Archaeological Project has documented additional phases and buildings. Building II has a long, divided plan and stood near a palace complex. Building XVI is another tripartite temple, associated with the period of Assyrian administration. Their plans show how entrances and interior spaces could be organized at different scales in the region.

[[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] conquered the city in 738 BCE. A tablet from the inner room of Building XVI is a copy of the [[esarhaddon-succession-treaty|Esarhaddon succession treaty]] of 672 BCE. It names the Assyrian governor of Kunalia in the oath. Its find context suggests that it was kept or displayed in the sanctuary. The recipient was a provincial official, so the find cannot by itself establish that an identical text reached every vassal kingdom.

Aegean-style pottery in earlier levels and inscriptions associated with a kingdom called Palistin or Walistin have prompted comparisons with the Philistines. The material and names are relevant to movements and changing identities in the early Iron Age. Their relationship remains disputed, and a shared style or a similar name does not establish a single political community.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 10:9 invokes Calno in a speech boasting of Assyrian conquests. Amos 6:2 names Calneh with other prominent places in a warning directed to people confident in their own security. The Assyrian conquest places the city within the historical setting of such rhetoric. First Kings 6:2–3 and 7:1–12 describe temple and palace structures in Jerusalem. The Tayinat buildings offer material comparisons for layouts and the proximity of royal and cultic architecture, without establishing a direct architectural model.

Deut 31:24–26 situates a written document beside the ark. Deut 13 and Deut 28:23 have also been compared with treaty requirements and curses. The tablet’s temple context shows that a written imperial oath could be kept in a sanctuary. Its wording gives comparanda for the biblical texts, but the excavation cannot identify the precise document or line of transmission behind Deuteronomy.
