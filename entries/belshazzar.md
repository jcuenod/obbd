## Overview

Belshazzar was the eldest son of Nabonidus, the last king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. He exercised authority in Babylonia during his father's extended stay in Arabia in the sixth century BCE. Cuneiform records identify him as the king's son and describe a delegation of power. The book of Daniel presents him as a king whose feast ends with a message announcing the loss of his kingdom. The Babylonian sources establish his historical identity and help explain his prominence, while the biblical story gives the fall of his rule its own setting and meaning.

## Extrabiblical Sources

One of the [[nabonidus-cylinders|cylinders of Nabonidus]] identifies Belshazzar by name as the king's firstborn son and asks the moon god Sîn to protect him. The [[nabonidus-chronicle|Nabonidus Chronicle]] reports that the crown prince remained in Babylonia with officials and troops while [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] stayed at Tayma. The chronicle also says that the Babylonian New Year festival was suspended in some of those years. Regular temple offerings continued, but the festival required the presence of the king. [@anet, 313] [@abc, 106–107]

The [[verse-account-of-nabonidus|Verse Account of Nabonidus]], a hostile account written after the Persian conquest, describes Nabonidus placing the army under his eldest son and entrusting kingship to him. This is evidence that leading functions of the crown were delegated. The Babylonian evidence consistently assigns the formal royal title and the regnal dates used in administrative records to Nabonidus. Belshazzar exercised considerable power as the king's son. [@anet, 562–563] [@glassner-chronicles]

## Biblical Portrayal

Dan 5:1–30 portrays Belshazzar giving a feast and bringing out vessels taken from the Jerusalem temple. A hand writes on the wall, and Daniel interprets the writing as a divine judgment on the ruler and an announcement that his kingdom will pass to the Medes and Persians. Belshazzar is killed that night in the story. Daniel's visions in Dan 7:1 and 8:1 are also dated by numbered years of Belshazzar's reign.

Dan 5 repeatedly calls Nebuchadnezzar Belshazzar's father. The royal inscriptions identify Nabonidus as his father. This kinship language can have broader literary or dynastic force, but no established line of descent from Nebuchadnezzar accounts for the usage here. The narrative brings the two rulers together to contrast Belshazzar's actions with the experience of his predecessor. Bar 1:11–12 also presents Belshazzar as Nebuchadnezzar's son. [@abd, 2:29–30, 2:38–39]

## Historical Assessment

The cuneiform evidence established Belshazzar's identity after earlier writers had questioned whether he was historical. It also clarified how a ruler drawn from Babylonian historical memory could be depicted as a king. The title and year numbering in Daniel reflect the narrative's presentation of Belshazzar. The offer to make Daniel “the third ruler in the kingdom” (Dan 5:7, ESV) is sometimes read as an echo of Belshazzar's subordinate position. The wording alone cannot establish such an arrangement.

The reports of the [[fall-of-babylon-539|Persian capture of Babylon in 539 BCE]] name Nabonidus and Cyrus but leave Belshazzar's fate unknown. The story of his death and the appearance of [[darius-the-mede|Darius the Mede]] immediately afterward belong to Daniel's account of the transfer of rule. The extrabiblical sources illuminate Belshazzar's earlier authority more directly than they illuminate the events of his final night. [@abc, 109–110] [@glassner-chronicles, no. 26, 232–239]
