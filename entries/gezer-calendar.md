## Overview

The Gezer Calendar is a small limestone tablet inscribed with a list of farming tasks from [[gezer|Gezer]]. The author groups the tasks in periods of one or two months. The sequence begins with ingathering and moves through sowing, crop harvests, and work on vines to summer fruit. It is usually dated by its script to around the tenth century BCE. The text illustrates how early alphabetic writing could record the rhythm of agriculture in the southern Levant and supplies comparisons for the vocabulary of work and seasons in the Hebrew Bible.

## Description and Provenance

R. A. S. Macalister discovered the tablet in 1908 during excavations for the Palestine Exploration Fund. Its find context does not supply a secure date, so scholars have compared its letterforms with other early alphabetic inscriptions. The tablet is now in the Istanbul Archaeology Museums. Seven main lines and writing near one edge cover part of its surface. Apparent traces of erased writing have been taken as evidence that the stone was reused.

## Contents

A common reading divides the text into eight periods totaling twelve months. There are two months for ingathering, two for sowing, and two for late sowing. Periods of one month each are assigned to working flax, harvesting barley, and a subsequent harvest whose accompanying term is disputed. Two months for tending the vines and a month for summer fruit complete the sequence. Some interpreters associate the harvest after barley with wheat. The text groups time by recurring work rather than specifying month names or exact dates.

The word for month is written *yrḥ* in some entries and *yrḥw* in others. The latter form is often read as a dual, “two months,” although other grammatical analyses have been proposed. Writing at the margin is sometimes understood as a personal name, possibly Abijah. The reading and the suggestion that it identifies the writer remain uncertain.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Two laws associate a festival of ingathering with the end or turn of the year (Exod 23:16 and 34:22). The tablet opens with a cognate term for ingathering. Deut 16:9–15 also sets festival observance in relation to cutting grain and gathering produce. The inscribed list shows how such tasks could locate events within a familiar agricultural cycle. In the book of Ruth, barley harvest begins when Naomi and Ruth reach Bethlehem, and Ruth continues gleaning through the wheat harvest (Ruth 1:22 and 2:23).

The calendar's *lqš*, often translated “late sowing,” is related to the term for late growth in Amos 7:1. Its use of *zmr* for work on vines is comparable with the verb for pruning in Lev 25:3–4. Its final entry, *qṣ*, means summer fruit, the image Amos uses to sound a warning through the similarity between “summer fruit” and “end” in Hebrew (Amos 8:1–2).

## Scholarly Issues

Scholars differ over whether to call the language early Hebrew, Phoenician, or a local Canaanite variety sharing features with both. Letterforms and spelling give a limited basis for distinguishing nearby dialects at this date. Proposed uses include a scribal exercise, a mnemonic for remembering the sequence, and a practical schedule. The list itself gives neither an official issuer nor festival names.
