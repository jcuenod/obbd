## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern rulers exchanged gifts, letters, and envoys to maintain diplomatic ties. The practice is particularly well documented in the Late Bronze Age Amarna letters from Egypt. The rulers who wrote these letters expected appropriate gifts in return and could complain when a partner disappointed them. Biblical stories describe similar exchanges among kings, from supplies sent for Solomon's building projects to the Babylonian delegation that visits Hezekiah. The meaning of a gift depended on the relationship between the parties and on who described the exchange.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] record exchanges among Egypt, Babylonia, Mittani, and other kingdoms. Gifts include gold, horses, chariots, and luxury goods. Courts also requested skilled workers and other forms of assistance. Envoys carried both the gifts and the letters that interpreted their purpose. One ruler could appeal to what an earlier king had given or complain that a current gift lacked the expected quantity or quality [@moran-amarna, EA 22, 35] [@cos3, 3.92A–C, 3.92E–G].

Gifts allowed rulers to assert both material interests and prestige. Mario Liverani emphasized how reciprocity could meet a practical need while sustaining the status claimed by both kings. A court could also portray goods received from abroad as submission, especially in public images or inscriptions celebrating royal power. Letters between the parties may convey a different account of the relationship.

In the Aramaic [[adon-letter|Letter of King Adon]], a ruler appeals to Pharaoh for help against a Babylonian force, citing his loyalty. It illustrates the kind of appeal for Egyptian military support behind Ezek 17:15 and Jer 37:5–7. The sender's city and historical setting remain uncertain. James D. Moore considers whether the surviving papyrus was a later scribal exercise [@tad, A1.1] [@moore-deaps-adon].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 2 Sam 8:9–10 the king of Hamath sends his son to congratulate David on defeating a shared opponent and to bring valuable vessels. In 1 Kgs 5:1–12 (MT 5:15–26), [[hiram-of-tyre|Hiram]] provides cedar and cypress in exchange for supplies of wheat and oil. Later, Hiram makes his dissatisfaction clear after receiving towns in Galilee (1 Kgs 9:11–14). The exchange shows the possibility of negotiation and grievance within an ongoing royal relationship.

The queen of [[sheba|Sheba]] brings luxury goods and receives gifts from Solomon in 1 Kgs 10:1–13. The visit's historicity is debated, and the narrative uses the exchange to emphasize Solomon's wisdom and standing. In 1 Kgs 15:18–19 Asa sends silver and gold to persuade Ben-hadad to break an alliance with Israel. Here a transfer is part of a proposal to change an existing political commitment.

## Interpretive Significance

The Hebrew word *minḥâ* can mean a gift, a tribute payment, or a ritual offering. The visitors in 1 Kgs 10:24–25 bring goods yearly to Solomon. Whether “gift” or “tribute” best conveys the scene depends in part on the account's portrayal of his royal standing. A transfer to another court can likewise shade into a payment to secure cooperation.

In 2 Kgs 20:12–13 [[merodach-baladan|Merodach-baladan]] sends a letter and a gift after hearing that Hezekiah was ill. Hezekiah displays his wealth to the visitors. Some scholars connect the embassy with Babylonian efforts to find allies against Assyria, often placing it near Merodach-baladan's brief return to power in 703 BCE. The date and purpose of that embassy remain debated [@rinap3-1, nos. 1, 22 i 20–65].
