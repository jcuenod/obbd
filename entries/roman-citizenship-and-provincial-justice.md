## Overview

Roman citizenship was a legal status that could matter when a person faced coercion or trial. Roman governors heard cases in the provinces, while local magistrates and other authorities exercised powers of their own. Surviving evidence comes from speeches, correspondence, and narratives written for different purposes. Together they help explain why citizenship matters when Paul is beaten or threatened with interrogation in Acts, and why his appeal to Caesar changes the course of the proceedings.

## Greco-Roman Evidence

In his prosecution of Verres, Cicero describes a man who asserted Roman citizenship while being subjected to corporal punishment. Cicero's outrage depends on his audience recognizing the significance of the claim. The speech was delivered in the late Republic and aimed to establish abuse of power. Its rhetoric gives evidence for the importance of citizenship while leaving much later provincial procedure to other sources [@cicero-verres, 2.5.161–170].

Around 112 CE Pliny asks Trajan how to deal with people accused of being Christians. He reports marking those who held citizenship for transfer to Rome. The correspondence gives a later example of status affecting a provincial governor's handling of a case. Pliny's letters and Acts describe different accusations and proceedings [@pliny-letters, 10.96–97].

## In the Bible

In Acts 16:19–39 the magistrates at Philippi have Paul and Silas beaten and imprisoned. Paul invokes their citizenship before accepting release. In Acts 22:24–29 he raises the issue before being scourged for interrogation, and the officers stop. One scene concerns what has already happened and the other a threatened action. Both use the citizenship claim to expose the authorities to scrutiny.

Acts 24:1–23 describes a hearing before Felix in which an advocate states the charge and Paul gives his defense. In Acts 25:6–12, before Festus, Paul appeals to Caesar and is told he will go to Caesar. Luke depicts an appeal made before a governor in a particular dispute over venue and adjudication. The details of how the appeal operated across the provinces cannot be reconstructed from this scene alone.

## Interpretive Significance

The local magistrates, military officers, and governors in Acts occupy distinct positions. The claim to citizenship has force within each encounter, but the immediate issue can be punishment, interrogation, or the forum for trial. See [[courts-and-legal-procedure]] for earlier legal settings and [[pliny-trajan-correspondence]] for the later exchange about provincial proceedings.
