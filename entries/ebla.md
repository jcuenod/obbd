## Overview

Ebla was a city-state in northwestern Syria, identified with Tell Mardikh. Its palace archives preserve thousands of cuneiform tablets and fragments, mainly from the twenty-fourth century BCE. They document government, diplomacy, religious life, and an early Semitic language now called Eblaite. Initial claims that the tablets directly confirmed biblical names and places have largely been rejected. For biblical studies their lasting contribution is to show how early some religious names and scribal practices were established in Syria, many centuries before the surviving texts of the Hebrew Bible.

## Location and Identification

Tell Mardikh lies south of Aleppo. An Italian expedition led by Paolo Matthiae began excavating in 1964. An inscribed statue fragment bearing the name of a ruler of Ebla provided evidence identifying the tell with the city already known from Mesopotamian texts. Architectural remains and documents then revealed occupation in both the third and second millennia BCE.

## Archaeological History

The records from Palace G came to light in the 1970s. The number of inventoried pieces is about 17,000, including many fragments that belong to the same tablets. The records cover the distribution of textiles and metals, palace personnel, diplomacy, and offerings to gods. They also include lexical lists in Sumerian and Eblaite. These lists are records of [[scribes-and-scribal-education|scribal learning]] and helped scholars identify how a writing system developed in Mesopotamia was adapted to a Semitic language in Syria. The precise classification of Eblaite within early Semitic remains discussed.

The palace burned in the later third millennium BCE, and the destruction preserved much of the archive. Sargon and Naram-Sin of Akkad each claimed campaigns involving Ebla, and scholars have proposed these rulers and other agents for the destruction. Evidence for Ebla's conflict with [[mari|Mari]] also affects reconstructions. The identity of the party responsible remains uncertain. A later fortified city occupied the site in the Middle Bronze Age and was destroyed near the end of that period, around 1600 BCE.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The earliest publications by Giovanni Pettinato and subsequent popular accounts suggested that Ebla named cities of the plain in Genesis, preserved the divine name YHWH in a personal-name element, or identified an Eblaite official with the biblical Eber. Those identifications have not held up. Pettinato qualified some early claims, and work on the tablets now identifies Ibrium as a minister rather than a king. Similar syllables or similar personal names cannot by themselves identify the same places, individuals, or gods.

The evidence for religious vocabulary is more substantial. Ebla's tablets attest the Syrian god Dagan, whose name corresponds to [[dagon|Dagon]], the god worshipped at Ashdod in 1 Sam 5:2–5. A god called Kamiš provides an early comparison for [[chemosh|Chemosh]], the god of Moab in Num 21:29. The tablets also name Rašap, comparable with [[resheph|Resheph]], whose name can be read as a personification of pestilence in Hab 3:5. Aštar is among the other deities attested. The names show the age and geographical reach of religious language. The form of worship and the roles of the gods could change widely from one place and century to another [@ddd, 216–218].
