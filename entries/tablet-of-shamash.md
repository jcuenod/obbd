## Overview

The Tablet of Shamash is a ninth-century BCE Babylonian monument from Sippar. Its Akkadian inscription relates how Nabû-apla-iddina restored the image and offerings of Shamash, the city's sun god. A relief presents the king before the god and depicts a prominent solar disk. The tablet combines royal patronage, temple administration, and the proper representation of a deity in one object. It supplies a concrete Babylonian comparison for biblical passages about solar worship and images, although the Jerusalem rites criticized in Kings and Ezekiel are not described in enough detail to identify them with the Sippar cult.

## Description and Provenance

The limestone tablet is about 29 centimeters high and 18 centimeters wide. Hormuzd Rassam recovered it in 1881 at Abu Habbah, ancient Sippar. It is now in the British Museum (BM 91000). A clay container and clay renderings of the carved scene were associated with it. One of the clay pieces carries the name of Nabopolassar, showing that a later king was involved in the monument's preservation or renewal. The stone's relief occupies the head of one face, and cuneiform text continues below and around it [@anep].

## Contents

At one side of the relief the sun god sits inside a columned shrine, holding a rod and ring. Introduced by attendants, the king stands before him. Between the two sides is a large rayed disk on a support. A band below the scene represents water, and a caption names the astral figures Sin, Shamash, and Ishtar in relation to the Apsu. The image situates the king's approach to the god within a setting marked by both temple architecture and cosmic imagery.

The inscription recounts the interruption of cult and the earlier use of a disk associated with Shamash. It relates the finding of a model and grounds the new image in recovery of an authoritative form. It also records provisions for the cult and temple personnel. The inscription presents image-making, materials, offerings, and royal grants as related parts of restoring service to the god.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 2 Kgs 23:11 Josiah removes horses dedicated to the sun and burns solar chariots. Ezek 8:16 depicts men turning eastward to worship the sun at the Jerusalem temple. The Tablet of Shamash shows how a temple could give visible form to a solar deity through both a disk and an anthropomorphic image. It also shows royal participation in a solar cult, a point of comparison with the association of the horses and chariots with the kings of Judah.

Isa 44:9–20 portrays the making of a cult image as evidence of its maker's folly. The account from Sippar justifies the work of artisans by appealing to an authorized model and restoration of worship. Reading the texts together brings out the issue of authority implicit in the biblical polemic.

## Scholarly Issues

The disk and the enthroned figure both refer to Shamash. Interpretations differ over whether the scene represents the movement of the disk during restoration, a continuing installation, or a pictorial composition that combines several moments. The image and text should be compared without assuming they record an identical sequence [@horowitz-cosmic-geography].
