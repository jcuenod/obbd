## Overview

Cyrus II founded the Persian Empire in the sixth century BCE by bringing Media, Lydia, and Babylonia under his rule. His conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE ended the Neo-Babylonian Empire and brought its former territories, including Judah, into the Persian sphere. Babylonian sources commemorate Cyrus's victory and present him as a restorer of worship. Isaiah describes him as YHWH's agent, and Ezra links his rule to the rebuilding of Jerusalem's temple. These sources illuminate both the political change and the distinct hopes attached to it by different communities.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[nabonidus-chronicle|Nabonidus Chronicle]] records Cyrus's defeat of the Median king Astyages and the capture of Babylon in 539 BCE. The latter followed fighting at Opis. The chronicle describes the Persian army entering Babylon itself without battle. It is a more concise account than the [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]], which describes Marduk choosing Cyrus and presents the conquest as the restoration of just rule. The [[verse-account-of-nabonidus|Verse Account of Nabonidus]] similarly discredits [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] and praises the new regime. Each account selects events for its own purpose. [@abc, 106, 109–110] [@glassner-chronicles, no. 26, 232–239] [@anet, 305–307]

Greek accounts describe a wider career that includes the defeat of Croesus and conquest of Lydia. Herodotus and Ctesias give different accounts of Cyrus's career and death. Xenophon's *Cyropaedia* organizes the life of Cyrus around a model of education and rulership. It is especially valuable for later Greek thinking about kingship. The Babylonian records offer firmer dates for events such as the [[fall-of-babylon-539|fall of Babylon]].

## Biblical Portrayal

Isa 44:28 calls Cyrus YHWH's shepherd, an image of a ruler assigned responsibility for others. Isa 45:1–7 describes him as YHWH's anointed and as equipped for victories over other kings. Applying “anointed” to a foreign ruler is exceptional in the Hebrew Bible. The oracles are commonly read in the context of Cyrus's rise in the 540s BCE, near the conquest of Babylon. The use of [[shepherd-kingship|shepherd imagery]] connects his victories with the expectation of rebuilding Jerusalem.

Ezra 1:1–4 and 2 Chr 36:22–23 present Cyrus as authorizing return and the rebuilding of the temple. Ezra 5:13–16 recalls his authorization and the return of vessels taken by Nebuchadnezzar. The Aramaic memorandum in Ezra 6:3–5 includes royal funding and provisions for the restored temple. These passages portray a change in the conditions of exile through a decision by the new imperial ruler. The form and authenticity of the surviving versions of the decrees are debated. [@abd, 2:732–740]

## Historical Assessment

Babylonian texts show that Cyrus supported the restoration of some cult centers. They do not independently attest a decree for Judah, and the scope and timing of local Persian initiatives are uncertain. Evidence for continuing Judean communities in Babylonia is consistent with a gradual movement of some returnees and the persistence of communities abroad. Josephus later relates that Cyrus read Isaiah's prophecy. That account documents reception of the biblical story in antiquity, while contemporary evidence for the episode is lacking.
