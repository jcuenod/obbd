## Overview

The Epic of Gilgamesh is a Mesopotamian poem about a king of Uruk, his friendship with Enkidu, and his search for a way to escape death. The story took shape over many centuries, from Sumerian poems and early Akkadian versions to a form widely copied in the first millennium BCE. In that form, a flood survivor tells Gilgamesh how he escaped a catastrophe sent by the gods. His story provides a close comparison for the flood in Genesis. Other episodes and versions have been compared with biblical reflections on human mortality, friendship, and enjoyment of life.

## Manuscripts and Date

Individual Sumerian poems describe episodes involving Bilgames, the Sumerian form of the hero's name. An Akkadian epic was already circulating in the Old Babylonian period in the early second millennium BCE. Later second-millennium witnesses show the story traveling beyond Mesopotamia. A fragment found at [[megiddo|Megiddo]] documents its circulation in the Late Bronze Age Levant. These copies preserve stages in a developing tradition. [@george-gilgamesh]

The form commonly called the Standard Babylonian Epic is attributed in a later scholarly tradition to Sîn-lēqi-unninni. Important copies come from the seventh-century BCE [[library-of-ashurbanipal|collections at Nineveh]]. The narrative reaches its conclusion in Tablet XI. Tablet XII was appended and translates part of a separate Sumerian poem about the realm of the dead. Its events do not fit seamlessly into the plot of the preceding tablets. [@george-gilgamesh]

## Contents

The opening presents Gilgamesh as a ruler whose behavior burdens Uruk. The gods provide a counterpart in Enkidu, who becomes his companion. Together they face Humbaba, the guardian of the Cedar Forest, and the Bull of Heaven. After Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh wanders in search of an escape from his own mortality. His journey brings him to Utnapishtim, the man who survived the flood and received exceptional life from the gods. [@george-gilgamesh] [@anet]

In Tablet XI, Utnapishtim tells how he built a boat after receiving a warning, survived the flood, released birds, and offered sacrifice. This part of the epic reworks material known from the older [[atrahasis|Atrahasis tradition]]. Gilgamesh fails a test of wakefulness. He obtains a plant that could restore youth, but a serpent takes it while he bathes. At the end of the journey he returns to Uruk, where the walls recall the poem's opening praise of the city. [@george-gilgamesh] [@lambert-millard-atrahasis]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The sequence of a vessel coming to rest, birds sent to test conditions, and an offering after the flood closely resembles Gen 8:4, 6–12, 20–21. Many scholars infer that the biblical writers drew on a Mesopotamian flood tradition, while the route and timing of transmission are debated. The [[gilgamesh-flood-tablet|Nineveh flood tablet]] is one surviving manuscript in that tradition. The serpent and lost plant have also been compared with the loss of access to life in Gen 3:22–24. The plant in the epic offers rejuvenation, and the circumstances of its loss differ from the human actions in Genesis. [@george-gilgamesh]

In the Old Babylonian version, a tavern keeper advises Gilgamesh to enjoy food, clothing, and family life within the limits of mortality. Eccl 9:7–9 urges comparable pleasures. The speech in that form is absent from the Standard Babylonian version, and a direct literary link remains disputed. The comparison is clearest when the particular witness to the speech is identified. [@george-gilgamesh] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Attempts to trace one route of transmission must account for the epic's many recensions, or forms of the text, and the independent movement of individual stories. The name Gilgamesh also appears in the Aramaic Book of Giants tradition among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Its appearance in that setting shows how material connected with Mesopotamian heroes could be recast within a very different story.
