## Overview

Shalmaneser III ruled Assyria from 858 to 824 BCE and campaigned repeatedly in the Levant. His inscriptions name Ahab of Israel among the opponents at Qarqar in 853 BCE and record tribute from Jehu in 841 BCE. Other accounts describe his encounter with Hazael of Damascus. These records provide some of the earliest firm absolute dates for Israelite history. Shalmaneser is unnamed in the books of Kings, whose accounts of the same rulers offer different perspectives on regional alliances and changes of power.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[kurkh-monoliths|Kurkh inscription]] lists a coalition opposing Assyria at the [[battle-of-qarqar|battle of Qarqar]]. It names Ahab the Israelite and attributes to him a substantial contingent. Assyrian records calculated the time of the campaign through an official dating system that can be correlated with an absolute chronology. The inscription’s claims about victory and the forces involved serve royal self-presentation. The chronic effort required in later campaigns makes the longer-term outcome more complex than the victory language suggests.

The [[black-obelisk|Black Obelisk]] identifies tribute received from Jehu and depicts a delegation bringing goods before the king. A prostrate figure represents the submission, though whether he is Jehu in person or an envoy is uncertain. Other inscriptions describe Shalmaneser’s fighting with Hazael. Their account of Hazael as a man with no dynastic pedigree is an Assyrian judgment about the legitimacy of the new ruler.

## Biblical Portrayal

First Kings 16:29–34 introduces Ahab and stresses his religious conduct. First Kings 22:1 refers to a period without war between Aram and Israel and goes on to describe new hostilities. The biblical narratives do not mention Ahab’s participation at Qarqar. Political cooperation against Assyria in one year can coexist with conflict between regional rulers at other times. The two sets of evidence select episodes for different reasons.

Second Kings 8:7–15 gives an account of [[hazael|Hazael]]’s accession that fits the Assyrian portrayal in broad outline, although it describes his relationship with the earlier king in its own terms. Second Kings 9–10 centers on [[jehu|Jehu]]’s revolt and its consequences, including losses to Aram in 10:32–33. The account gives no report of the tribute known from Shalmaneser’s inscriptions. The payments are intelligible as one response to a regional situation in which Assyria could threaten Damascus and receive goods from rulers farther south.

## Historical Assessment

The dates 853 and 841 BCE anchor the reigns of Ahab and Jehu regardless of uncertainties within the regnal scheme in Kings. The inscriptions establish contact with Assyria, though they cannot date every event that the biblical history associates with those rulers. Shalmaneser continued campaigning after his first clashes in the west. Late in his reign a major revolt led by one of his sons divided Assyria. It diverted resources and helps explain why the sustained pressure of Shalmaneser’s earlier western campaigns did not continue in the same form immediately afterward.
