## Overview

The tomb of Rekhmire at Thebes preserves paintings and inscriptions from Egypt's Eighteenth Dynasty. Rekhmire was a vizier, a senior official under Thutmose III and Amenhotep II. His tomb shows visitors presenting goods, craftspeople at work, and foreign captives making mud bricks for the temple of Amun. The paintings provide unusually detailed evidence for Egyptian labor and royal administration. They help readers picture the work described in Exodus and the office attributed to Joseph in Genesis.

## Description and Provenance

The tomb, catalogued as TT100, is in the cemetery at Sheikh Abd el-Qurna on the west bank of the Nile at [[thebes|Thebes]]. It was decorated with scenes that represent Rekhmire in roles he held as a royal official. Norman de Garis Davies published the tomb in detail, and facsimiles painted by Nina de Garis Davies preserve images of individual scenes. They are particularly useful where the paintings are difficult to view or have suffered damage.

## Contents

One scene shows workers making mud bricks under supervision. It represents successive tasks, including mixing the material and shaping and drying bricks. The accompanying text identifies foreign captives as laborers making bricks for the temple works of Amun. Other paintings show delegations bringing objects and animals from a variety of regions, as well as skilled work in temple workshops.

Texts associated with Rekhmire's office are conventionally called the Installation of the Vizier and the Duties of the Vizier. They describe the official's responsibility for administration and handling business brought to his attention. Some material may draw on administrative traditions older than the surviving copy. Together the texts and images present an ideal of service to the king and oversight of his resources.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 1:13–14 associates the oppression of the Hebrews with mortar and brick. Exod 5:6–19 describes the withdrawal of straw supplies while overseers still require the same number of bricks. The tomb helps readers see the roles of workers and supervisors and the organization needed to produce bricks for large building projects. Documents from the Ramesside period add evidence for designated quotas and problems obtaining straw, including the Louvre Leather Roll and Papyrus Anastasi IV. The paintings give a comparison for the conditions described in Exodus without establishing a link to the people in the narrative.

In Gen 41:40–43 Pharaoh gives Joseph authority second only to his own and invests him in public. The office of vizier provides a comparison for delegation of such broad responsibility, while the Genesis story uses a setting and terminology of its own. Rekhmire's representations of visiting delegations also illuminate the court ceremony behind images of foreign rulers bringing gifts or tribute in Ps 72:10–11.

## Scholarly Issues

A decorated tomb portrays a version of public office intended to honor the official. Its representations of labor and foreign delegations reflect Egyptian conventions and political claims. They give evidence for how Egyptian elites understood the organization of work and tribute, while direct conclusions about individual workers require additional evidence.
