## Overview

The Laws of Lipit-Ishtar is a Sumerian law collection associated with the king of Isin, in southern Mesopotamia, who reigned c. 1934–1924 BCE. It presents rules about matters such as inheritance and hired animals within a larger account of the king's duty to establish justice. Its opening credits the gods with giving the king that task, and its closing invokes blessings and curses concerning the treatment of his monument. This arrangement helps readers compare how Mesopotamian and biblical law collections use their closing sections to explain what is at stake in honoring or disregarding them.

## Manuscripts and Date

The collection is written in Sumerian in cuneiform, a script made up of wedge-shaped signs. Much of it is reconstructed from broken clay tablets, many from Nippur, a city in southern Mesopotamia [@roth-law]. These Old Babylonian copies, made during the early second millennium BCE, show that scribes continued to copy the work beyond its original royal setting. The collection also refers to a stele, an upright inscribed stone, and surviving stone fragments preserve parts of its opening.

## Contents

In the prologue, or opening section, the gods call Lipit-Ishtar to establish justice and restrain violence. The surviving rules take an if–then form: a particular situation is followed by its legal consequence. They address disputes over property, family arrangements, and damage to hired animals. In the epilogue, or closing section, the king presents his work as the fulfillment of his commission and invokes a blessing for honoring the monument and curses against damaging or effacing it [@roth-law].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Like the earlier [[laws-of-ur-nammu|Laws of Ur-Nammu]], this collection presents justice as a royal responsibility granted by the gods. Its opening, rules, and closing already show the arrangement familiar from the later [[code-of-hammurabi|Code of Hammurabi]]. Among [[ane-law-collections|ancient Near Eastern law collections]], it is an early surviving example of how a royal frame gives individual rulings a larger purpose.

The [[blessings-and-curses|blessings and curses]] of Lev 26:3–39 and Deut 28 also follow bodies of law, but they concern Israel's obedience to God's commands rather than the treatment of a royal inscription. Exod 23:20–33 likewise closes the Covenant Code, a collection of laws in Exodus, with promises and warnings, including a promise of protection if Israel listens. The voice in Exodus and Leviticus is God's, while Deuteronomy presents Moses addressing Israel about what God will do. These are comparisons of form and purpose, not evidence that a biblical writer used Lipit-Ishtar's collection.

The hired-ox rules set payments for particular injuries, while Exod 22:14–15 (MT 22:13–14, following the Hebrew numbering) distinguishes borrowing from hiring and considers whether the owner was present. In another provision, when a wife has borne no children, children borne to the man by a prostitute can be his heirs [@roth-law]. In Judg 11:1–2, by contrast, Jephthah's father has sons by his wife, and they drive Jephthah out and deny him a share. The texts show why a child's place in the household mattered to [[inheritance-and-birthright|inheritance]], but they depict different family circumstances, and Judges tells a story rather than issuing a rule.

## Scholarly Issues

The damaged copies make it difficult to decide whether every fragment of a Sumerian law text belongs to this collection.
