## Overview

The Jehoiachin ration tablets are cuneiform records from the palace administration of Nebuchadnezzar II at Babylon. They record oil allocations to a man identified as king of Judah, to five royal sons and to other people of Judean origin. One tablet dates to 592/591 BCE, within a few years of the king’s deportation from Jerusalem. The records show that Babylonian officials still used his royal title and supplied his household. They complement biblical accounts of the deportation and of later changes in the former king’s standing.

## Description and Provenance

The tablets came from the archive excavated by Robert Koldewey’s expedition in the area of Nebuchadnezzar’s Southern Palace at [[babylon|Babylon]]. The findspot was within a vaulted structure near the palace’s northeastern corner. Ernst F. Weidner published the relevant selections in 1939. Most of the dated documents from the structure fall within the middle years of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign. One tablet concerning the royal household comes from his thirteenth year, which began in 592 BCE and extended into 591 BCE. That date applies securely to the dated text, while the other Jehoiachin notices are less precisely placed. [@anet, 308] [@abd, 3:661–662]

## Contents

The tablets are distribution records that identify recipients and amounts of oil. They write [[jehoiachin|Jehoiachin’s]] name in Babylonian forms such as Yaʾukin and call him king of the land of Judah. They also list five sons of the king of Judah and other Judeans. A variant entry apparently describes him as a royal son. It supplies evidence that the scribes did not always use a single formula when identifying the same recipient. [@anet, 308]

The broader archive includes people from many regions, including Ashkelon, Egypt, the eastern Mediterranean and Anatolia. Some were skilled craftspeople. Its diversity reflects the mobility of specialists and the concentration of deportees and foreign dependents at the capital of a large empire. These texts describe a palace system of provision and represent only part of the experience of the [[babylonian-exile|Babylonian exile]]. [@anet, 308] [@abd, 3:661–662]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 24:12, 15 names Jehoiachin and his household among the people taken to Babylon when [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] captured Jerusalem in 597 BCE. The tablets place a man of that name and title in the capital soon afterward. 1 Chr 3:17–18 also gives names of royal descendants, while the tablets give a count of five sons included in the rations. The sources need not describe the family at the same date or for the same purpose. Dating by Jehoiachin’s exile in Ezek 1:2 provides another sign that the king remained an important point of reference. [@anet, 308] [@abd, 3:661–662]

## Scholarly Issues

2 Kgs 25:27–30 and Jer 52:31–34 report that [[amel-marduk|Amel-Marduk]] released Jehoiachin from prison and gave him a continuing allowance. The tablets predate that event by about three decades, so the oil allocations represent provision under the earlier king. The administrative lists do not specify Jehoiachin’s living quarters, the restrictions on his movement or whether those conditions changed before the release. They document an allowance that is compatible with a range of arrangements for a royal captive. [@anet, 308] [@abd, 3:661–662]
