## Overview

Ahiqar is a wise adviser to the Assyrian kings Sennacherib and Esarhaddon in an ancient story told in Aramaic. After his adopted nephew plots against him, Ahiqar escapes execution and is later vindicated. The earliest surviving copy comes from fifth-century BCE Egypt and places a collection of proverbs alongside the story. Tobit brings Ahiqar into its own account of a family living at Nineveh. The Aramaic work also shows how sayings about speech, discipline, and loyalty could circulate alongside stories about the dangers of serving a king.

## Manuscripts and Date

The oldest surviving witness is a damaged papyrus among the [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine papyri]], found at Egypt's southern frontier. The Ahiqar text was written on a reused papyrus that had carried a customs account from the reign of Xerxes I, dating to c. 475 BCE. The surviving literary copy is usually placed in the fifth century BCE, after that account. Its narrative uses the administrative form of [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]] widely used under the Persian Empire. James Lindenberger proposed that some proverbs preserve features of an older western variety of Aramaic, a distinction that concerns the formation of the work as well as the date of the physical manuscript. [@tad, C1.1] [@anet, 427–430]

## Contents

Ahiqar serves Sennacherib and then [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]]. He adopts Nadin, his nephew, and prepares him to take his place among the [[court-officials|royal officials]]. Nadin turns the king against him, and a man charged with carrying out the execution hides Ahiqar instead. The damaged Aramaic papyrus leaves parts of the narrative uncertain. Later versions tell of his vindication and Nadin's exposure more fully, often through an episode in which Ahiqar answers challenges from the king of Egypt. [@tad, C1.1] [@anet, 427–430]

The sayings address such matters as discretion before rulers, the dangers of uncontrolled speech, and the education of a son. They make the work a witness to [[ane-wisdom-literature|wisdom literature]], the sayings and teachings that circulated among educated readers across the region. Several make particularly good sense beside a story about a loyal adviser and an ungrateful heir. Their independent form also makes it possible that some circulated before they were joined to the story. [@tad, C1.1]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Tob 1:21–22 presents Ahiqar as Tobit's nephew and as a senior official under Esarhaddon. He supports Tobit during his blindness (Tob 2:10). Near the book's end, Tobit recalls how Ahiqar survived betrayal and the betrayer was caught by his own plot (Tob 14:10). The names vary across textual traditions. The episode assumes enough familiarity with the tale for a short allusion to carry the moral point.

The advice about disciplining a son is comparable to Prov 23:13–14. A damaged Aramaic saying about the power of a soft tongue resembles the image in Prov 25:15, though the surviving wording differs. Dan 6 shares the pattern of a trusted courtier whose opponents secure a death sentence and whose survival reverses their success. A proposed parallel between Nadin and the abusive servant in Matt 24:45–51 remains debated. The widespread figure of a disloyal subordinate allows comparisons without establishing a link between the works. [@tad, C1.1]

## Scholarly Issues

A much later tablet from Seleucid-period Uruk lists a scholar at Esarhaddon's court called Aba-Enlil-dari, whom it identifies by an Aramaic name corresponding to Ahiqar. That entry may preserve a memory of a historical court scholar. It also comes from an age when the story had long been in circulation, so its independent value for the seventh century BCE is uncertain. Later Syriac, Armenian, and Arabic forms, and the reworked episode in the Greek Life of Aesop, show the reach of the tradition. They have their own settings and wording, which matter when identifying the source of a saying.
