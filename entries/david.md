## Overview

David is remembered in the Hebrew Bible as the king who established his rule in Jerusalem and founded a dynasty. His reign is conventionally placed in the early tenth century BCE, using the biblical sequence and lengths of reign. The later Tel Dan Stele gives the clearest evidence outside the Bible for a dynasty bearing his name. Archaeologists debate what the remains of Jerusalem and nearby fortified sites reveal about his kingdom. The existence of a remembered founder and the extent of royal power attributed to him are questions that rest on different kinds of evidence.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The ninth-century BCE [[tel-dan-stele|Tel Dan Stele]] is an Aramaic victory inscription. Most specialists read a surviving expression as “House of David,” a designation for a royal dynasty or the state it ruled. The inscription names the house in connection with conflicts later than David's lifetime. It is strong evidence that a neighboring kingdom knew the dynasty by that name, although it does not record actions by David himself. [@ahituv-echoes] [@cos2]

A proposed reading of a damaged line of the [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]] would supply another reference to the House of David. Scholars working from the stone and early impressions of its surface disagree about the traces and how to restore the missing letters. A proposed reference to David in Shoshenq I's list of places in the Levant is also disputed. The Tel Dan reading rests on stronger evidence than either of these proposals. [@ahituv-echoes] [@anet]

## Biblical Portrayal

In 2 Sam 5:6–10 David captures Jerusalem and occupies the stronghold called the City of David. Second Samuel also portrays YHWH promising him a house, in the sense of a continuing dynasty (2 Sam 7:11–16). These traditions help explain the significance of the name attested at Tel Dan. The military successes described in 2 Sam 8:1–14 have a wider geographical reach, but their scale and historical setting are debated. The forty-year reign reported in 1 Kgs 2:10–11 underlies conventional estimates such as c. 1010–970 BCE, which are approximate.

## Historical Assessment

In the [[city-of-david|City of David]], the Stepped Stone Structure and the adjacent remains that Eilat Mazar called the Large Stone Structure are central to discussion. Mazar interpreted the latter as part of a substantial building that could be linked to David's palace. Others question whether all its wall segments were contemporary or whether enough survives to identify its date and function. The wider [[low-chronology-debate|Iron Age chronology debate]] affects which buildings can be placed in the tenth century. Scholars favoring an earlier date for certain phases see stronger evidence for organization in Judah, while lower chronologies associate some monumental remains with later rulers.

At [[khirbet-qeiyafa|Khirbet Qeiyafa]] above the Elah Valley, excavators documented a fortified settlement and an ostracon, a potsherd used as a writing surface. Its writing and the site's political affiliation are debated. The excavators interpreted it as a Judahite center, while others have proposed a local polity or another regional affiliation. The fortifications provide evidence for organized labor in the borderland. Their relationship to David or to the confrontation set in the Elah Valley in 1 Sam 17:1–3 remains uncertain.
