## Overview

Shahar and Shalim are Ugaritic deities whose names are conventionally understood as Dawn and Dusk. A Late Bronze Age tablet from Ugarit describes their conception by El within a composition that combines ritual language and myth. Biblical poets also personify dawn, and some interpreters connect the name Salem or Jerusalem with Shalim. These comparisons show how times of day and names could carry religious associations in Northwest Semitic traditions. The etymology of Jerusalem and the relationship between the Ugaritic and biblical passages remain open to interpretation.

## Sources and Iconography

The principal evidence is a tablet conventionally catalogued as KTU 1.23, often called the Birth of the Gracious and Beautiful Gods. Sections with directions or invocations stand alongside a myth in which [[el|El]] fathers Shahar and Shalim. The combination suggests a performance or ritual setting. Whether it represents an actual sacred marriage ceremony, a royal ritual, or another form of recitation is debated. The text supplies the names and their relationship more directly than it supplies a precise historical occasion.

The names correspond to words associated with morning and evening. The myth’s treatment of them presents these times within a larger pattern of generation and divine order. The surviving tablet does not securely identify visual attributes by which the two deities could be recognized on an independent object.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 14:12 addresses a falling figure with a title that can be translated “shining one, son of dawn.” The personified dawn in the expression is a close linguistic comparison with Shahar. The figure being taunted is the son, rather than Dawn himself. Other proposed comparisons involve the morning star or the god Athtar, whose climb and descent have been compared with the poem. The passage uses celestial language in a taunt against a fallen ruler.

Ps 139:9 speaks of the wings of the dawn. Job 3:9 and Job 41:18 use imagery associated with the first light, while Ps 110:3 includes a difficult reference to dawn and dew. These uses illustrate the range of poetic personification. They do not by themselves attest a cult of Shahar. Gen 14:18 names Salem and Ps 76:2 associates Salem with Zion. The resemblance to Shalim has encouraged comparison with the name of Jerusalem, though a resemblance in spelling is only part of the etymological case.

## Scholarly Issues

A common explanation understands Jerusalem as a foundation or establishment of Shalem. Ancient spellings and the divine name favor the comparison, but the original sense cannot be established with certainty. Suggestions that names such as Absalom or Solomon invoke Shalim are more speculative. Similar elements can belong to ordinary words for peace or well-being.

An older reading of a ritual line in the Ugaritic composition was compared with Exod 23:19 and its prohibition concerning a kid and its mother’s milk. Photographic reexamination weakened that reading. The [[kid-in-mothers-milk|biblical law]] therefore should not be explained by assuming the Ugaritic tablet documents the same action.
