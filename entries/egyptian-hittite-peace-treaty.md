## Overview

Ramesses II of Egypt and Ḫattušili III of Ḫatti concluded a peace treaty around 1259 BCE. Its terms survive in hieroglyphic inscriptions in Egypt and in clay tablets written in Akkadian from the Hittite capital. The rulers agreed to peace, mutual aid, and the return of fugitives, with protection for those returned. The treaty is a well-preserved example of diplomacy between sovereigns who called each other brothers. It helps explain the language of royal agreements in Kings and provides a comparison for biblical rules about fugitives.

## Description and Provenance

The Egyptian inscriptions at Karnak and the Ramesseum date the agreement to year 21 of [[ramesses-ii|Ramesses II]], sixteen regnal years after the [[kadesh-battle-reliefs|Battle of Kadesh]]. The surviving Akkadian copies are among the tablets from Ḫattuša, the Hittite capital. The parties exchanged treaty texts on silver tablets, which are lost. The Egyptian text presents the agreement from the Hittite side as it was received in Egypt. The copies found at Ḫattuša preserve the text sent in the name of Ramesses [@anet, 199–203] [@beckman-hdt, 90–94].

## Contents

The agreement commits both great kings to maintaining peace and supporting each other against enemies. Its reciprocal provisions express a relationship of parity, or formal equality. Certain clauses reflect more particular interests. Ramesses, for example, promises support for the succession of Ḫattušili's son, a matter of consequence to a king who had displaced his own nephew. Both sides also agree to return fugitives. Provisions against reprisals protect the returned people and their households. The gods of both lands witness the agreement [@beckman-hdt, 90–94] [@anet, 199–203].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

1 Kgs 5:12 (MT 5:26) describes peace and a treaty between Solomon and [[hiram-of-tyre|Hiram of Tyre]]. Hiram addresses Solomon as brother in 1 Kgs 9:13, even as he objects to the towns Solomon has given him. In 1 Kgs 20:32–34, Ahab's use of brother for Ben-hadad leads to negotiations and a covenant. Amos 1:9 condemns Tyre for disregarding a covenant of brothers without identifying the original rulers. In each of these settings [[brotherhood-language|kinship language]] helps express or negotiate a political relationship.

The extradition clauses are comparable with biblical discussions of fugitives. In 1 Kgs 2:39–40 Shimei goes to Gath and retrieves two enslaved people who fled from him, although Kings mentions no treaty obligation. Deut 23:15–16 (MT 23:16–17) requires that an escaped slave be given a place to live and protection from oppression. The law takes a different position from the treaty's requirement to return fugitives, and scholars debate the scope of the biblical provision and the relevance of the diplomatic comparison.

About thirteen years after the treaty, a Hittite princess married Ramesses II. The later marriage illustrates how [[diplomatic-marriage|royal marriage]] could reinforce relations. Solomon's marriage to Pharaoh's daughter in 1 Kgs 3:1 is compared with that wider practice.

## Scholarly Issues

Each side's surviving text presents the other ruler's overture as leading to the compact. The variation shows how diplomatic language could safeguard the prestige of both courts. The agreement belongs with ancient parity treaties, a category distinct from the [[hittite-vassal-treaties|vassal treaties]] frequently discussed in relation to biblical [[covenant-and-treaty-form|covenant forms]]. The Egyptian-Hittite agreement is exceptionally well documented among early treaties, and agreements between states predate it.
