## Overview

Amorites were people identified with the west in texts from late third- and early second-millennium BCE Mesopotamia. Families called Amorite came to rule cities in Syria and Mesopotamia, among them Mari and Babylon. Later, Amurru was the name of a specific kingdom on the northern Levantine coast, and Assyrian usage could apply the name more broadly to the west. Biblical writers use “Amorites” for several groups associated with the land before Israel. The shared name has a long history, and its meaning depends on the date and setting of each text.

## Sources

Mesopotamian scribes used the Sumerian sign sequence *MAR.TU* and the Akkadian word *Amurrum* for people associated with the west. The designation could apply to people who kept herds and moved between seasonal pastures. Early second-millennium texts also record people with Amorite names in cities, armies and ruling households. The [[mari-letters|letters from Mari]] show relations between settled towns and mobile groups and give a fuller picture than the stereotypes of some Mesopotamian literary texts. [@abd] [@cos3]

Personal names preserve important evidence for Amorite speech. Many names represent a West Semitic language, the broad group of languages to which Hebrew also belongs. Scribes usually wrote their documents in Akkadian, so their records tell historians more about names and social affiliations than about spoken Amorite grammar.

## History

By the early second millennium BCE, dynasties often described as Amorite ruled major cities. [[hammurabi|Hammurabi]] belonged to the dynasty of Babylon, and Zimri-Lim ruled at [[mari|Mari]]. Their courts used established Mesopotamian forms of kingship and administration. Historians continue to debate how migration, local alliances and the rise of new families each contributed to these political changes. [@abd] [@cos3]

In the Late Bronze Age, Amurru designated a particular kingdom in the northern Levant. The [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] document the activities of Abdi-Ashirta and his son Aziru, who dealt with Egypt as they expanded their influence among neighboring towns. Aziru later entered into a treaty with the Hittite king, illustrating how the kingdom lay between rival powers. In first-millennium Assyrian texts, Amurru could again refer more generally to western lands. [@moran-amarna] [@beckman-hdt] [@abd]

## In the Bible

In Num 13:29 the Amorites are among the peoples said to occupy the hill country. Num 21:21–31 associates the name with the territory of Sihon east of the Jordan, while Josh 10:5 calls the kings of five southern cities Amorites. In Amos 2:9–10 the term represents the earlier inhabitants whom God displaced before Israel. Gen 14:13 also portrays an Amorite, Mamre, as Abram's ally, a reminder that the term does not always mark an enemy.

Some scholars have used preferences for “Amorite” and [[canaanites|“Canaanite”]] as clues to distinct traditions or layers of writing within biblical books. The names can each carry a broad sense, and the distribution of the labels alone provides little basis for identifying a particular source. Mid-twentieth-century scholars including William F. Albright once used Amorite migrations to date the ancestors in Genesis to the Middle Bronze Age. That reconstruction has lost support because comparisons in names and ways of life are too widely distributed to date the narratives or establish a connection with particular migrants. [@abd]
