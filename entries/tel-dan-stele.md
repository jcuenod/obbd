## Overview

The Tel Dan Stele is a fragmentary Aramaic victory inscription from the ninth century BCE. Avraham Biran's excavations recovered three pieces in 1993 and 1994. The author was a king who celebrated victories over Israel and a polity designated by the consonants *bytdwd*, usually read “House of David.” The ruler is commonly identified as Hazael of Damascus. The stele gives independent evidence for a Davidic dynastic designation and preserves an Aramaean account of warfare also narrated from other perspectives in Kings. Reconstructing the identities and deaths of individual monarchs depends on damaged lines.

## Description and Provenance

The three basalt fragments carry Old Aramaic in an alphabetic script. The largest fragment, conventionally called A, was found in 1993 in reused masonry near Dan's gate complex. Two smaller fragments, B1 and B2, came to light in the same general area in 1994. Their secondary contexts show that the monument had been broken up before the stones were incorporated into later features. The original place of erection is unknown. The fragments are held by the Israel Antiquities Authority and have been exhibited at the Israel Museum [@neaehl, 1:329–332].

## Contents

The surviving text invokes Hadad and refers to the author's father, whom a king of Israel opposed. Its author boasts of defeating opposing forces. In the first editors' arrangement, the remaining words at lines 7–9 form claims about killing a king of Israel and a king associated with *bytdwd*. The letters of the dynastic expression survive on fragment A. The full personal names depend on combining the fragments and supplying missing letters. The most familiar reconstruction identifies the kings as Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah [@kai, no. 310; @ahituv-echoes, 467–473].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Second Kings associates Hazael with the deaths and wars of the period. Elisha meets Hazael before he takes power (2 Kgs 8:7–15), and Joram is wounded fighting Aram (2 Kgs 8:28–29). In 2 Kgs 9:14–28, Jehu brings about the deaths of both kings. If the stele refers to the same deaths, its author claims responsibility in a way that differs from the narrative in Kings. One possible explanation is that Aramaean victory set the conditions for Jehu's revolt. The extant texts do not establish how their accounts should be reconciled.

The probable reading “House of David” gives an Aramaean designation comparable to the dynastic language of Isa 7:2, 13 and the promise about David's house in 2 Sam 7:11–16. The inscription attests use of that name in the first millennium BCE. It supplies little evidence about the territory or institutions of David's own reign.

Dan's frontier position is consistent with changes of control in the biblical narratives. The city appears in accounts of Jeroboam's sanctuary and the earlier attack by Ben-hadad (1 Kgs 12:28–30; 15:20). Hazael's attacks on Israel in 2 Kgs 10:32–33 provide a historical setting for renewed pressure on the northern kingdom.

## Scholarly Issues

Hazael's name is missing, and the placement of the small fragments beside A is reconstructed. The normal reading of *bytdwd* fits dynastic naming conventions. Proposals for a place name or other referent have won less support. Each question depends on different evidence, and the survival of the key consonants is more secure than the restoration of the full royal names.
