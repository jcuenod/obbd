## Overview

The East India House Inscription is a stone tablet commissioned by Nebuchadnezzar II, who ruled Babylon from 604 to 562 BCE. It describes the king's restoration of sanctuaries and his work on the walls, gates, streets, and palace of Babylon. The tablet is named for the London building where it was once displayed and is now in the British Museum. It shows how a Babylonian king presented monumental building as both a royal achievement and an act of service to the gods. This combination of claims gives context to Nebuchadnezzar's boast in Daniel.

## Description and Provenance

The tablet is a nearly square limestone slab covered by ten columns of Akkadian cuneiform. The signs use an archaising script, meaning that the forms imitate an older style of writing. Its probable origin is Babylon, although its archaeological findspot is unrecorded. The tablet was known through the museum in East India House, the headquarters of the East India Company. The British Museum records it as BM 129397, with a registration dated 1938.

## Contents

[[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar]] introduces himself as the son of Nabopolassar and describes his standing with [[marduk|Marduk]] and [[nabu|Nabu]]. He claims personal responsibility for work at Esagil, Marduk's temple at [[babylon|Babylon]], and at Ezida, Nabu's temple at Borsippa. He records further work on temples and divine ceremonial boats. This makes the tablet a substantial example of a royal [[temple-building-accounts|account of building]].

The inscription also concerns the city as a whole. It describes the double inner wall of Babylon and additional defensive works, the raising and paving of the main processional street, work on the [[ishtar-gate|Ishtar Gate]], and renovation of the royal palace. The style makes room for details of materials and construction alongside praise of the gods. The overall picture is of a king enhancing the places through which the gods, the court, and the inhabitants experienced the city.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Dan 4:30 (MT 4:27), Nebuchadnezzar credits his own power and majesty with building great Babylon as a royal residence. The monumental rebuilding described on the tablet gives the boast a recognizable setting. The account in Daniel puts self-exaltation at the center of the king's speech. Nebuchadnezzar's own inscriptions routinely frame similar claims in terms of his duties to the gods and their favor toward him.

Jer 51:58 anticipates the destruction of Babylon's wide walls and high gates. The tablet helps explain why these structures had become a fitting image for the strength of imperial Babylon. Isa 46:1 depicts Bel and Nebo bowed down and their images loaded onto animals. Bel (“lord”) was a title of Marduk, and Nebo is the biblical form of Nabu. The inscription attests their standing in the royal cult, which makes the reversal in Isaiah recognizable to readers.

## Scholarly Issues

The tablet illustrates royal building rhetoric generally, and a specific literary relationship to Daniel remains unestablished. Its descriptions can be compared with other inscriptions and excavated buildings, while its account of the king's piety belongs to the official portrayal of his reign. Jerusalem and Judah fall outside the inscription's account of building and worship. Other sources provide the evidence for Nebuchadnezzar's campaigns in Judah.
