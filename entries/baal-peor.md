## Overview

Baal of Peor is a deity associated with a site in the territory of Moab, east of the Jordan River. The name occurs in the Hebrew Bible's accounts and recollections of an episode at Shittim, where Israelites join in sacrifice and worship and a plague strikes them. Later biblical writers recall Peor as a particularly serious case of turning to another god. The accounts illuminate how a local shrine could be imagined as a place where communal, sexual, and religious boundaries were crossed. A phrase in the Psalms also raises a disputed question about whether rites for the dead were involved.

## Sources and Iconography

The title means “Lord of Peor” and probably designates a local form of [[baal|Baal]]. The title links the god to Peor, a name connected in biblical tradition with the district of Beth-peor. No known inscription or securely identified image from the region attests Baal of Peor by this title. Information about the deity's worship comes chiefly from the biblical accounts, which are concerned with Israel's conduct and preserve few details about the religion of the [[moabites|Moabites]] [@ddd].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Num 25:1–9 begins with Israel at Shittim. Moabite women invite Israelites to sacrifices for their gods, and the people eat and bow down. The narrator describes Israel as joining itself to Baal of Peor. The passage then turns to an Israelite man and a Midianite woman, whom the priest Phinehas kills before the plague ends. Many scholars see the change from Moabite women to a [[midianites-and-kenites|Midianite]] woman as evidence that the narrative joins two strands, one centered on joining the god and the other on Phinehas's response.

Other biblical passages use the episode for different purposes. Num 31:16 attributes the trouble at Peor to Balaam's advice, linking the events to a figure independently known from the [[deir-alla-inscription|Deir ʿAlla inscription]]. Deut 4:3 recalls the destruction of the people who followed Baal of Peor as a warning. Hos 9:10 recalls Israel devoting itself to what the prophet calls shame. Paul probably recalls the plague in 1 Cor 10:8, where he gives the number of deaths as twenty-three thousand in one day, compared with the twenty-four thousand in Num 25:9.

## Scholarly Issues

Ps 106:28–31 recounts the plague and its end, adding that the people ate “sacrifices offered to the dead” (ESV). Some scholars take “the dead” as a disparaging term for lifeless gods in keeping with the reference to sacrifices for other gods in Numbers. Others understand the phrase as sacrificial meals connected with [[care-for-the-dead|the care or veneration of the dead]]. The psalm gives too little ritual detail to decide between the readings.

The rabbinic commentary Sifre Numbers 131 associates the meals at Peor with *marzeaḥ* gatherings. A [[marzeah|marzeaḥ]] was a West Semitic association known for communal feasting, sometimes proposed to have links with the dead. The commentary shows how a later Jewish tradition understood the meals. Its description is considerably later than the setting of the biblical episode and cannot establish how worship at Peor was practiced.
