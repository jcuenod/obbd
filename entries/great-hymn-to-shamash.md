## Overview

The Great Hymn to Shamash is an Akkadian poem of about two hundred lines praising the Mesopotamian sun god. It survives in first-millennium BCE copies from Assyria and Babylonia. The hymn connects the sun's daily journey and ability to see far with the god's attention to cases of wrongdoing. It describes travelers, captives, merchants, and judges, bringing divine justice into ordinary settings. The combination of sunlight and justice helps explain why scholars compare the hymn with Psalm 19, which moves from the sun's course across the sky to praise of YHWH's instruction.

## Manuscripts and Date

The work is also called the Great Prayer to Shamash. It is known from fragments and copies found at Nineveh and sites in Babylonia, including Sippar, a center of the worship of [[shamash|Shamash]]. Modern editions reconstruct the poem from several witnesses. All the known copies date to the first millennium BCE. Its language and topics may preserve earlier material, but the date and extent of its composition and expansion remain uncertain. [@lambert-bwl, 121–138] [@cos1, 1.117]

## Contents

The hymn depicts Shamash traveling across the sky and looking over lands and peoples. His reach includes people far from their homes and those in danger. Travelers, fishermen, captives, and the sick appear among those whose circumstances the god can see. Divine oversight concerns daily life, including places and activities beyond the control of local officials. [@cos1, 1.117] [@lambert-bwl, 121–138]

The same ability to see makes Shamash a witness against dishonest conduct. He can distinguish a judge who takes a bribe from one who judges fairly. The poem also treats dishonest weighing and trade as matters of divine concern. In a society that used [[weights-and-silver-money|weighed goods and metal]] for payment, a false measure could be hard for buyers to detect. The hymn treats these abuses of public authority and commerce as visible to the god. [@cos1, 1.117] [@lambert-bwl, 121–138]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ps 19:1–6 (MT 19:2–7, using the Hebrew verse numbering) describes the heavens and the sun's passage, whose heat reaches everywhere. Ps 19:7–14 (MT 19:8–15) praises YHWH's instruction and asks for protection from hidden faults. Nahum Sarna compared this conjunction of sunlight and right conduct with ancient Near Eastern solar religion. In the psalm the sun is part of the ordered heavens, and the instruction comes from YHWH. The hymn shows one ancient setting in which solar sight and moral judgment were already closely associated. [@keel-symbolism]

Job 34:21–22 likewise describes divine scrutiny that darkness cannot prevent. The rules against accepting bribes in Exod 23:8 and against unequal weights in Deut 25:13–16 name kinds of misconduct also found in the hymn. The examples show how concerns about [[justice-and-righteousness|justice]] could take concrete form in very different kinds of texts.

## Scholarly Issues

The poem combines praise with themes also known from collections of wisdom and instruction. Its transmission in scholarly manuscripts allows for continued copying and revision, and the place of this particular text in temple worship remains uncertain. The Akkadian hymn should be distinguished from the Egyptian [[great-hymn-to-the-aten|Great Hymn to the Aten]], which is usually compared with Psalm 104. [@lambert-bwl, 121–138] [@cos1, 1.117]
