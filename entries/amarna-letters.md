## Overview

The Amarna Letters are nearly four hundred clay tablets found at the site of Akhetaten, the Egyptian capital founded by [[akhenaten|Akhenaten]]. Most contain diplomatic correspondence from the fourteenth century BCE and are written in Akkadian, the shared written language of much of the region. The letters connect the Egyptian court with other great kingdoms and with rulers of towns in Canaan, including Jerusalem. They provide contemporary evidence for Canaan's political geography and for the different ways rulers expressed rank, loyalty and alliance in the world behind biblical narratives.

## Manuscripts and Date

The tablets preserve correspondence from the reigns of Amenhotep III, Akhenaten and probably their immediate successors. Most letters were sent to Egypt, but the collection also preserves some messages from the Egyptian court. A smaller group of tablets contains literary or scribal material. The conventional designations use the prefix EA, for el-Amarna, in a numbering system established by J. A. Knudtzon and extended as further tablets entered the corpus. [@moran-amarna]

## Contents

Correspondence with major powers includes letters from Babylonia, Mitanni, Hatti (the Hittite kingdom), Assyria, Alashiya and Arzawa. Great kings commonly addressed a counterpart as “brother,” a diplomatic claim to comparable standing. Their exchanges of envoys, marriage proposals and valuable goods show how kings maintained relationships and complained when an exchange fell below expectations. [[brotherhood-language|Brotherhood language]] and [[diplomatic-gift-exchange|gift exchange]] helped define what one ruler could ask of another. [@moran-amarna]

The letters from Canaan describe a different relationship. Local rulers regularly present themselves as Pharaoh's servants and say that they fall at his feet, often using a formula that repeats the number seven. They report local rivalries, defend their loyalty and request troops or supplies. Such declarations are arguments addressed to a distant superior, so accusations against a neighbor should be read with the sender's interests in mind. [@moran-amarna]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The letters of Abdi-Heba, conventionally numbered EA 285–290, attest a ruler based at [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] in the fourteenth century BCE. He appeals for help and portrays Egyptian interests as threatened. Joshua likewise portrays Jerusalem as the seat of a local king (Josh 10:1–5). The comparison establishes that Jerusalem had political standing in the Late Bronze Age, while the letters and the biblical narrative describe different situations. The remains of fourteenth-century Jerusalem are difficult to assess, and the letters give little indication of the built city's size. [@moran-amarna] [@mazar-archaeology]

Diplomatic expressions also help explain scenes in the Bible. When Ahab calls the defeated Ben-hadad his brother, Ben-hadad's envoys seize on the title as favorable (1 Kgs 20:32–33). Likewise, Jacob's repeated bows in Gen 33:3 can be read alongside formal Near Eastern gestures of submission. In each case the expression or gesture helps communicate a relationship between parties.

## Scholarly Issues

The letters refer to people called [[habiru|ʿApiru]], also known as Habiru in other texts. The label crosses political boundaries and could refer to people outside established communities or in the service of armed leaders. Scholars usually interpret it as a social designation, and an often-proposed linguistic connection to the biblical term “Hebrew” remains debated. The unusual reference to Hebrews formerly with the Philistines in 1 Sam 14:21 invites comparison, but the letters provide no grounds for identifying the ʿApiru with the Israelites of the conquest narratives. [@moran-amarna] [@abd]

Canaanite scribes often wrote Akkadian with features of their own language. Some tablets include local glosses, or words added to clarify expressions, and their grammar can reflect local ways of speaking. Since the letters precede extensive written evidence for Hebrew, they help linguists reconstruct earlier stages of related Canaanite languages. [@moran-amarna]
