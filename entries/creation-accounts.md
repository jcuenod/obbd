## Overview

Creation accounts explain how the world and its inhabitants came to be and why they are arranged as they are. In ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt, accounts of beginnings survive in epics, hymns, ritual texts, and inscriptions. The opening chapters of Genesis present two distinct accounts, while biblical poems and sayings describe creation through still other images. Comparison shows how ancient writers used water, speech, conflict, and craftsmanship to make sense of beginnings. Particular similarities can clarify what a biblical text is doing, while the relationship between any two surviving works must be assessed case by case.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Babylonian poem [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]] tells how Marduk defeats Tiamat, a deity associated with the primeval sea. He uses her body to organize the world, arranges the heavenly bodies, and establishes his authority among the gods. These episodes form part of a larger story that exalts Marduk and Babylon. The poem's broad influence in Babylonia is compatible with the existence of other accounts that present the world's beginnings differently [@lambert-bcm].

The Old Babylonian story of [[atrahasis|Atrahasis]] begins with the gods' labor and the making of humans to take it over. The creation of humanity supplies the setting for a story that eventually reaches a flood. The fragmentary Sumerian work often called the [[eridu-genesis|Eridu Genesis]] combines the beginnings of human society, the founding of cities, and a flood. Other material about creation occurs in incantations, poems in which aspects of the world dispute their merits, prologues to myths, and learned lists. Creation could set the stage for a story or explain the arrangement of a particular institution [@lambert-millard-atrahasis] [@lambert-bcm].

Egyptian accounts can emphasize the emergence of a creator from the primordial waters, the first land to appear, or the formation of a community of gods. Several traditions associated with Heliopolis, Hermopolis, and Memphis could coexist and be combined. At Memphis, a text preserved on the [[shabaka-stone|Shabaka Stone]] attributes to Ptah the planning and enactment of creation through heart and tongue. The [[great-hymn-to-the-aten|Great Hymn to the Aten]] gives a different view of creation by celebrating the sunlight that sustains the daily life of the world [@ael].

The Late Bronze Age poems of Ugarit supply further comparisons. The conflict of Baal and Sea belongs to stories about divine kingship. It helps explain how victory over the sea could express authority over the world. The surviving poem does not narrate the making of the physical world by Baal. The broader tradition of [[chaoskampf|divine conflict with Sea]] could be associated with creation in other settings [@parker-unp] [@day-dragon].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 1:1–2:3 describes creation through a sequence of divine commands, separations, and assignments. God orders times and places, gives living creatures their domains, and rests on the seventh day. Many critical reconstructions assign this account to the Priestly tradition, a group of biblical texts with characteristic language and concerns. Its comparative profile includes the separation of waters, the assignment of functions to heavenly bodies, and the establishment of order by divine decree. The primary emphasis is on a world ordered and declared good by God.

In Gen 2:4–25 the narrative focuses on the ground, the garden, its water, and human relationships. God forms the first human from the ground, plants a garden, and makes a companion for the human. This account is generally distinguished from the Priestly account in language and composition. It is especially comparable to ancient texts about humanity's material formation and responsibility for work. Those themes are treated under [[creation-of-humanity|the creation of humanity]].

Biblical poetry also depicts creation through other actions. Ps 74:12–17 associates the defeat of the sea and its monsters with the setting of boundaries and the establishment of seasons. Job 38:4–11 presents the world as a building whose foundations God measured and the sea as water restrained by doors. Prov 8:22–31 presents Wisdom as present while God established the world's features. These passages select images that serve the needs of a lament, a divine speech, or a description of Wisdom.

## Interpretive Significance

The discovery of Mesopotamian creation texts led some early scholars, including Friedrich Delitzsch, to treat Genesis as derived from a Babylonian original. W. G. Lambert emphasized the limited force of several proposed matches with Enuma Elish. Parallels such as the ordering of waters and the making of humans are real points of comparison. The Babylonian poem describes a conflict among gods as part of the process, while Genesis 1 organizes creation through a sequence of divine commands. Most current comparisons proceed from a wider shared repertoire of ideas, with the possibility of more specific relationships considered passage by passage.

The command that brings light in Gen 1:3 is comparable to the efficacy of divine speech in the Memphite theology. The similarity is general and a historical relationship is debated. Ps 104:20–30 is closer to the Great Hymn to the Aten in its combination of night, the activity of animals, human work, and dependent life in the sea. Scholars disagree over whether the psalm reflects the hymn through a line of transmission or shares themes from wider traditions of praise for creation. The Hebrew psalm’s account of divine breath participates in its own account of how life is sustained.
