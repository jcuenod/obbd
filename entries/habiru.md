## Overview

The names Habiru and Apiru occur across the ancient Near East in second-millennium BCE texts, from Mesopotamia and Syria to Canaan and Egypt. They describe people in varied circumstances, including workers, armed groups, and people attached to local rulers. The evidence generally points to a social designation rather than one ethnic community. The resemblance between the name and the biblical word Hebrew has prompted a long debate. The comparison helps explain how labels for people outside established networks could be used, without identifying all Habiru as ancestors of Israelites.

## Sources

Cuneiform texts write the term in syllables or use the sign group conventionally rendered SA.GAZ, whose meanings can overlap with it. Egyptian writings also refer to people conventionally called Apiru. The sources assign them different roles, including hired fighters, dependent laborers, or armed people who threatened settlements. The label could mark a relationship to established households or authorities rather than a common place of origin. Its force varies with the source and the activity described. ## History

The [[amarna-letters|Amarna Letters]] give particularly vivid evidence from fourteenth-century BCE Canaan. Rulers report that towns are being lost to the Apiru or accuse opponents of cooperating with them. In the correspondence concerning Labayu of [[shechem|Shechem]], the term appears in accusations and rebuttals within local power struggles. These appeals were written to an Egyptian court, giving their authors reasons to emphasize disorder and rival disloyalty. A stele of Seti I from Beth-shean also names Apiru in the context of hostilities. [@moran-amarna] [@anet]

## In the Bible

Gen 14:13 calls Abram a Hebrew. In Gen 39:14, 17, the label occurs in the speech of an Egyptian character about Joseph. In 1 Sam 29:3, Philistine leaders use it in referring to David's party. These and similar passages often involve encounters in which someone marks a group from outside it. Exod 21:2 and Deut 15:12 also use the word in laws about [[slavery|enslavement or servitude]]. These settings help explain why some scholars have looked for a social history behind the biblical term. [@abd]


The superficial resemblance between the terms does not settle the differences in their consonants and use. Some researchers propose a relationship through the history of the words or through the later literary use of an older social label. Others view the linguistic case as weak. The Habiru evidence concerns people of many origins and cannot establish a simple equation with the biblical Hebrews. Some people operating outside established cities might have participated in the [[emergence-of-israel|formation of early Israel]], but the designation itself does not identify who they were or how they contributed.
