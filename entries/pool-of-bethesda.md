## Overview

The Pool of Bethesda is generally identified with the remains of two adjoining pools near the Church of St Anne in Jerusalem. John locates a healing at a pool with five porticoes near the Sheep Gate or a feature associated with sheep. The excavated basins and their dividing wall explain how a pool complex could have five colonnaded walks. The date and use of individual features are debated. Evidence for a later Roman healing sanctuary nearby needs to be distinguished from what can be established for the time depicted in John.

## Location and Identification

The ruins lie immediately north of the [[herods-temple|Second Temple precinct]], near the Church of St Anne. The northern and southern basins are separated by a barrier or dam. Four sides and the central division would provide space for five colonnades. That arrangement accounts for the unusual description in John 5:2, though the exact colonnades are not all recoverable from the archaeology. The location near the temple’s northern approaches contributes to the widely accepted identification.

The name appears as Bethesda, Bethzatha, and other variants in Greek manuscripts of John. English translations also differ on the exact understanding of the phrase involving sheep. Neh 3:1 and 12:39 mention a Sheep Gate in an earlier account of Jerusalem’s walls. They supply evidence for a name in that part of biblical geography without fixing the gate’s location in the first century CE.

## Archaeological History

The two basins were built or modified at different times and have been interpreted as reservoirs. The southern pool has also been understood as a place for ritual immersion. Its steps and arrangement are relevant to that interpretation, while dates and functions of individual phases remain debated. The scale and setting of the pools show that water storage or use was important near the temple even if only some phases were associated with ritual bathing.

A healing complex known from the Roman period has been connected with Asclepius or Serapis. The evidence is clearer for phases after the Roman refounding of Jerusalem. A predecessor before 70 CE has been argued, but remains uncertain. The existence of a later healing sanctuary does not require that the Gospel scene took place within a sanctuary of the same kind.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In John 5:2–9 the pool is a gathering place for sick people, and a man describes his difficulty reaching the water when it is stirred. Jesus heals him through a command to get up. The topography gives a context for people waiting along shaded approaches near the water. The association with stirred water enters the narrative in the man’s explanation. The added explanation about an angel in John 5:3–4 appears in some manuscripts and is absent from important early witnesses. Its presence in later copies cannot establish what was believed or practiced at the site in the first century.

The Copper Scroll has sometimes been taken to contain a place name resembling a term for two pools and related to Bethesda. Both the reading and geographical identification involve interpretation. The strongest evidence for the identification remains the convergence of the archaeological layout and the location in Jerusalem described by John.
