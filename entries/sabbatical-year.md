## Overview

The sabbatical year is the seventh year in a biblical cycle during which cultivated land is to rest. Exodus and Leviticus prescribe leaving fields, vineyards and other plantings without the regular cycle of sowing and harvesting. Deuteronomy links the seventh year to debt remission and public instruction. The rules extend the Sabbath pattern to agricultural time. Accounts from the Second Temple period attest the practice, while later Judaean Desert documents show how the year of release could figure in contracts.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Crop rotations and periods of fallow can be useful in rain-fed [[agriculture-and-farming|agriculture]]. A rule that farmland should rest every seventh year combines that practice with a recurring obligation. No securely attested ancient Near Eastern source institutes the same fixed septennial fallow. Mesopotamian royal edicts remit certain obligations and release some debt dependents, but their timing generally depended on royal proclamation. The seven-year drought in the Ugaritic [[aqhat-epic|Aqhat epic]] illustrates the literary use of a seven-year span. It provides no evidence for an agricultural institution. ## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 23:10–11 provides for six years of sowing and gathering and a seventh in which the land is left alone. The poor may eat what grows, and the remainder is for wild animals. Vineyards and olive groves fall within the same regulation. Some interpreters understand the text to permit separate cycles for individual plots, while others infer a common year from its formulation. Lev 25:1–7 describes a Sabbath for the land, with no sowing, pruning or ordinary harvesting. The spontaneous yield may still be eaten by household members, dependents and animals. Lev 25:18–22 anticipates the supply problem by promising a sixth-year harvest that will sustain the people through the fallow and the next sowing. [@abd, 5:857–861] [@king-stager-life]

Deut 15:1–2 concerns the [[debt-release|remission]] of claims, and Deut 31:10–13 calls for a public reading of the law at Booths in the year of remission. Neh 10:31 (MT 10:32) connects the agricultural year and the relinquishing of claims in the same pledge. The timing relates land use, obligations to creditors and public remembrance while the texts assign different purposes to these activities. [@abd, 5:857–861]

## Interpretive Significance

Lev 26:34–35, 43 imagines that the land will rest while its inhabitants are in exile. 2 Chr 36:21 draws on this image and the seventy years associated with Babylon in Jer 25:11. The Chronicler interprets exile as a consequence of neglected Sabbaths. The number supplies no independent count of the occasions on which a fallow was missed. [@abd, 5:857–861]

1 Macc 6:49, 53 connects shortages at Beth-zur and Jerusalem with the sabbatical year. Josephus (*Antiquities* 14.202) preserves a decree attributed to Julius Caesar that exempts Judaea from a payment in the seventh year because the people did not sow or gather from their trees. Tacitus mentions a seventh-year cessation in a hostile description (*Histories* 5.4). The Murabbaʿat debt document Mur 18 contains a release-year expression whose legal force is disputed. The later land lease Mur 24E reckons its term to the eve of a release year. Such records support the practical significance of the cycle. Aligning all reported release years in a single chronology remains debated. [@abd, 5:857–861]
