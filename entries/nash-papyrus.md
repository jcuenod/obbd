## Overview

The Nash Papyrus is a Hebrew manuscript from Egypt containing a form of the Ten Commandments followed by the opening of the Shema. It consists of fragments of a single sheet that probably served a liturgical or devotional purpose. Its letterforms are commonly dated to the second century BCE. Before the discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls, it was the oldest known manuscript preserving substantial portions of Hebrew biblical text. Its wording and order help document the variety of forms in which familiar biblical passages circulated.

## Description and Provenance

W. L. Nash acquired the papyrus in Egypt in 1898. It is held by Cambridge University Library as MS Or.233. The exact place and circumstances of its ancient use are unknown. The surviving fragments preserve the text across parts of a single papyrus leaf. William F. Albright placed its handwriting in the Maccabean period. Comparisons with hands from the [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] broadly support a late Second Temple setting.

## Contents

The first part presents the Decalogue in a form that shares readings with both Exod 20:2–17 and Deut 5:6–21. Some wording matches one form more closely and some the other. The prohibitions of adultery and murder also come in a sequence attested in some Greek biblical witnesses. The papyrus then introduces the Shema (Deut 6:4–5) with a clause comparable to one found in the [[septuagint|Septuagint]] and absent from the corresponding Masoretic text. The combination gives evidence for how Hebrew readings related to those reflected in some Greek witnesses.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The sequence of adultery, murder, and theft in Luke 18:20 and Rom 13:9 also occurs in the Nash Papyrus. This small agreement shows that the commands could be recalled in different orders. It provides no basis for supposing that either New Testament author knew this particular sheet. In Mark 12:29–30 Jesus begins his reply about the greatest commandment with the Shema and the instruction to love God. The papyrus shows the prominence of the same passage in an earlier Jewish setting.

The Mishnah describes the Decalogue and Shema being recited together by priests in the temple (m. Tamid 5:1). The Babylonian Talmud refers to withholding the Decalogue from regular communal recitation because of sectarian claims (b. Ber. 12a). These later witnesses help explain why scholars have proposed a devotional or liturgical purpose for a manuscript containing both texts. The sheet itself gives no explicit record of its use.

## Scholarly Issues

The date rests primarily on handwriting, and acquisition through the antiquities market limits what can be said about provenance. The combined text could have been used in prayer, study, or another setting in which a short selection sufficed. Similarities to the Greek tradition are important witnesses to textual variation. The surviving evidence does not establish a single source for every distinctive reading.
