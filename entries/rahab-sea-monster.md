## Overview

Rahab is a hostile power subdued by God in biblical poetry. Job and Psalms place the defeat of Rahab alongside the restraint of cosmic waters, while Isaiah brings the figure into portrayals of Egypt and the crossing of the sea. The Hebrew name is associated with tumult or arrogance. It differs in spelling from the name of Rahab of Jericho. Other ancient Near Eastern traditions feature battles against Sea or against serpentine enemies, though the name Rahab itself has no secure counterpart in those traditions.

## Sources and Iconography

Biblical pictures of a divine victory over turbulent waters can be compared with the [[chaoskampf|combat traditions]] of Egypt, Syria and Mesopotamia. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, the storm god Baal contends with [[yam|Yam]], whose name means “Sea.” In the Babylonian [[enuma-elish|*Enuma Elish*]], Marduk defeats Tiamat and organizes the cosmos. These works provide parallels for the wider portrayal of a god establishing or securing order through combat. The Ugaritic forms corresponding to biblical [[leviathan|Leviathan]] and [[tannin|tannin]] offer closer lexical parallels for those separate figures. Rahab has no securely identified depiction in surviving art. [@smith-baal-cycle-1] [@lambert-bcm] [@day-dragon]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Job 9:13, Rahab's helpers bow beneath God's anger. Their presence suggests a defeated host, and Job uses that image to convey the disparity between divine power and his own standing. In Job 26:12, the striking down of Rahab accompanies God's mastery over the sea. Ps 89:9–10 (MT 89:10–11) pairs the calming of waves with the crushing of Rahab and the scattering of enemies. The imagery can encompass both the ordering of the world and victory over forces that threaten it. [@day-dragon] [@ddd]

Isa 51:9–10 invokes the arm of YHWH that cut Rahab in pieces and pierced the dragon. It immediately recalls the drying of the deep and the making of a passage so that the redeemed could cross. The sequence brings combat with the monster and Israel's deliverance through the sea into a single appeal. It asks that the power displayed in the past be exercised again. [@day-dragon]

Isa 30:7 calls Egypt Rahab in an oracle about the futility of relying on its aid. A name associated with tumult sharpens the irony of a power that can sit idle. Ps 87:4 groups Rahab with Babylon and other foreign peoples in a poem centered on Zion. There the name works as a poetic designation for Egypt in a setting concerned with belonging. [@ddd]

## Scholarly Issues

The Hebrew wording after Rahab in Isa 30:7 is difficult. Translations sometimes understand the transmitted wording as describing a power at rest. Other proposals redivide or adjust the consonants to obtain a smoother expression. The surrounding oracle consistently treats Egyptian assistance as ineffectual. Ps 40:4 (MT 40:5) uses a related plural form usually understood of proud or arrogant people. A deliberate echo of Rahab or the monster's allies is possible, though the verse does not name them.
