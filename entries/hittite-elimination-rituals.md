## Overview

Ritual tablets from the Hittite capital describe ways to remove illness, impurity, or divine anger from an afflicted person or community. Some rites send away a live animal or a human substitute, carrying what has been placed upon it. The tablets preserve practices from several parts of Late Bronze Age Anatolia and its neighbors. These texts provide some of the closest comparisons for the goat sent into the wilderness on the Day of Atonement and the release of a bird after purification in Leviticus.

## Manuscripts and Date

The sources survive chiefly in tablets from Ḫattuša, the capital of the [[hittites-and-hethites|Hittite kingdom]]. Many are copies from the Late Bronze Age, and their named practitioners and language point to several regional traditions. The rituals of Ašḫella and Uḫḫamuwa are associated with Arzawa in western Anatolia. Others draw on traditions associated with Kizzuwatna, in southeastern Anatolia, where Hittite, Luwian, and Hurrian practices met [@cos1, 1.61–1.64].

## Contents

Ašḫella's ritual is intended to remove plague from an army camp. Rams are made ready and sent away, and the accompanying speech addresses the divine powers associated with the affliction. Uḫḫamuwa's ritual also concerns plague and sends an animal toward enemy territory. In the ritual associated with Puliša a human substitute has a role. Some rites from Kizzuwatna employ birds. Across this evidence the act of removal can be joined with an offering, a substitute for someone endangered, or a request that an affliction leave the people.

A much earlier comparison comes from [[ebla|Ebla]] in third-millennium BCE Syria. A text discussed by Ida Zatelli associates sending a goat into the steppe with a purification. The evidence suggests a long history for the broad practice. Neither the Ebla example nor the variants from Ḫattuša establishes a single fixed ritual.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Lev 16:8–10, 20–22 describes the [[day-of-atonement-elimination|goat sent away on the Day of Atonement]]. Aaron lays both hands on its head and confesses the people's wrongdoing. The goat carries their iniquities to a remote place in the wilderness. The Hittite parallels help explain the movement of an affliction or burden from a community to a living carrier. The biblical destinations and speech have their own roles, and the precise meaning of Azazel remains debated [@ddd, 128–131].

Lev 14:4–7, 49–53 calls for the release of a live bird into the open country after a rite for a person or house. The transferred burden is more explicit in Lev 16. In Zech 5:5–11, wickedness is personified and transported to Shinar. It resembles elimination at the level of imagery. In 1 Sam 6:1–12 the Philistines send objects associated with their affliction as they return the ark. The analogy with an elimination rite is debated because the objects are also called a reparation offering and the movement restores a captured sacred object.

## Scholarly Issues

David P. Wright and later scholars have compared the biblical and Anatolian evidence within traditions extending through Syria. The particular meaning of transfer, substitution, and appeasement differs by text. Touching or placing a hand on ritual objects or animals does occur in Hittite sources. In Lev 16 the use of both hands and the accompanying confession give the transfer its stated content. The sources support a comparison with a regional family of rites, while derivation from a specific Hittite text remains unestablished.
