## Overview

First Enoch is a collection of Jewish writings attributed to the figure whom Genesis says God took away. Its sections took shape in the Second Temple period, from roughly the third century BCE into the early Roman era. They describe rebellious angels, Enoch's journeys, the movements of the heavenly lights, and a future judgment. The complete collection survives in Geʿez, the classical Ethiopic language, and is canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Aramaic fragments from Qumran preserve early forms of several parts. The collection gives important context for the New Testament's language of angels, judgment, and the Son of Man.

## Manuscripts and Date

The complete text survives in Ethiopic manuscripts. Some sections also survive in Greek and other languages, and Aramaic fragments are among the [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]]. The Qumran fragments belong to four of the collection's five main divisions. Their dates establish that some of the writings circulated well before the emergence of Christianity. The Parables, also called the Similitudes, have yet to be identified among the Qumran fragments.

## Contents

The Book of Watchers describes angels who descend to earth and father giants, teach forbidden arts, and incur judgment. It also describes Enoch's missions and journeys through the world of the dead and the heavens. The stories were probably circulating by the third century BCE. They develop the brief account of the [[sons-of-god|sons of God]] and the giants in Gen 6:1–4.

The Parables describe visions of judgment and give an important role to a heavenly figure called the Son of Man or Chosen One. Specialists often place this part between the late first century BCE and the early first century CE. Many favor a date before 70 CE, but the exact time and setting remain debated. The work offers a distinctive example of [[messianic-expectations|Jewish expectations of a heavenly deliverer and judge]].

The Astronomical Book, an early portion probably in circulation by the third century BCE, presents knowledge of the sun, moon, and calendar as revelation. The Dream Visions include a narrative in which animals represent people and kingdoms. Its second vision is usually dated to the time of the Maccabean uprising in the second century BCE. The Epistle includes instructions, warnings, and accounts of history and judgment. It brings together material developed in the later centuries of the Second Temple period.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 5:21–24 presents Enoch as a man who walked with God and whom God took. The later writings fill out his role as a recipient of knowledge inaccessible to other humans. James VanderKam and others compare Enoch with [[enmeduranki-and-the-apkallu|Enmeduranki]], a king who is seventh before the flood in some Mesopotamian traditions and receives divine knowledge. Mesopotamian traditions about sages who taught humans arts and skills also give a comparison for the Watchers, whose teachings are portrayed as dangerous.

Jude 14–15 cites a prophecy corresponding closely to the opening of the Book of Watchers. Its use shows the authority of the tradition for Jude without settling the limits of a Christian canon in that period. Jude 6 and 2 Pet 2:4 also associate rebellious angels with confinement until judgment. The suggestion that the imprisoned spirits in 1 Pet 3:19–20 belong to the same story world is debated.

## Scholarly Issues

Dan 7:9–14 gives a vision of divine rule that is important for comparison with the Parables. Matt 25:31 likewise portrays the Son of Man sitting on a throne to judge. The similarity places the writings within overlapping traditions of judgment and heavenly authority. The dating of the Parables and the possibility of shared sources leave the direction and nature of influence open.
