## Overview

The Melqart Stele is a basalt monument found near Aleppo in northern Syria in 1939. Its Aramaic inscription records a dedication to Melqart by a ruler named Bar-Hadad. A carved figure of the god appears above the inscription. The monument gives direct evidence that a god closely associated with Tyre received worship inland. Its worn royal names have also led scholars to compare it with the Ben-hadads in Kings, but competing readings leave the dedicator's specific identity uncertain.

## Description and Provenance

The stele was recovered at Breij north of Aleppo. Its setting among later remains does not establish where it originally stood. The upper panel depicts a standing male deity with an axe and an object generally identified as an ankh, an Egyptian symbol of life. The combination of an Aramaic inscription with visual forms known from the coastal Levant shows the circulation of religious and artistic traditions in Iron Age Syria [@kai, no. 201] [@anep, fig. 499].

## Contents

The inscription describes the setting up of a monument by Bar-Hadad for his lord [[melqart|Melqart]], who heard his appeal. It thus belongs to the practice of dedicating an object after receiving a favorable divine response. The name Bar-Hadad, meaning “son of Hadad,” is readable. Parts of the donor's ancestry and royal designation are much less clear. Proposed dates are generally in the ninth century BCE or near its end [@cos2, 2.33] [@kai, no. 201].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 1 Kgs 15:18–20, Asa seeks the aid of Ben-hadad son of Tabrimmon son of Hezion. W. F. Albright and Frank Moore Cross saw a correspondence with the worn letters on the stele and proposed the same lineage. Wayne Pitard and others have read the letters differently and connected the donor with northern Syrian rulers, including the political setting of Arpad. The identification depends on the reading of damaged writing and has not been settled.

1 Kgs 20:1 and 2 Kgs 8:7–9 name kings called [[ben-hadad|Ben-hadad]] in accounts about Ahab and Hazael. The stele shows that the name circulated among Aramaic-speaking rulers. It cannot resolve how many rulers of this name lie behind the biblical references or how they should be placed in sequence. 1 Kgs 16:31–32 describes Ahab's service to Baal after his marriage to [[jezebel|Jezebel]]. The dedication demonstrates that a Tyrian god could be worshipped in an inland kingdom, but the biblical text leaves that Baal's identity open.

## Scholarly Issues

The carved figure and the clearly readable words support a study of the god's cult even while the prosopography, or identification of individuals across records, remains contested. The inscription connects Melqart with one votive act in northern Syria. Its religious importance therefore does not depend on matching the donor with a king of Damascus.
