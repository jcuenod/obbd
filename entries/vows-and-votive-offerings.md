## Overview

A vow is a promise made to a deity. In biblical stories it often takes a conditional form in which a person asks for help and promises a gift or act of devotion in return. A votive offering is an object or sacrifice given in fulfillment of a vow. Ugaritic stories and Northwest Semitic inscriptions preserve comparable promises and dedications. Biblical texts describe vows in distress or thanksgiving and regulate how they are fulfilled, including when another member of a household can intervene.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the [[kirta-epic|Kirta Epic]] from Ugarit, Kirta vows a gift to Asherah of Tyre and Sidon if his undertaking succeeds (KTU 1.14 IV). The story later connects the vow with the goddess's anger and the king's illness. It uses the promise to explain why success brings an ongoing obligation.

The [[melqart-stele|Melqart Stele]] says that a dedicator fulfilled a vow after the god heard his appeal. Punic dedications frequently describe a gift in similar terms. Some examples occur on stelae from precincts conventionally called [[tophet|tophets]]. Their wording gives evidence for a public record of the response to a vow. The objects and rites associated with a dedication vary by context.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 28:20–22 Jacob promises devotion and a tenth if God sustains him and brings him home. Hannah vows to dedicate a son if her prayer is answered (1 Sam 1:11). In Judg 11:30–31 Jephthah's promise concerns what comes out to meet him if he returns victorious, setting up the tragedy that follows. 2 Sam 15:7–8 records Absalom saying that he made a vow in Geshur and will fulfill it at Hebron. The narratives place vows within relationships of dependence, gratitude, and political action.

Lev 7:16 distinguishes an offering made for a vow from other sacrifices. Lev 27:1–34 sets out valuations and rules for redeeming certain dedicated persons, animals, or property. Num 30:1–16 (MT 30:2–17) recognizes the binding force of promises and describes when a father or husband can annul a woman's vow. If he hears it and says nothing, it stands. The chapter treats a widow's or divorced woman's vow as binding upon her. It therefore relates the right to make a religious commitment to legal standing within a household.

## Interpretive Significance

Deut 23:21–23 (MT 23:22–24) says that a person who makes a vow must fulfill it without delay and that abstaining from a vow incurs no guilt. Eccl 5:4–5 (MT 5:3–4) likewise compares the freedom to make a vow with the obligation incurred once it is made. Ps 22:25 (MT 22:26) and 116:14, 18 describe fulfilling vows in a gathering of worshipers. In Jonah 1:16 the sailors make vows after surviving the storm, and in Jonah 2:9 (MT 2:10) Jonah promises sacrifice and fulfillment in a prayer of thanksgiving. These settings show how a promise made under pressure could later become an act of public acknowledgment.
