## Overview

The Izbet Sartah abecedary is a potsherd incised with an early alphabet near the transition from the Late Bronze to the Iron Age. Archaeologists found it in 1976 at a small settlement near modern Rosh HaʿAyin. Its last line gives a nearly complete sequence of letters, while the first four probably reflect practice. The placement of the letter pe before ayin agrees with examples from elsewhere and with the order of some biblical poems. The sherd is also evidence that writing was practiced at a small settlement.

## Description and Provenance

The object is an [[ostraca|ostracon]], a piece of broken pottery used as a writing surface. Excavators recovered it at ʿIzbet Ṣarṭah on the western edge of the central hill country. The excavation assigned it to the early Iron Age, and the letter forms are consistent with a date near the beginning of that period. Moshe Kochavi published the find in 1977, and Aaron Demsky analyzed the script and the letter sequence in the same journal issue. [@neaehl, 2:653–655] [@kochavi-izbet, 1–13; @demsky-izbet, 14–27]

## Contents

The inscription contains five lines. The upper lines are commonly interpreted as writing practice, and no continuous reading for them has gained wide acceptance. The bottom line, written from left to right, records an alphabetic sequence. An abecedary is a text written to list or practice the letters in order. This example is nearly complete, though some identifications and the ordering of individual signs are debated. Pe precedes ayin, a sequence also attested at [[kuntillet-ajrud|Kuntillet ʿAjrud]]. That independent evidence makes this ordering better understood as a variant convention than as an isolated mistake. [@neaehl, 2:653–655] [@demsky-izbet, 14–27]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

An alphabetic acrostic uses the sequence of letters to organize the starts of verses or stanzas. Lam 2:16–17, 3:46–51 and 4:16–17 place pe before ayin in the Hebrew text. The sherd shows that such an order was already available in early alphabetic practice. Psalm 119 and Prov 31:10–31 illustrate the more familiar ordering. These points of comparison concern the circulation and development of alphabet traditions, and the order alone cannot date the poems. [@abd, 1:59–60] [@demsky-izbet, 14–27]

Judg 8:14 has a young man write down local leaders’ names. Practice like the exercise on the sherd shows one way in which someone might have acquired the skills required by the episode. The repetitive sounds in Isa 28:9–10 have also been read as a possible echo of rudimentary instruction, but that interpretation is disputed. The text has several plausible readings and does not identify an exercise like this one. [@abd, 6:1002–1005]

## Scholarly Issues

The transitional script is often called Proto-Canaanite, referring to a stage of linear alphabetic writing before later regional scripts acquired distinctive forms. The alphabetic sequence provides little evidence for the language or ethnic identity of the writer. Some scholars identify the site with Ebenezer in 1 Sam 4:1, partly because it lies near Aphek, but no local inscription establishes that identification. A writing exercise attests some activity at or connected with a small settlement. It cannot establish how common [[writing-and-literacy|literacy]] was among its inhabitants. [@neaehl, 2:653–655] [@kochavi-izbet, 1–13; @demsky-izbet, 14–27]
