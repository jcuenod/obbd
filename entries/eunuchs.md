## Overview

Eunuchs held positions of trust at several ancient Near Eastern courts, including those of Assyria, Babylonia and Persia. Some administered royal households, guarded members of the royal family or exercised authority outside the palace. The Hebrew word *sārîs*, borrowed from an Akkadian title, can refer to a eunuch or a court official. Its meaning varies by passage. Biblical texts depict eunuchs as royal attendants, agents of political change and people whose place among worshipers raises questions about bodily status, ancestry and covenant membership.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Akkadian title *ša rēši* means approximately “one at the head” and designated a personal attendant or royal official. Old Babylonian uses include officials understood to be physically intact. Most specialists interpret the Neo-Assyrian title as normally designating a eunuch. Some Assyrian texts contrast its bearers with bearded officials, and palace reliefs often distinguish clean-shaven attendants from bearded figures. A smooth face in an individual sculpture is an uncertain guide to the person's anatomy. [@rinap1, no. 22 fragment 4] [@anep, figs. 373, 623]

Assyrian eunuchs served as commanders, governors and royal representatives as well as attendants. Appointment could place an official within the royal household and give the king a powerful servant without a recognized line of male heirs. Babylonian and Persian evidence places eunuchs or similarly titled officials near rulers and royal households. Greek writers also associate eunuchs with the Achaemenid court, often in tales of palace intrigue shaped by Greek conventions about Persia. [@rinap1, no. 22 fragment 4] [@rinap4] [@abd]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Potiphar bears the title in Gen 37:36 and Gen 39:1, and the Joseph narrative presents him as married. Translators often use “officer” there. Marriage alone does not resolve the question of physical status. In 1 Sam 8:15 Samuel expects the king's *sārîsîm* to receive revenues. In 2 Kgs 9:32 palace eunuchs side with Jehu against Jezebel. In Jer 38:7 Ebed-melech, a [[cush|Cushite]] courtier, challenges the treatment of Jeremiah. Esther places the eunuchs Hegai and Shaashgaz in charge of different groups of women in the royal residence at Susa (Esth 2:3, 14–15). [@abd, 5:426–427]

In 2 Kgs 20:18 Isaiah warns that some of Hezekiah's descendants will serve as eunuchs in the Babylonian palace. Dan 1:3–18 places Daniel and other Judean youths under the chief of the eunuchs and shows their preparation for royal service. Reading Daniel with Isaiah's warning led some Jewish and Christian interpreters to regard Daniel as a eunuch. Daniel's own status remains unstated in the narrative. [@abd, 1:769–770]

## Interpretive Significance

Deut 23:1 excludes a male with specified genital injuries from the assembly of the Lord. Isa 56:3–5 promises faithful eunuchs a lasting name within God's house, using the image of a dry tree to express anxiety about descendants. The promise addresses the loss that exclusion and childlessness could bring. Matt 19:12 distinguishes eunuchs from birth, those made eunuchs by others and those who become so for the kingdom. Acts 8:27 presents a eunuch who administers the treasury of the queen of Cush and has traveled to Jerusalem to worship. The combination of political responsibility and religious participation recalls both the courts and the questions of belonging attested in earlier sources. [@abd, 2:667–668]
