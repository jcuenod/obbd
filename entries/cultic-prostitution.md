## Overview

Older studies of ancient Near Eastern religion often described “sacred prostitution” as an institution in which temples required or organized sexual acts. They drew chiefly on Greek accounts and on Hebrew words associated with both worship and prostitution. Modern studies of administrative and legal texts have weakened this reconstruction for Mesopotamia and the Levant. The evidence identifies several kinds of religious personnel, while their duties vary and are often unclear. This distinction matters for readings of Genesis, Hosea, Deuteronomy, and Kings, where English translations sometimes identify people as shrine prostitutes.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian laws refer to women known as *qadištu*, a category with religious and social associations. Some texts connect women bearing this title with tasks such as nursing children. The term by itself gives no evidence of ritual intercourse. Related words among the [[ugaritic-texts|Ugaritic texts]] likewise designate personnel connected with worship, with little information about their particular duties. Similar word forms across languages help identify a field of meaning, but they do not establish an identical office or practice in every setting. [@roth-law, 109] [@ktu3, 4.29:1–4, 4.36:1–4, 4.38:1–3]

Herodotus describes Babylonian women having intercourse with strangers in a religious setting. His account, written for a Greek audience in the fifth century BCE, became a principal basis for the older reconstruction. Later Greek writers such as Strabo and Lucian have also been used in support of the idea. Their accounts refer to different settings and traditions. Scholars now question how much of this evidence reflects local institutions as opposed to an outsider's interpretation, literary convention, or confusion about a practice. The surviving local records do not corroborate a Babylonian obligation of the kind Herodotus describes.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 38:15, 21–22, Judah takes Tamar to be a prostitute, while his companion enquires after the woman using another Hebrew word, *qĕdēšâ*. The two expressions refer to the same person within the story. The episode describes negotiations for sex, but no sanctuary or temple office is part of the scene. Deut 23:17–18 (MT 23:18–19, using the Hebrew verse numbering) forbids Israelites to take on the roles described by *qĕdēšâ* and the related masculine *qādēš*, and it forbids certain payments to be brought into the sanctuary. The two rules help explain how the terms came to be read together. [@abd, 5:505–513]

Hos 4:13–14 places consorting with prostitutes beside sacrificing with women called *qĕdēšôt*, the plural of *qĕdēšâ*. Hosea condemns the men's conduct and does not describe the duties of the women. In 2 Kgs 23:7, Josiah removes quarters connected with men designated by the related masculine term from the temple area, where women also made woven objects for the [[asherah|asherah]]. The text links the personnel to a worship setting without specifying sexual services. [@ddd] [@abd, 5:505–513]

## Interpretive Significance

Some English translations retain “temple prostitute” or “shrine prostitute,” reflecting the older interpretation. Others use a broader expression or leave the institutional role open. The objections to the older interpretation concern the evidence for an organized practice. Biblical writers can associate sexual conduct and unacceptable worship in the same argument, and that association may reflect social practices, rhetorical choices, or both. The separate phenomenon conventionally called [[sacred-marriage|sacred marriage]] has its own texts and requires its own assessment.
