## Overview

The Hyksos were the rulers of Egypt's Fifteenth Dynasty. They governed from Avaris in the eastern Nile Delta during the Second Intermediate Period and were defeated by Ahmose near the beginning of the New Kingdom, around 1550 BCE. Archaeology shows strong Levantine connections and immigration to the Delta before their rise. Ancient and modern authors have compared their rule and expulsion with the Joseph and exodus traditions. The comparison illustrates the range of relations between Egypt and its Levantine neighbors, while the connection to any biblical event remains disputed.

## Sources

The name Hyksos comes from Egyptian *ḥqꜣw ḫꜣswt*, “rulers of foreign lands.” It refers to rulers, although later writers sometimes used it for a whole people. Egyptian evidence includes royal names, accounts of conflict from the Theban side, and the tomb biography of Ahmose son of Ebana, who describes service in the campaign against Avaris. The Egyptian historian Manetho's much later account survives through authors including [[josephus|Josephus]]. In *Against Apion* 1.73–105, Josephus reproduces the explanation “shepherd kings,” which reflects an ancient misinterpretation of the Egyptian title [@ael, 2:12–15] [@anet, 230–234, 554–555].

## History

Manfred Bietak's excavations at [[avaris|Tell el-Dabʿa]] uncovered the Hyksos capital and earlier phases of settlement. Buildings, pottery, and burial practices show strong connections with the Middle Bronze Age Levant alongside Egyptian traditions. The material changes over generations, and Levantine-related settlement precedes the dynasty. A study of strontium isotopes in teeth identified numerous people who had grown up outside their burial region. It supports mobility into the eastern Delta before the Hyksos came to power. The measurements do not determine the ethnicity or precise place of origin of each person.

The fifteenth-dynasty rulers controlled northern Egypt from Avaris during an era of competing political centers. The Theban kings eventually attacked, and Ahmose captured Avaris. Egyptian accounts describe the defeat from the victor's perspective. Much later Egypt developed the Ramesside capital of [[pi-ramesses|Pi-Ramesses]] beside the former Hyksos center, demonstrating the sustained importance of the region.

## In the Bible

In Gen 41:39–43, Joseph rises to a position of authority at the Egyptian court. Gen 47:11 has his family settle in the land of Rameses. The eastern Delta is a recognizable setting for a story of people from the Levant settling in Egypt, and its history of immigration spans several periods. Some readers associate the new king of Exod 1:8 with a change of dynasty after the Hyksos. The verse leaves the king unnamed. Exod 1:11 refers to Raamses, a name usually associated with the later Pi-Ramesses. The persistence of the place and changes in its name are important to the comparison.

Josephus associates the Hyksos expulsion with Israel's departure. Some modern scholars, including Donald Redford, have considered whether cultural memories of Hyksos-era conflicts contributed to [[exodus-historical-questions|the exodus tradition]]. Others see the chronological and narrative differences as reasons to leave the connection unestablished. An Egyptian account of military defeat in the sixteenth century BCE, a Ramesside place name, and biblical narratives of deliverance are different types of evidence. The archaeology of Avaris supplies context for Levantine settlement in Egypt without identifying Joseph or Israel among the dynasty's officials or subjects.
