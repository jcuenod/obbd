## Overview

Nehushtan is the name given in Kings to a bronze serpent that received offerings in Judah and was destroyed by Hezekiah. The text associates it with the serpent Moses made in the wilderness to protect people who had been bitten. Small metal serpents from archaeological sites show that such images were familiar in the southern Levant. The biblical stories give the image different roles in healing, worship, and reform. Later Jewish and Christian writers use the wilderness episode to reflect on how God saves.

## Archaeological Evidence

Metal serpents have been recovered at several sites in the southern Levant, including Megiddo and Gezer. A copper snake with traces of gilding was found in the shrine near the mining area at [[timna|Timna]]. Beno Rothenberg associated it with a phase of local worship following the shrine's Egyptian phase. The identification of that local phase with Midianites is a proposal about the site's users. The object itself attests that a metal serpent could be placed in a sanctuary in the southern Arabah.

Snake images can carry associations of danger, protection, healing, or divine presence. Their significance in a particular case depends on context and accompanying evidence. The Timna find offers a close material comparison because of its medium and sanctuary setting. Comparing objects of this kind helps explain the material plausibility of the biblical image while leaving each object's purpose open.

## In the Bible

In Num 21:4–9 venomous serpents strike the people in the wilderness. At God's direction, Moses makes a serpent and puts it on a pole. Those who look at it after being bitten live. The Hebrew pairs *nāḥāš*, “serpent,” with *nĕḥōšet*, “copper” or “bronze.” The wordplay carries over to the related form Nehushtan.

2 Kgs 18:4 says that the Israelites were making offerings to the serpent that Moses had made and that [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] broke it during his reforms. Nehushtan could be an established name or a designation intended to reduce the serpent to its material. The account places the act alongside the removal of other objects and settings of worship. The scale and archaeological visibility of the changes attributed to Hezekiah remain debated. See [[cult-reforms-archaeology|Cult Reforms and Archaeology]].

Some scholars read the wilderness story as an explanation of how a known cult object acquired a Mosaic origin. The story itself describes a response to immediate danger, and Kings then presents the care given to the object as inappropriate worship. Wis 16:5–7 treats it as a sign of deliverance and attributes healing to God. John 3:14–15 compares the lifting up of the Son of Man with Moses lifting the serpent. These readings pick up the act of looking or the lifting of the object and give it new significance.
