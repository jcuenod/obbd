## Overview

The Weld-Blundell Prism is a clay copy of the Sumerian King List from about 1800 BCE. It lists kings and cities from the age before the flood through the dynasty of Isin. Several early reigns last tens of thousands of years in its reckoning. The text organizes a legendary and historical past around the transfer of kingship. Its long-lived early rulers and the flood as a marker between ages offer comparisons with the genealogies of Genesis, while the two works organize their characters for different purposes.

## Description and Provenance

The object is a four-sided prism inscribed in Sumerian cuneiform, now in the Ashmolean Museum. The museum dates it to around 1800 BCE and lists Larsa as its probable origin. It lacks a controlled excavation context. Herbert Weld-Blundell presented it to the museum in 1923. The object is conventionally called WB 444, and its current accession number is AN1923.444. Its date belongs to the manuscript and does not establish when every part of the composition was first assembled.

## Contents

The prism begins with kingship descending from heaven. It places eight kings in several cities before the flood and assigns them reigns far beyond ordinary human lifetimes. After the flood, kingship again descends from heaven. The text proceeds through sequences of cities and rulers, ending in the period of Isin. The extreme figures mark an early age as different from recent political history, though durations remain schematic in parts of the later list as well.

Other witnesses do not always give the same sequence. The text known as WB 62 gives a list with ten rulers before the flood. The differences matter for the history of the [[sumerian-king-list|king list]] and caution against treating the prism as the only form used by ancient scribes. The antiquity of kingship and its movement among cities are central to the work’s political structure.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 5:1–32 names a succession of figures whose lifetimes greatly exceed those familiar to most readers. Gen 6:5–9:17 makes the flood a decisive division, and Gen 11:10–26 gives a genealogy for the generations that follow. The prism offers a comparison for that broad structure. Its figures are kings whose reigns are counted, while Genesis traces a family and gives ages and lifetimes. The two series do not have the same names or durations.

Gen 4:17 connects the early generations with a city. The prism makes cities central as successive seats of kingship. These uses of urban places help define the purpose of each work. The ten rulers of WB 62 and the ten generations commonly counted in Genesis 5 are worth comparing. The presence of eight in WB 444 shows why agreement in number cannot establish a direct relationship.

## Scholarly Issues

Several versions of the list show that scribes adapted its ordering and scope. Some scholars regard the section before the flood as a secondary addition to an earlier form. The underlying idea of one transferable kingship gives the work a political coherence that does not require every dynasty to have ruled successively in history. The prism is evidence for a way of organizing that history and assigning legitimacy, even where the chronology represents an idealized past.
