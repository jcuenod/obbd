## Overview

Hemerologies are texts that classify days by the consequences of taking particular actions. Menologies organize comparable guidance by month. Mesopotamian examples prescribe when a king, diviner, or physician should act or refrain, and Egyptian examples relate favorable and dangerous days to events in the lives of gods. These sources help explain why choosing a date could matter in the ancient world. They have also figured in proposals about the origin of the biblical Sabbath, although the calendar rules and purposes of the two institutions differ.

## Manuscripts and Date

Mesopotamian hemerologies survive on clay tablets in multiple copies and arrangements. They are attested from the later second millennium BCE into the first millennium, with a substantial body of Neo-Assyrian material from Nineveh. Discoveries from other sites and later copies show the continuing work of scholars who transmitted and reorganized the material. The [[library-of-ashurbanipal|library collections at Nineveh]] are an important witness to that transmission, particularly for guidance framed around the king.

The Egyptian Cairo Calendar, Papyrus Cairo JE 86637, is a New Kingdom witness to a related kind of practice in a distinct textual tradition. Its daily entries can divide the day into parts and give an assessment associated with mythical events. These assignments belong to an Egyptian calendrical and religious setting. The resemblance in purpose leaves the transmission history of the Egyptian and Mesopotamian traditions an independent question.

## Contents

Some lists assign the calendar’s days favorable or unfavorable conditions for food, travel, offerings, medical work, official decisions, and other acts. A frequently discussed group of Mesopotamian days includes the 7th, 14th, 19th, 21st, and 28th of certain months. Restrictions may forbid the king particular foods or a change of clothing, the diviner an official pronouncement, or the physician treatment. These entries specify actions and officeholders. Other lists use different sets of days or give a warning together with ritual instructions. The inclusion of the nineteenth day has been explained by counting it as the forty-ninth day from the start of an idealized thirty-day preceding month. The explanation is a modern inference from the pattern.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Esth 3:7 Haman has a *pur*, or lot, cast to determine the timing of his action. This is an example of [[lots-and-cleromancy|lot casting]], a way of selecting a date that differs from consulting a day list. Both practices assume that time can be evaluated for action. Lev 19:26 and Deut 18:10, 14 forbid a form of [[divination|divination]] designated by the Hebrew root *ʿnn*. A traditional English rendering connects it with observing times, but the precise practice and any connection to calendar lists are uncertain.

Job 3:1–10 curses the day of Job’s birth and calls for the night of his conception to be excluded from the reckoning of time. A calendar assigning days characteristics provides an intelligible comparison for the poem. The curse functions as a wish for erasure and need not presuppose a handed-down classification of auspicious days.

## Scholarly Issues

Exod 20:8–11 commands cessation from labor every seventh day for the household and those within its sphere, grounding the practice in the creation week. Mesopotamian taboos on selected days have prompted comparison with the [[sabbath|Sabbath]], particularly because several dates are multiples of seven. The hemerological restrictions operate by numbered days of a month, apply in specific ways to different people, and include dates outside the sequence of multiples of seven. The biblical command follows a recurring week. A shared interest in structuring time is evident, but the sources do not establish a line of descent.
