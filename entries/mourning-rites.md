## Overview

Mourning in the Hebrew Bible is expressed through actions that make loss visible and audible. Mourners tear garments, wear sackcloth, put dust on their heads, fast, weep, or sing laments. Some passages also refer to shaving and cutting the skin. Texts from Ugarit and images from the Levant illustrate related practices. Biblical writers use these actions to describe private grief, public responses to disaster, and appeals for divine help. Some laws set limits on what the community may do in mourning for the dead.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In a scene from the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]] (KTU 1.5 VI 11–25), El hears that Baal is dead and descends from his throne. He sits on the ground, puts dust on his head, and cuts himself in grief. Anat also mourns Baal. The poem gives gods recognizable human responses to death and uses the extremity of the gestures to convey the size of the loss. Similar motifs occur in other Ugaritic literature, including the story of Aqhat.

Sculpted figures on one side of the [[ahiram-sarcophagus|Ahiram sarcophagus]] raise their hands to their heads. They are generally interpreted as mourning women. Images like these supply a complementary kind of evidence to a description in a poem. They show how grief could be represented through posture, clothing, and groups of participants. Such representations can commemorate a ceremony while also following established artistic conventions.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Jacob tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth, and mourns when he believes Joseph has died (Gen 37:34). Gen 50:10 describes Joseph's seven days of mourning for Jacob at the threshing floor of Atad. David and his men tear their clothes, weep, and fast after hearing of Saul and Jonathan's deaths (2 Sam 1:11–12). The response to Abner's death includes sackcloth, a procession behind the bier, a lament, and abstention from food (2 Sam 3:31–35). Public displays of grief could express the relationship between a leader and the people for whom he mourned.

Job's companions weep, tear their robes, scatter dust, and sit with him in silence in Job 2:12–13. Isa 15:2–3 describes baldness, shaved beards, and sackcloth in the grief of Moab's towns. Amos 8:10 turns festive occasions into mourning and compares the loss with the death of an only child. Jeremiah summons skilled mourning women and tells them to teach their daughters a lament (Jer 9:17–20, MT 9:16–19). Such women could give a public voice to grief that affected an entire community.

## Interpretive Significance

Deut 14:1 prohibits cutting the skin and shaving the forehead for the dead. Lev 19:27–28 restricts certain shaving practices and prohibits cuts for the dead. Yet Jer 16:6–7 assumes readers would recognize cutting, baldness, and the provision of food and drink for mourners. Jer 41:5 describes pilgrims with shaved beards, torn clothes, and cuts. The coexistence of laws and narratives shows that acts recognizable within Israel and Judah could also be prohibited as part of communal religious discipline.

In Ezek 24:15–24, Ezekiel's wife dies and he is instructed to refrain from customary rites, including the gestures and food expected in ordinary grief. The suspension of familiar practice gives the prophetic sign its force. Mourning also extends beyond a death to circumstances of petition, penitence, and disaster. Its actions can express solidarity, distress, or an appeal, depending on who performs them and in what setting.
