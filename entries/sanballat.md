## Overview

Sanballat was a governor of Samaria in the late fifth century BCE and is usually identified with Sanballat the Horonite, Nehemiah's principal opponent. The Elephantine correspondence attests the governor's name and names two sons. Later records from Wadi ed-Daliyeh have led scholars to propose that a family linked to Sanballat held office for several generations. The certain evidence helps locate Nehemiah's conflicts within the politics of neighboring Persian provinces. The number of later governors named Sanballat remains disputed.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The petition sent from Elephantine in 407 BCE to the governor of Judah mentions sending the same account to Delaiah and Shelemiah, sons of Sanballat, governor of [[samaria|Samaria]]. A related memorandum associates Delaiah with a response about rebuilding the temple of YHW. Sanballat's name reflects Akkadian *Sîn-uballiṭ*, “Sîn has kept alive.” The names of his sons contain a form of the name of YHWH. Such names show how different naming traditions could occur in a single family and are consistent with ties to northern Yahwistic practice. [@tad, A4.7–A4.9]

A sealing among the [[samaria-papyri|Wadi ed-Daliyeh finds]] bears a fragmentary inscription commonly read with the name Sanballat and the title governor of Samaria. The reading of the personal name preceding the patronymic and the identification of the governor are debated. The find provides a possible connection between the family named at Elephantine and the later provincial administration. [@avigad-sass-wsss]

## Biblical Portrayal

In Neh 2:10, 19, Sanballat the Horonite resents Nehemiah's mission and joins others in questioning whether the rebuilding represents rebellion. Neh 4:1–8 (MT 3:33–4:2) describes mockery before the force of Samaria and a plot to attack Jerusalem. In Neh 6:1–14, invitations to meet in the plain of Ono are followed by an open letter alleging royal ambitions, and Nehemiah reports that Sanballat and Tobiah hired Shemaiah. The book presents these actions from Nehemiah's perspective. [@abd]

Neh 13:28 adds that a grandson of the high priest Eliashib married a daughter of Sanballat and that Nehemiah drove him away. The episode shows how marriages could connect influential families across provincial boundaries despite political tensions. The designation Horonite has been connected with Beth-horon, the Hauran and Horonaim. Its reference remains uncertain. [@abd]

## Historical Assessment

The Elephantine letter confirms a governor of the right name, place and general period for identification with Nehemiah's opponent. Josephus (*Antiquities* 11.302–347) places a Sanballat in the age of Darius III and Alexander and assigns him the foundation of the [[mount-gerizim|Gerizim sanctuary]]. Frank Moore Cross proposed that the name passed down across generations, allowing several holders of the office. Other scholars question whether the later sources establish those individuals. A Persian-period date for the earliest sanctuary, as proposed by its excavators, also complicates Josephus's reconstruction. [@tad, A4.7] [@abd]
