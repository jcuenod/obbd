## Overview

The Temple Mount, also known as Haram al-Sharif, is the elevated precinct in Jerusalem associated with the First and Second Temples. Herod’s expansion gave much of the platform its surviving outline. Biblical traditions connect the hill with Solomon, David’s altar, and the land of Moriah, while the name Zion changed its geographical application over time. Excavated approaches and visible retaining walls give substantial evidence for the late Second Temple precinct. The precise location and design of the earlier sanctuary remain archaeologically unconfirmed.

## Location and Identification

The platform stands north of the southeastern ridge associated with the [[city-of-david|City of David]]. Valleys and slopes around the hill helped determine how it could be enlarged and approached. Temple Mount and Haram al-Sharif are names used in different historical and religious settings for the precinct. The present complex contains buildings and places of worship from later periods, while the surviving perimeter incorporates masonry associated with Herod’s extension.

The name Zion first appears in 2 Sam 5:7 for the stronghold that became the City of David. Later writings associate Zion with the sanctuary or use it more broadly for Jerusalem. In still later tradition the name was applied to part of the western hill. The same word can therefore refer to different areas depending on the period and text.

## Archaeological History

Much is known about the platform’s retaining structures and entrances from surveys and excavations around its edges. Monumental walls, gates, stairs, and street levels belong to the setting of [[herods-temple|Herod’s Temple]]. Scholars have proposed earlier platforms based on seams and alignments in the visible masonry. Such proposals include a square pre-Herodian enclosure, but the form and date of those phases are disputed. Visible masonry cannot by itself identify the earlier sanctuary’s plan.

There has been no controlled stratigraphic excavation of the sanctuary area on the platform. Material recovered by sifting displaced soils can establish the presence of artifacts from different periods in those deposits. Because the soil is mixed and removed from its original setting, individual finds ordinarily cannot locate a building or date a floor. The limitations are most consequential for claims about the First Temple.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 22:2 places Abraham’s journey in the land of Moriah without naming Jerusalem. Second Samuel 24:18–25 associates a city altar with Araunah’s threshing floor. Second Chronicles 3:1 brings these traditions together by naming Mount Moriah and the threshing floor in its account of Solomon’s project. First Kings 6 describes the temple building without the same geographical explanation. The explicit link in Chronicles is evidence for biblical interpretation of the site as well as a claim about its history.

Ps 2:6 and Isa 2:2–4 use Zion and the mountain of God’s house in royal and prophetic language. Ezra 3:8–13 depicts laying foundations for the restored temple, and Mark 13:1–2 draws attention to the scale and future destruction of the late Second Temple buildings. The visible retaining walls give context for their monumentality. They are not themselves the sanctuary whose destruction the Gospel foretells.
