## Overview

Pithom is one of the two store cities that Exodus attributes to Israelite forced labor. The name corresponds to Egyptian *Per-Atum*, “House of Atum.” Egyptian records place a site of that name in the Wadi Tumilat, an eastern approach to the Nile Delta. Excavations distinguish a Ramesside fortress at Tell el-Retabeh from a major later settlement at Tell el-Maskhuta. Both have been proposed as the biblical Pithom. The site chosen affects how scholars reconstruct the geography of Exodus, but neither identifies its laborers.

## Location and Identification

The Wadi Tumilat links the eastern Delta to routes leading toward the Sinai. The low corridor carried water and traffic, with settlements at Tell er-Retabeh and Tell el-Maskhuta along it. Egyptian *Per-Atum* can name a sanctuary or the place associated with it. [[papyrus-anastasi|Papyrus Anastasi VI]] refers to the pools of the Per-Atum of Tjeku and describes admitting pastoral groups to that area. The text gives New Kingdom evidence for the names and their relationship without placing the sanctuary on a modern map.

## Archaeological History

Édouard Naville excavated at Tell el-Maskhuta in 1883 and identified it with Pithom. Inscriptions naming Per-Atum favored the association, and substantial buildings there were interpreted as storage structures. Later excavations directed by John Holladay identified occupation in the Middle Bronze Age and substantial new development in the Saite period, around the end of the seventh century BCE. The intervening evidence gives little support for a Ramesside city at the site. The settlement’s later growth may relate to work on navigation under [[necho-ii|Necho II]].

Tell er-Retabeh, farther west in the wadi, preserves a fortified Ramesside settlement. Polish and Slovak excavations have refined its sequence and documented the settlement that developed over earlier defenses. Its chronology makes it a plausible candidate for the location named in the Ramesside documents. One proposal is that the designation moved east as settlement and administration shifted toward Tell el-Maskhuta. This would allow evidence from the two sites to describe different periods in the history of the same regional name. The actual transfer is a reconstruction.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 1:11 names Pithom along with [[pi-ramesses|Raamses]] and describes both as store cities. The two names have plausible Egyptian contexts, but their appearance together can be explained in more than one way. Some scholars take them as preserving a Ramesside setting, with Pithom located at Tell er-Retabeh. Others understand the mention of Pithom as reflecting the first-millennium geography in which Tell el-Maskhuta was important. If the name was used for more than one site over time, it supplies no simple date for the written narrative.

Exod 12:37 presents Succoth as the first destination after leaving Rameses. Connecting Succoth with Egyptian Tjeku is possible, but Tjeku may designate a district as well as a specific place. Gen 46:28–29 places the journey of Jacob’s household in the region of Goshen. Topography and the Egyptian frontier reports make this broad setting intelligible. They do not fix the route of the biblical travelers or prove a historical connection with the individual settlements excavated.
