## Overview

A threshing floor was a cleared surface where harvested grain was separated from stalks and husks. Threshing could involve beating the stalks, driving animals over them, or drawing a toothed sledge. Winnowing then used moving air to separate lighter material from the grain. The space could also serve as a place of assembly, encounter, or ritual. Biblical writers draw on its practical work and public setting in stories, laws, and images of judgment. Ugaritic literature supplies a comparison for its civic role.

## Archaeological Evidence

Threshing floors could be made of hard-packed soil or prepared rock and were often open to breezes. Their simple surfaces can be difficult to identify in excavation. Later ethnographic accounts and archaeological remains of implements help explain the work but need to be distinguished from direct evidence about a particular ancient floor. A threshing sledge could have sharp stones set in a wooden underside. As it passed across the harvested plants the stones broke up the stalks and freed the grain.

After threshing, workers tossed the mixture with a fork or shovel so air could carry away lighter chaff. The heavy grain fell for collection before storage and eventual grinding. Different crops could require different equipment and amounts of force. The open area that provided room for work could also accommodate meetings or ceremonies outside the threshing season.

## In the Bible

Judg 6:37 places Gideon's fleece on a threshing floor. Ruth 3:2–14 shows Boaz winnowing barley and sleeping there when Ruth approaches him. The story places the encounter near stored produce and at night, circumstances that make Ruth vulnerable and raise questions about the scene's sexual overtones. Deut 25:4 forbids muzzling an ox while it treads grain. Isa 28:27–28 compares the use of various tools and degrees of force for different crops.

In the [[aqhat-epic|Aqhat Epic]] Danel sits at the gate, in a passage that associates the place with a threshing floor, and hears the cases of widows and orphans (KTU 1.17 V). In 1 Kgs 22:10 the kings of Israel and Judah sit near the gate of Samaria at a threshing floor to hear the prophets. Both scenes use the open space for matters of public concern. In 2 Sam 24:18–25 David buys Araunah's threshing floor and builds an altar. 2 Chr 3:1 locates the later [[temple-mount|temple]] at the floor of Ornan on Mount Moriah.

Hos 9:1 denounces Israel's love of illicit payment on threshing floors. Scholars have understood the imagery in several ways, including a metaphor for unfaithful worship associated with harvest. In Isa 41:15 Israel becomes a sharp threshing sledge, and in Mic 4:12–13 nations are gathered like sheaves for Zion to thresh. In Matt 3:12 John the Baptist uses the separation of grain and chaff to picture judgment. These passages derive their force from familiar agricultural processes.
