## Overview

Nimrud is the modern name of Kalhu, a city on the Tigris that became the Assyrian capital under Ashurnasirpal II in the ninth century BCE. Its palaces, temples, ivories, and royal tombs document the wealth and organization of the empire. Several monuments from the site name rulers of Israel and describe their dealings with Assyria. They provide independent evidence for episodes otherwise known mainly from the Bible. Finds brought from the western provinces also illuminate the luxury goods and artistic traditions of the Iron Age Levant.

## Location and Identification

Kalhu lay south of ancient Nineveh in northern Iraq. It is commonly identified with Calah, one of the cities listed in Gen 10:11–12. The passage can be read as attributing its construction to Nimrod or to Asshur. The modern name Nimrud reflects a later identification. The Assyrian name and the ancient city's history are attested by inscriptions.

## Archaeological History

Ashurnasirpal II rebuilt Kalhu and made it his royal center. A stele describing a great banquet marks the inauguration, commonly placed around 879 BCE. The Northwest Palace contained reception rooms lined with carved slabs and gateways guarded by large human-headed beasts. Other major buildings include the temple of Nabû and Fort Shalmaneser, a palace and arsenal where excavators found large groups of ivory objects.

Austen Henry Layard directed nineteenth-century excavations, and Max Mallowan led later work that greatly expanded knowledge of the ivories and architecture. Iraqi excavations from 1988 onward discovered royal women's burials beneath palace floors, including tombs with gold jewelry and other furnishings. These materials document court burial practices as well as craft and trade. In 2015 ISIS deliberately damaged the site and destroyed much of the Northwest Palace.

The ivories include forms assigned to Phoenician, North Syrian, and Assyrian workshops or styles. Some may have reached the capital by tribute or booty, while individual objects can have different histories. Their combination of uses and styles shows how objects and craft traditions moved among the regions of the empire.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The [[black-obelisk|Black Obelisk]] from Kalhu depicts and labels tribute from Jehu's realm. 2 Kgs 9:1–13 describes [[jehu|Jehu's]] accession, and the monument shows that his reign involved submission to Assyria. 2 Kgs 15:19–20 records Menahem's tribute to a ruler called Pul. Inscriptions of [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] from Kalhu independently name Menahem among tributaries.

The ivories provide comparisons for Ahab's ivory house (1 Kgs 22:39) and the beds of ivory censured in Amos 6:4. Ornament inlaid with carved plaques helps explain how such furnishings acquired their appearance and social meaning. The [[samaria-ivories|ivories excavated at Samaria]] supply the closer local evidence. Paint and carved figures in Mesopotamian palaces also provide background for Ezek 23:14–15, which describes Chaldean men portrayed in red on a wall. The prophetic text evokes the appeal of imperial images in a setting of its own.
