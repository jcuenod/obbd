## Overview

The Egyptian *Tale of Two Brothers* survives on a papyrus copied near the end of the Nineteenth Dynasty. Anubis and his younger brother Bata live and work together until Anubis's wife tries to seduce Bata and falsely accuses him. The resulting rupture leads into a story of separation, death, transformation, and accession to kingship. The opening has long been compared with Joseph's encounter with Potiphar's wife in Genesis. Both episodes turn on the vulnerability of a dependent young man who refuses a household superior's advances.

## Manuscripts and Date

Papyrus D'Orbiney (British Museum, EA 10183) preserves the tale in Late Egyptian, written in hieratic script. Its colophon names the scribe Inena, and the manuscript bears references associating it with the late Nineteenth Dynasty, around the end of the thirteenth century BCE. This dates the surviving copy. How long the story or its constituent episodes circulated before it was copied remains uncertain. The names Anubis and Bata correspond to Egyptian gods, and the narrative puts them in a recognizable farming household before taking them through a series of extraordinary adventures [@ael, 2:203–211; @anet, 23–25; @cos1, 1.40].

## Contents

Bata tends livestock and helps Anubis work the fields. When he goes to the house for seed, Anubis's wife propositions him. Bata refuses, calling on the relationship between the brothers and the respect he owes the household. The woman acts as though she has been assaulted and tells Anubis that Bata was the aggressor. Anubis waits for his brother with intent to kill. Warned by the cattle, Bata flees and is separated from Anubis by water. When he hears Bata's account, Anubis returns and kills his wife.

The later part follows Bata to the Valley of the Cedar, where he places his heart on a tree. His new wife enters Pharaoh's household and brings about the tree's destruction. Anubis restores Bata by recovering the heart. Bata takes further forms, including a bull and trees. A splinter enters the woman's mouth, she bears a son, and Bata eventually becomes king. The brothers' reconciliation and Bata's rise extend the tale beyond the household dispute.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Gen 39:7–20, Potiphar's wife repeatedly solicits Joseph. He refuses on grounds of loyalty to his master and accountability to God. Joseph flees when she catches his garment, and she presents the garment when she tells the household and her husband that Joseph attacked her. The Egyptian episode also gives the woman a way to support the accusation, but she relies on a staged appearance of injury. Both stories use unequal status to make the accusation dangerous. The Egyptian accusation produces flight and a confrontation between brothers. Genesis brings Joseph to prison and continues its account of how imprisonment eventually leads to public office.

## Scholarly Issues

The surviving Egyptian manuscript considerably predates the extant manuscripts of Genesis. The similar opening plot has prompted proposals of contact, but dates of manuscripts establish neither a route of transmission nor the direction of borrowing between particular forms of the story. Both episodes can also be examined as examples of a wider tale pattern. Their distinct sequels and social relationships matter as much as the common motif.
