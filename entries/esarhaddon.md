## Overview

Esarhaddon succeeded his father Sennacherib as king of Assyria after a struggle over the throne in 681 BCE. During his reign he restored Babylon, captured Memphis in Egypt and imposed oaths intended to secure the succession of his son Ashurbanipal. His inscriptions name Manasseh of Judah among rulers who owed service to Assyria. The records of the reign help place biblical notices of Judah under Manasseh in an imperial context and provide comparisons for biblical language about loyalty and prophecy.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[esarhaddon-prisms|royal prisms]] describe Esarhaddon's designation as heir and the resistance he faced from his brothers. A Babylonian chronicle separately reports Sennacherib's murder by a son. After taking the throne, Esarhaddon undertook building works at Babylon, which his father had destroyed. His inscriptions also record the capture of Memphis in 671 BCE during war with the Kushite pharaoh [[taharqa|Taharqa]]. The occupation did not establish uninterrupted Assyrian control over Egypt. [@rinap4] [@abc]

Letters, queries to gods and prophetic messages give unusually detailed evidence for the reign. The texts show the court seeking information about political dangers, military decisions and the king's well-being. Some of the [[neo-assyrian-prophecies|prophetic oracles]] speak in a deity's voice to reassure him. They help readers see the range of aims and forms that could belong to prophecy at an ancient court. [@saa9] [@nissinen-prophets]

## Biblical Portrayal

Second Kings 19:37 names Esarhaddon as Sennacherib's successor after reporting that two men killed his father. The broad sequence agrees with the external evidence, while details of the killing and succession vary among the accounts. Second Kings 21:1–18 describes Manasseh's reign chiefly in terms of worship and wrongdoing. The Assyrian inscription identifies him as a western king owing service to Esarhaddon and gives a view of his position from the imperial side. [@rinap4] [@abc]

In Ezra 4:2, people seeking to help rebuild the Jerusalem temple say that Esarhaddon brought them into the land. Assyrian rulers did resettle communities, so the claim is plausible within broader imperial practice. The particular action attributed to Esarhaddon in the verse has no clear external confirmation. Isa 19:4 speaks of Egypt being given into the power of a harsh king. Esarhaddon has been proposed as the ruler in view, although the verse does not name the king. [@abd] [@rinap4]

## Historical Assessment

The [[esarhaddon-succession-treaty|succession oath]] of 672 BCE bound subjects to support Ashurbanipal, while another son, Shamash-shum-ukin, was designated to rule Babylon. A copy of the oath from Tell Tayinat documents its use in the west. The curses have been compared with Deut 28:23 and other passages in Deuteronomy. These parallels set biblical covenant language in a world of imperial demands, while the specific claim that Judean writers used the oath directly remains debated. [@saa2] [@rinap4]
