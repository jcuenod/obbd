## Overview

Cherubim are winged beings that guard sacred places, accompany the throne of Israel's God and appear in sanctuary decoration. The Hebrew Bible places them at the garden of Eden, above the ark and in Ezekiel's visions. Art from the Late Bronze and Iron Age Levant depicts comparable creatures with wings, a human head and an animal body. These images help readers understand why biblical cherubim belong at a sacred boundary or near a king. The biblical accounts themselves give the creatures more than one form.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

A likely visual model for some biblical cherubim is the winged sphinx, ordinarily a creature with a human head and the body of a lion. Examples occur in Levantine art, including the [[ahiram-sarcophagus|sarcophagus of Ahiram]] at Byblos and ivory carvings from Megiddo and [[samaria-ivories|Samaria]]. Such creatures can flank thrones or appear in decorative forms. Their royal and protective associations offer a basis for comparison with biblical descriptions. [@anep, figs. 129–130] [@keel-symbolism, 168–171] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 94–95, 167–169]

Ancient artists also devised other winged guardians. Assyrian [[lamassu]], for instance, could combine a human head with a bull or lion body and guard a palace entrance. Scholars sometimes connect the Hebrew *kərūb* (“cherub”) with Akkadian terms such as *kāribu*, associated with blessing or intercession. The proposed derivation and the meaning of the related terms remain debated. [@ddd, 189–192]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 3:24 places cherubim east of the [[garden-of-god|garden of Eden]] to guard access to the tree of life. Exod 25:18–22 describes two gold cherubim facing one another above the ark and spreading their wings over its cover. In 1 Kgs 6:23–28, two large carved figures spread their wings across the inner room of the temple. In these accounts, the figures mark a place that belongs to God or define the space associated with divine presence.

The association with a [[cherub-throne|divine throne]] appears when 1 Sam 4:4 describes Yahweh as seated above the cherubim. Ps 18:10 (MT 18:11) pictures Yahweh riding on a cherub. Ezekiel's vision gives the idea of movement a fuller description. The four-faced, four-winged creatures seen at the start of the book (Ezek 1:5–14) are identified as cherubim in Ezek 10:1–22, where they accompany the divine glory. Ezek 41:18–20 describes cherubim with two faces in the decoration of the envisioned temple.

## Interpretive Significance

Ezekiel's [[ezekiel-throne-chariot|throne vision]] has been compared with several types of Mesopotamian art, including winged guardians and figures holding up a throne or platform. No single visual model accounts for all the details. The sphinx comparisons are strongest for the royal and guardian roles, while Ezekiel develops those roles into an elaborate vision. The plump winged infants known as putti in Renaissance art come from a much later tradition of representation. [@keel-symbolism, 168–171] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 304–306]
