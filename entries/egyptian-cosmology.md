## Overview

Ancient Egyptian texts describe creation with several recurring images. Land emerges from primeval water, the sky and earth are held apart, and the sun renews its journey each day. Different cities gave their own gods a central place in these accounts. Egyptian traditions are preserved in funerary compositions, hymns, and temple inscriptions from across the pharaonic period. They offer comparisons for the waters, dry land, and ordered sky of Genesis, as well as biblical references to the sun's course and to humanity as the image of God.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian sources describe *Nun*, the primeval waters, as the setting from which the ordered world came into being. In traditions associated with Heliopolis, Atum emerges and brings forth Shu and Tefnut. Their descendants include Geb, the earth, and Nut, the sky. Shu holds Nut up from Geb, giving the earth and sky their places. The *Pyramid Texts* and *Coffin Texts*, collections of funerary utterances, preserve elements of these traditions. The first dry mound was another way of imagining the beginning [@ael, 1:51–57, 1:131–133] [@anep, figs. 542–543].

The Hermopolitan tradition gives prominence to an Ogdoad, meaning a group of eight early deities in four pairs. The pairs personify features of the world before creation, such as darkness and water. The Memphite account on the [[shabaka-stone|Shabaka Stone]] gives the god Ptah a central role. It attributes creation to thought in his heart and speech by his tongue. Theban accounts give priority to Amun and sometimes make him the hidden source behind other gods and their activities. These approaches coexisted, and Egyptian writers could combine their elements [@ael, 1:51–57, 1:131–133] [@cos1, 1.14–1.16].

The sun's daily journey expresses the renewal of the created world. In accounts of its passage through the night, the serpent Apophis threatens the solar god, and the threat must be overcome repeatedly. The myth gives a comparison for conflict between order and its enemies. Its repeated daily setting differs from the founding battle of creation in the Babylonian epic Enuma Elish.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 1:2 presents darkness and waters before the ordering acts of creation. Gen 1:6–8 introduces the [[firmament|expanse]] dividing the waters, and Gen 1:9–10 exposes dry land. Egyptian traditions of the sky's separation from earth and of the primeval mound feature similar transformations. The biblical division is specifically between waters above and below, while the Egyptian image involves distinct deities embodying earth and sky. Job 26:7–11 pictures a horizon marked on the waters and the pillars of heaven trembling, further examples of poetic ways to describe the world's structure.

Ps 19:4–6 (MT 19:5–7) depicts the sun completing a circuit through the sky, with imagery drawn from a wedding and a race. Egyptian solar hymns, including the [[great-hymn-to-the-aten|Great Hymn to the Aten]], also emphasize the reach of the sun and its effects on life. In the Egyptian *Instruction for Merikare*, the creator provides for human beings, who are described as his images. This is a significant comparison with Gen 1:26–27, where humankind bears God's image [@ael, 2:96–100].

## Interpretive Significance

Comparisons with Egypt are particularly helpful for placing creation by divine speech, the primeval waters, and the image of God within ancient discussions of the world. James Hoffmeier and John Currid have emphasized Egyptian material in reading Genesis, while other interpreters give greater weight to Mesopotamian evidence. Both bodies of material offer close thematic comparisons. The use of several related images provides a setting for understanding Genesis without establishing direct dependence on a specific Egyptian text.
