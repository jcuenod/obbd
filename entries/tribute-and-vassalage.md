## Overview

Tribute was a payment made by a subordinate ruler or people to a stronger power. A vassal kept some local authority while recognizing an overlord and accepting obligations that could include regular payments, gifts, military service, or an oath. Assyrian inscriptions and biblical narratives describe several such arrangements affecting Israel and Judah. They also show how an overlord responded when a ruler withheld payment or turned to a rival. Differences between accounts can reveal what each writer wanted to emphasize about the relationship.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Assyrian documents distinguish *maddattu*, tribute, from *nāmurtu*, an audience gift. An audience gift could itself be obligatory. Other terms refer to taxes, deliveries, or other forms of payment. Assyrian rulers also used agreements called *adê* to impose obligations of loyalty. A sworn obligation gave the overlord grounds for treating a change of allegiance as oath-breaking.

The [[black-obelisk|Black Obelisk]] depicts the submission of Jehu or his representative. The Tell al-Rimah stele of Adad-nirari III names Joash of Samaria among rulers who paid tribute. Inscriptions of Tiglath-pileser III identify [[menahem|Menahem]], Ahaz under the fuller name Jehoahaz, and Hoshea in relation to Assyrian demands or intervention. The monuments combine lists of goods or people with claims about the king's authority over other rulers.

## In the Hebrew Bible

2 Sam 8:2, 6 describes Moabites and Arameans becoming David's servants and bringing tribute. 1 Kgs 4:21 (MT 5:1) places payments to Solomon in a wide vision of his rule. 2 Kgs 3:4 depicts Mesha of Moab providing sheep and wool to Israel. These texts present tribute in forms corresponding to the resources and political standing of different kingdoms.

In 2 Kgs 15:19–20 Menahem finances his payment to Pul through a levy on wealthy Israelites. In 2 Kgs 16:7–8 [[ahaz|Ahaz]] submits to Tiglath-pileser III as a servant and son and sends silver and gold from the temple and palace. 2 Kgs 17:3–4 describes Hoshea withholding tribute while appealing to Egypt. 2 Kgs 23:33–35 depicts a similar transfer of wealth to another power when Necho imposes payment on Judah and Jehoiakim taxes the land. In 2 Kgs 24:1 Jehoiakim serves Nebuchadnezzar for three years and then rebels.

## Interpretive Significance

2 Kgs 18:14–16 assigns Hezekiah a payment of thirty talents of gold and three hundred talents of silver. The [[sennacherib-annals|Assyrian annals]] give thirty talents of gold and eight hundred of silver. Different units of weight, calculations of what was included, and the aims of royal writing have all been considered in explaining this discrepancy. The sources agree that Judah made a substantial payment after the Assyrian campaign.

Hos 5:13 and 10:6 use a difficult title or expression conventionally rendered “King Jareb” or “great king.” In context the Assyrian king appears as a power courted by Israel. Ezek 17:11–21 describes a sworn agreement with Babylon and criticizes Zedekiah for breaking it. The passage shows how prophetic writers could interpret obligations between kingdoms as matters of fidelity before God.
