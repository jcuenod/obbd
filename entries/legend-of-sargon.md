## Overview

The Birth Legend of Sargon is a Mesopotamian story preserved in copies from the first millennium BCE. Speaking as the ancient king [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon of Akkad]], the narrator recounts how his mother gave birth in secret and set him on the Euphrates in a reed basket sealed with bitumen, a tar-like substance. A man drawing water rescues him, and the child eventually becomes king. The story offers a close parallel to the rescue of Moses in Exod 2:1–10, although the relationship between the two accounts is debated.

## Manuscripts and Date

The legend is written in Akkadian, a language of ancient Mesopotamia, in cuneiform, a script made of wedge-shaped marks. It survives on clay tablets, including Assyrian copies from Nineveh and a Babylonian witness. <!-- VERIFY: period and provenance of the Babylonian manuscript --> These copies are much later than the reign of Sargon of Akkad, in the third millennium BCE. They attest the circulation of the story, not the circumstances of the king's birth [@cos1, 1.133].

One proposal places the composition in the reign of [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]], the Assyrian king who ruled from 721 to 705 BCE. A story about an earlier king of the same name who rose from obscure origins and gained divine favor would have suited a ruler whose claim to the throne may have been contested. That is a possible setting for the story, not a date supplied by the text itself. The date of a surviving copy does not establish when the story first took shape.

## Contents

Sargon says his mother was a priestess and that he did not know his father. His mother seals the basket and sets it on the river, which carries him to Aqqi, a man who draws water. Aqqi raises the child as his son and makes him a gardener. Sargon then recalls the favor of Ishtar, a Mesopotamian goddess, before recounting his reign [@anet, 119–121]. The story thus presents divine favor, rather than known royal parentage, as central to his rise, a theme in Mesopotamian ideas about the right to rule (see [[royal-ideology]]).

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Both stories join several details: a hidden birth or infancy, a waterproofed basket made from plants, a river, and a rescue that leads to adoption. In Exod 2:3 Moses' mother coats a papyrus basket with tar and pitch and leaves it among reeds at the riverbank. Sargon's basket, by contrast, is carried along by the river. The comparison concerns materials and actions; it does not by itself show that the Hebrew account reproduces Akkadian wording.

In Exod 2:1–10 Moses' mother hides and protects him, and his sister watches the basket and arranges for his mother to nurse him. Pharaoh's daughter recognizes the child as Hebrew and later adopts him, bringing him into the royal household. The story explains his name by the act of drawing him from the water, while an Egyptian derivation of the name is also commonly proposed [@abd, 1:76–78]. Sargon becomes a king; the adoption scene in Exodus instead emphasizes the child's survival and his ties to both a Hebrew family and the Egyptian royal household.

## Scholarly Issues

The threatened infant who survives to become a prominent figure is a recurring story pattern, found also in traditions about Cyrus and Romulus. The particular combination of river, sealed basket, rescue, and adoption has led some scholars to propose that the Moses story adapts the Sargon legend, possibly in the seventh century BCE. <!-- VERIFY: sources and precise dating of proposed literary dependence --> Another explanation is that both accounts draw on a story pattern that circulated beyond any one written version. The likeness alone cannot date the Moses story or resolve the separate [[exodus-historical-questions|historical questions about the exodus]].
