## Overview

Etemenanki was the great stepped temple tower of Babylon, located near Marduk's temple. Its name evokes the foundations of heaven and earth. The excavated base, cuneiform descriptions and a monument depicting the tower provide evidence for its scale and form. Assyrian and Babylonian kings undertook work on it, especially in the seventh and sixth centuries BCE. It is frequently proposed as a context for the Tower of Babel in Genesis, though the biblical story does not name a building or identify a particular phase of construction.

## Location and Identification

Etemenanki stood in central [[babylon|Babylon]], near Esagil, the main sanctuary of [[marduk|Marduk]]. It was a [[ziggurat]], a tower or platform built in receding stages for a sanctuary. The Sumerian name is commonly understood as “House, Foundation Platform of Heaven and Earth.” Its location and name associate it closely with Babylon's religious role. Much of the superstructure has disappeared. [@abd] [@horowitz-cosmic-geography]

## Archaeological History

Archaeologists traced a large square base of roughly 91 meters on a side. The cuneiform document called the Esagil Tablet gives dimensions and describes seven stages, including the upper sanctuary. How closely its figures describe the structure as actually built is disputed. Herodotus also describes a monumental tower at Babylon. His account is later and cannot be treated as a survey of the remains. [@horowitz-cosmic-geography] [@abd]

Royal records attest work on the tower by Assyrian and Babylonian kings. Esarhaddon undertook restoration, and Nabopolassar and [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] claimed work on it during the Neo-Babylonian period. A stone monument formerly in the Schøyen Collection depicts Nebuchadnezzar beside the tower. The piece reached the collection through the antiquities market, and a later study has proposed a findspot in a trench near Esagil at Babylon based on reconstructed discovery circumstances. It is now in the Iraq Museum. [@rinap4] [@cos2]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 11:3–4 describes making fired bricks and using bitumen, a tar-like substance, for mortar. It also gives the builders' aim of raising a tower whose top reaches the sky. Comparing such language with Nebuchadnezzar's claims about the height of Etemenanki places the passage in a world of monumental construction and royal boasts. In Genesis the builders seek a name and resist dispersal, themes that the temple tower's archaeology cannot by itself explain. [@abd]

The Babylonian setting and the tower's fame make it a plausible background for the [[ziggurat-and-babel|Tower of Babel]]. Earlier stages of the monument leave open the possibility that knowledge of the tower circulated before the Neo-Babylonian rebuilding. Other Mesopotamian cities had comparable towers, and the date of the Genesis narrative in its present form is debated. The identification remains a historical proposal, alongside the firmer comparison with regional building practices. [@abd] [@horowitz-cosmic-geography]

The scale of such projects also presupposed a supply of clay and fuel and the coordination of a large labor force.
