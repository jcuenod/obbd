## Overview

Cisterns stored water, often from rainfall, while wells, shafts and tunnels could give people access to water underground or at a spring. In the ancient southern Levant, both household storage and large communal installations helped settlements endure dry seasons. Bronze and Iron Age examples include rock-cut cisterns and access routes to springs, such as those associated with Jerusalem's Gihon spring. Biblical accounts use these installations as settings for everyday life, royal building and siege. The Gospels also name pools that can be compared with excavated remains.

## Archaeological Evidence

A cistern is a container for collected water. In rocky ground, communities could cut one into bedrock and plaster the walls to limit leakage. William F. Albright associated the spread of lime-plastered cisterns with early Iron Age village settlement in the highlands. Earlier examples of plastered reservoirs and evidence that villages drew on several kinds of water supply make it difficult to attribute settlement to this single innovation. [@mazar-archaeology, 480–485] [@king-stager-life, 122–128, 210–222]

Larger systems could connect a protected part of a settlement to a water source that would otherwise be difficult to reach in a siege. [[megiddo|Megiddo]] and [[hazor|Hazor]] have monumental shafts or tunnels, while [[gibeon|Gibeon]] preserves a large rock-cut pool and a stepped route toward a spring. [[beersheba|Beersheba]] has a large installation for collecting and storing water. The systems reflect different local conditions. Several are dated to the ninth or eighth century BCE, although particular phases and their attribution to rulers remain disputed. [@neaehl, 1:170–173, 2:511–513, 2:710–712] [@mazar-archaeology, 480–485]

At the [[city-of-david|City of David]], Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron reexamined the tunnels near the Gihon spring and argued that Warren's Shaft was not part of the earlier water-drawing system. They dated the large tower protecting the spring to the Middle Bronze Age. Later radiocarbon measurements have led some investigators to place at least part of the construction in the Iron Age, while others defend the earlier date or propose later modifications. [@neaehl, 2:705–712]

## In the Bible

Cisterns appear as inherited household assets in Deut 6:11 and as places of confinement in Gen 37:22–24 and Jer 38:6. The image of broken cisterns in Jer 2:13 assumes that a damaged installation loses stored water. These passages use familiar technology as part of their settings or arguments, even when they give few clues about construction.

Second Kings 20:20 credits Hezekiah with making a pool and a conduit, and 2 Chr 32:30 connects his works with redirecting Gihon's water. These claims are commonly discussed in connection with [[hezekiahs-tunnel|the rock-cut tunnel below the ridge]]. A different text, 2 Sam 5:8, uses a difficult Hebrew term once often associated with Warren's Shaft. The word's meaning and the shaft's early function remain disputed. [@neaehl, 2:705–712] [@abd, 2:61–64]

John 9:7 names the [[pool-of-siloam|Pool of Siloam]] at the southern end of the ancient city, and John 5:2 describes a pool at the Sheep Gate with five porticoes. The latter is commonly associated with the [[pool-of-bethesda|pools of Bethesda]] near the Church of St Anne. The pools illustrate how Jerusalem continued to use and reshape water installations into the Roman period. [@neaehl, 2:746, 2:766–767, 2:801]
