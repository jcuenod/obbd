## Overview

Inscriptions and drawings from Kuntillet ʿAjrud in northeastern Sinai survive on large storage jars and wall plaster; further inscriptions appear on stone vessels. The finds date to c. 800 BCE and include blessings that invoke YHWH with the place names Samaria and Teman. The blessings also mention a disputed term often rendered "his asherah." Together with a fragmentary poem about a divine appearance, the finds show how people in the Iron Age invoked YHWH and offer comparisons for biblical blessings and descriptions of God appearing.

## Description and Provenance

Ze'ev Meshel's excavations at [[kuntillet-ajrud|Kuntillet ʿAjrud]] in 1975–1976 recovered the inscribed vessels and plaster fragments. The Hebrew jar texts were written in ink, while some plaster texts use Phoenician letter forms, which do not by themselves establish the language of the text. Radiocarbon measurements support an occupation around 800 BCE; they do not date individual inscriptions. Letter forms in some texts, pottery comparisons, and the reference to [[samaria|Samaria]] suggest involvement from the northern kingdom of Israel at this station on a southern route. <!-- VERIFY: check which specific pottery groups support northern involvement rather than exchange alone. -->

## Contents

The two best-known *pithoi*, or large storage jars, are designated A and B. A blessing on Pithos A invokes YHWH of Samaria, and a blessing on Pithos B invokes YHWH of Teman; both pair the invocation with the word *ʾšrth*, often rendered "his asherah" [@cos2]. Pithos B also preserves a wish that the recipient be blessed and kept safe. The texts cut into stone bowls include personal names [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions].

One damaged plaster text is commonly read as a theophany, a description of a god appearing. On a widely used reading, the god El shines forth and mountains melt. The fragment is too incomplete to recover the whole scene, and the deity in the opening line should not simply be identified with the YHWH named in the jar blessings.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Pithos B's wish for blessing and protection resembles the opening of the priestly blessing in Num 6:24 and wording on the [[ketef-hinnom-amulets|Ketef Hinnom amulets]]. The similarity shows that such language could appear outside the biblical text, not that the jar writer copied a biblical passage. Deut 16:21 prohibits setting up an asherah beside YHWH's altar, while 2 Kgs 21:7 reports that Manasseh installed an image of Asherah in YHWH's temple. These passages show why an object used in worship is a plausible interpretation of the jar wording without settling what the writers meant.

Hab 3:3 pictures God coming from Teman, and Deut 33:2 associates YHWH's coming with Sinai, Seir, and Paran. These southern associations are relevant to discussions of [[yhwh-origins|YHWH's origins]], but the jar title alone does not establish where YHWH worship began. The quaking mountains in Judg 5:4–5 and the melting mountains in Mic 1:3–4 offer a different comparison: like the plaster text, they portray the landscape reacting to a divine appearance.

Amos 8:14 links oath language with Samaria, Dan, and Beersheba, though its wording is not identical to the jar titles. In 2 Sam 15:7, it is disputed whether Hebron qualifies the divine name or simply names the place where Absalom plans to pay his vow. It is likewise debated whether Deut 6:4 counters distinctions between local manifestations of YHWH; the verse does not name any such manifestations.

## Scholarly Issues

Some scholars understand *ʾšrth* as the goddess [[asherah|Asherah]]; others see it as a sacred object, possibly one associated with the goddess. The ending commonly read as "his" is unusual on a personal name in Hebrew, which favors the object reading but does not resolve the debate. The related wording in the [[khirbet-el-qom-inscription|Khirbet el-Qom tomb inscription]] is an independent comparison, not an explanation of the term.

Pithos A shows two standing figures resembling [[bes|Bes]], an Egyptian protective deity, and a seated figure commonly identified as a lyre player. An early interpretation identified the standing pair as YHWH and Asherah, but most specialists do not read the figures as illustrations of the nearby blessing [@keel-uehlinger-ggig]. The religious content fits the interpretation of a caravan station where religious practices also took place. It does not by itself show that the buildings housed a temple.
