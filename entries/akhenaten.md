## Overview

Akhenaten, also known as Amenhotep IV, ruled Egypt in the fourteenth century BCE. He founded a royal city at Akhetaten, modern Amarna, devoted to Aten, the visible disk of the sun. His court increasingly concentrated worship on Aten, and the names and images of Amun were attacked on monuments. Akhenaten's building inscriptions, hymns, and diplomatic correspondence show different sides of his reign. His reforms provide a comparison for biblical language about the one God, and a hymn from his reign describes daily rhythms of creation that also appear in Psalm 104.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Akhenaten came to the throne as Amenhotep IV and began building for Aten at Karnak. He changed his name and established Akhetaten early in his reign. Inscriptions cut on the cliffs around the site designate it as a place for Aten and record the king's building plans. In the characteristic imagery of the court, the sun disk extends rays ending in hands toward the king and his family. The royal family occupies a central place in the worship reflected in those images. [@ael, 2:48–51]

The [[amarna-letters|Amarna Letters]] preserve diplomatic correspondence from the reigns of Akhenaten, his father Amenhotep III, and their immediate successors. Letters from the rulers of Canaan report local rivalries and appeals to Egypt for assistance. This evidence for Egyptian administration and diplomacy complements the monuments that describe the king's religious program. Some letters refer to groups called [[habiru|ḫabiru]], people outside established political communities, whose proposed association with the biblical Hebrews remains debated. [@moran-amarna, xiii–xviii]

## Biblical Portrayal

The most sustained biblical comparison concerns the [[great-hymn-to-the-aten|Great Hymn to the Aten]]. Like Ps 104:20–30, it depicts animals active in the dark, human labor in daylight, and the dependence of living things on divine provision. The shared sequence makes comparison worthwhile. The hymn addresses the sun disk and gives the Egyptian king privileged knowledge of the god, while the psalm addresses YHWH and celebrates the world as his work. Scholars have proposed a relationship between the compositions, but the path by which the hymn could have reached a biblical writer remains uncertain. [@ael, 2:96–100] [@cos1, 1.28]

Deut 6:4 confesses YHWH as Israel's God and uses language that can express oneness or exclusive allegiance. Atenism is a debated comparison for such language. The Egyptian evidence is centered on the reigning household and its mediation of worship. The biblical confession speaks in the setting of a relationship between YHWH and Israel as a people. The different contexts limit what can be inferred about [[yhwh-origins|the historical origins of YHWH worship]].

## Historical Assessment

A conventional chronology gives Akhenaten's reign as c. 1353–1336 BCE. Whether, or for how long, he shared authority with Amenhotep III remains debated. Scholars also disagree about whether his late reforms amount to monotheism, belief in only one god, or henotheism, the elevation of one god while allowing for others. Describing the program as a royal cult emphasizes the king's role and leaves open how much the wider population adopted the new worship.

Under Akhenaten's successors, the traditional cults regained official support. Later rulers dismantled his monuments and erased his name, limiting the evidence for an enduring public Aten cult. Sigmund Freud proposed a historical route from Atenism to Moses, a reconstruction for which the surviving evidence is insufficient. Jan Assmann instead studied Moses in cultural memory, asking how later societies remembered and recast religious distinctions.
