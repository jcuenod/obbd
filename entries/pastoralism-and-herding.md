## Overview

Herding supplied meat, milk, wool, hair, and other products in the ancient Near East. In the southern Levant, many families combined sheep and goat keeping with farming. Other groups moved animals or households seasonally between grazing areas and settlements. Archaeology, law collections, and letters show both the economic value of herds and the risks involved in caring for them. They help explain conflicts over pasture in Genesis, the responsibility of hired herders in Exodus, and the varied roles that biblical texts describe with terms for tending flocks.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Assemblages of animal bones show the importance of sheep and goats at many Levantine sites. Cattle supplied meat and could be used for plowing and other heavy work. Flocks could yield milk, wool, and hair while remaining alive, which made them valuable to households and estates. Herding near villages could use stubble after harvest and seasonal grazing on land less suitable for crops. Longer movements depended on access to water and grazing and on relations with settled communities.

The eighteenth-century BCE [[mari-letters|Mari archives]] describe groups such as the Binu Yamina who maintained ties to settlements while people and animals moved across the steppe. Michael Rowton used the term dimorphic society for settings where mobile and settled sectors coexisted and were interconnected. Subsequent research has emphasized that households and communities could combine both ways of life. Long-distance, specialized camel pastoralism offers a poor model for every mobile group described in older texts.

Hammurabi’s Laws §§261–267 address the hiring of herders and responsibility for losses. In some circumstances a herder is liable. In others an oath can establish that the loss resulted from a cause outside the herder’s control. The provisions show why owners and workers needed agreed ways to assess events that happened far from witnesses.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 4:2 introduces keeping sheep alongside working the land. In Gen 13:2–12 Abram and Lot separate amid pressure on grazing resources. Gen 29:1–10 depicts a shared well and conventions for watering animals. In Gen 30:25–43 Jacob and Laban negotiate compensation involving the offspring of flocks. Gen 37:12–17 places Jacob’s sons some distance from home with the animals. These passages depict household wealth and negotiations over labor, movement, and access to resources.

Exod 22:10–13 (MT 22:9–12) sets out liability for loss of entrusted livestock. It calls for an oath in some cases and for evidence of predation in another. In Gen 31:38–40 Jacob claims that he personally bore losses beyond what he considered fair. Amos 3:12 uses the retrieval of small parts of a sheep from a lion’s mouth as an image of scant survival. The pieces also make sense as evidence from a predator attack. The danger itself features in David’s account in 1 Sam 17:34–35, and the celebration of sheep shearing forms the setting for 1 Sam 25:2–8.

The designation *nōqēd* occurs in Amos 1:1 and for King Mesha in 2 Kgs 3:4. A related Ugaritic term has encouraged interpretations such as flock manager or breeder. Amos 7:14–15 also associates Amos with herding and tending sycamore figs. The vocabulary leaves the precise scale of his enterprise open. Luke 2:8 presents the practical task of remaining with animals at night.

## Interpretive Significance

Some accounts of the [[emergence-of-israel|emergence of Israel]] connect growth in early Iron Age highland settlements with the settlement of formerly mobile households. Material similarities can reveal shared economic practices, while changes in village density do not supply individual biographies or ethnic identities. The Mari evidence helps illustrate how mobility and cultivation could coexist. Its time and place limit its use as a direct model for the origins of Israel.
