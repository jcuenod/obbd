## Overview

Excavations on Mount Ebal north of the valley of Shechem uncovered an early Iron Age enclosure with a large stone structure. The excavator, Adam Zertal, identified it as a sacrificial altar and proposed a connection with the account of Joshua building an altar on the mountain. Other scholars have suggested different functions or accept a ritual use while leaving the biblical connection open. A much later study of an object recovered from the excavated spoil has led to a separate controversy about whether it carries an ancient inscription.

## Location and Identification

Mount Ebal lies opposite [[mount-gerizim|Mount Gerizim]], with the valley of [[shechem|Shechem]] between them. The excavated site lies on Ebal's northeastern slope. Its location on the mountain named in the traditional Hebrew texts prompted a comparison with the biblical account. The identification of the mountain, the function of the structure, and its association with a specific person or episode depend on different kinds of evidence.

## Archaeological History

Zertal's excavations in the 1980s exposed an enclosure and a rectangular stone structure approached by a feature interpreted as a ramp. The fill contained pottery, ash, and animal bones, including remains of sheep or goats, cattle, and fallow deer. Pottery and small finds place the principal activity around the transition from the Late Bronze Age to Iron Age I. Zertal interpreted the construction and its deposits together as evidence for a cult installation and specifically an altar for burnt offerings.

The interpretation depends in part on the function of the ramp and on how the animal remains and ash entered the fill. Alternative proposals have included a watchtower or an agricultural structure. Some scholars favor a cultic function without identifying the builders or connecting it directly to Joshua. The surviving structures and finds give a basis for comparing these proposals, while leaving the identity of the people who used the installation uncertain.

A folded lead object was found during the wet-sifting of discarded material from the earlier excavations. Its publishers used tomography to propose an early Hebrew curse containing a short form of the divine name. Several archaeological and epigraphic responses find that the images fail to demonstrate readable letters and that the marks have plausible physical explanations. The object came from a spoil heap, so it cannot securely be placed in a specific deposit or phase of the site. The proposed reading remains disputed.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Deut 11:29 sets the [[blessings-and-curses|blessing and curse]] on Gerizim and Ebal respectively, and Deut 27:11–13 divides the tribes between the two mountains. In the Masoretic form of Deut 27:1–8 Moses commands an altar of unworked stones on Ebal. The Samaritan Pentateuch reads Gerizim at Deut 27:4, an important difference for the history of the text. The prescription of unworked stones can also be compared with Exod 20:25.

Josh 8:30–35 connects an altar on Ebal with the public reading of the law. The fragmentary Qumran manuscript 4QJoshᵃ has been interpreted as placing material connected with that episode near the Jordan crossing, earlier in the narrative. The extent of that rearrangement is debated. These textual differences show the importance of studying the history of the altar tradition when relating it to the archaeological remains.
