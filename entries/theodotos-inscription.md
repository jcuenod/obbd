## Overview

The Theodotos Inscription identifies a synagogue built in Jerusalem to read the Law, teach the commandments, and provide lodging for visitors. The Greek text names Theodotos, a priest and synagogue leader, and records that his father and grandfather held the same synagogue title. Found in excavations in the City of David, the inscription is generally dated before the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE. It is the principal inscriptional evidence for such a synagogue in Jerusalem during the Second Temple period.

## Description and Provenance

Raymond Weill's excavations of 1913–1914 recovered the inscribed stone from a cistern in the southeastern part of ancient Jerusalem. The stone is a dedicatory plaque, with the Greek text cut across ten lines. The cistern records a secondary location for the plaque. The find gives evidence for a Jerusalem synagogue without identifying which excavated building, if any, housed it.

## Contents

The dedicant identifies himself as Theodotos son of Vettenos, priest and *archisynagōgos*, or synagogue leader. He is also the son and grandson of synagogue leaders. The text credits him with building or restoring a [[synagogues|synagogue]] for reading the Law and teaching the commandments. It also refers to lodging, rooms, and installations commonly understood as water facilities for people arriving from abroad. At the end it associates the foundation with ancestors, elders, and a figure named Simon.

The inscription identifies the aims of the building through teaching and hospitality. It gives no full program of activities. The combination of a priestly title, several generations of synagogue leadership, and guest facilities shows that a synagogue could serve several needs within a community that also had a temple.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Luke 4:16–21 depicts synagogue reading and interpretation on the Sabbath. Acts 15:21 refers to the regular reading of Moses in synagogues, and Acts 13:15 uses the same title for leaders who invite a visitor to speak after the reading. These passages and the inscription attest practices and offices shared by Jewish groups in different towns.

Acts 2:5–11 depicts people from many regions in Jerusalem for a festival. The lodging mentioned by Theodotos offers evidence of institutions that could accommodate people coming from outside the city. Some scholars connect the inscription with the Synagogue of the Freedmen in Acts 6:9. The Latinate patronymic makes the comparison possible, while neither the stone nor Acts supplies enough detail to establish the identification.

## Scholarly Issues

The plaque supplies no explicit year. Palaeography, language, and excavation context have led most specialists to favor a date before 70 CE. A later date has also been argued. The evidence for the institution depends on interpreting the dedication, while the actual appearance and size of its building remain unknown. Its most direct testimony concerns the community that commemorated the building and the purposes it chose to record.
