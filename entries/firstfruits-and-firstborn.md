## Overview

The Hebrew Bible describes offering the first produce of a harvest and dedicating the firstborn of people and animals to God. These practices connect the beginning of a crop or family line with an obligation to the deity. Ancient Near Eastern temples likewise received seasonal gifts and offerings from agricultural production. The biblical laws use several ways to fulfill the obligation, including giving produce, offering animals, and redeeming people through payment or substitution. Differences among the laws help explain the debate over whether a command to give the firstborn could ever have been understood as requiring child sacrifice.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The beginnings of harvests could be marked with ritual gifts. At Ugarit, Late Bronze Age ritual texts prescribe offerings in the month called the first wine, including a rite involving a bunch of grapes. Offerings from agriculture also supplied provisions for temples and their staff across the ancient Near East. This puts biblical gifts of first produce within a broader setting of sacred giving, although details such as recipients and timing depended on local traditions [@ktu3] [@cohen-cultic-calendars].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 23:19 and 34:26 direct people to bring the best of their first produce to the house of YHWH. Lev 23:9–14 describes presenting a first sheaf before eating from the new crop. In Deut 26:1–11, an offerer brings a basket of first produce and recites the story of the ancestors' descent into Egypt, oppression, deliverance, and arrival in the land. The gift acknowledges both produce and possession of the land.

In Exod 13:1–2, 11–16, the firstborn belongs to God in memory of the deliverance from Egypt. The account distinguishes offerings from redemption, which fulfills a claim through a payment or substitute. Firstborn sons are redeemed, and a donkey may be redeemed with a lamb. Num 3:11–13, 40–51 depicts the Levites taking the place of Israel's firstborn, with payment for the excess number. Num 18:12–18 gives priests a share of first produce and distinguishes firstborn animals to be sacrificed from those to be redeemed.

## Interpretive Significance

Exod 22:29–30 (MT 22:28–29) instructs Israel to give firstborn sons to YHWH and gives related instructions for livestock. These verses do not state how the obligation for sons is met. Jon Levenson has argued that laws of redemption preserve traces of an earlier claim that included sacrifice. Other interpreters read the instruction against the biblical provision for redemption and see the terse wording as allowing for it from the beginning. The historical question remains disputed.

Ezek 20:25–26 speaks of harmful decrees and offerings involving firstborn children. Scholars debate whether the prophet refers to an interpretation of earlier law, denounces an existing form of [[child-sacrifice|child sacrifice]], or explains the practice through a theological account of judgment. The passage attests a forceful argument over the firstborn without establishing the original meaning of Exodus. Luke 2:22–24 reflects a later Jewish setting in which Jesus is presented at the temple and an offering is brought.

Gerhard von Rad called the recital in Deut 26:5–9 a small historical creed and thought it preserved an early core of Israel's account of its past. Many scholars now date the formulation later. The date remains debated, while the text clearly connects first produce with a public act of remembering deliverance.
