## Overview

Hurrian-speaking communities lived across northern Mesopotamia, Syria, and adjoining regions in the third and second millennia BCE. Hurrian was used at the court of Mitanni, a major Late Bronze Age power, and Hurrian names and religious traditions reached Canaan. The Bible calls some of the earlier inhabitants of Seir Horites. Scholars have compared that designation and a few Greek textual variants with the name of the Hurrians. The historical connection remains disputed. The evidence helps readers distinguish the spread of Hurrian culture from a proposed identification of particular biblical groups.

## Sources

Inscriptions attest Hurrian names and language at Urkesh in northern Mesopotamia in the late third millennium BCE. The [[amarna-letters|Amarna correspondence]] includes a long letter from Tušratta written in Hurrian, alongside diplomatic correspondence written in Akkadian. Texts from Alalakh and [[nuzi-texts|Nuzi]] preserve extensive evidence of Hurrian names and social life. Egyptian records can use *Ḫurru* for a territory in Syria-Palestine, a geographical expression with changing scope [@moran-amarna, EA 24] [@anet, 483–490] [@moran-amarna, EA 24].

## History

Mitanni was a major kingdom of the fifteenth and fourteenth centuries BCE, with interests in northern Mesopotamia and Syria. Its rulers exchanged letters and negotiated royal marriages with Egypt. Over time Hittite and Assyrian expansion weakened Mitanni. Hurrian names, gods, and ritual traditions circulated beyond its political borders. Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem in the Amarna letters, for example, has a name that combines a Semitic element for servant with a form of the name of the Hurrian goddess Ḫebat. A person's name gives evidence of cultural contact, while ancestry requires other evidence.

Nuzi's records of family transactions once led scholars including E. A. Speiser and Cyrus Gordon to date or explain patriarchal customs with reference to a specifically Hurrian setting. Thomas L. Thompson, John Van Seters, and others showed that many such practices had a broader distribution and that some proposed matches involved legally different cases. The tablets remain useful evidence for comparative institutions such as [[adoption]], with limited force for dating a biblical narrative.

## In the Bible

Gen 14:6 places Horites in the hill country of Seir. Gen 36:20–30 names Seir the Horite and lists his descendants, and Deut 2:12, 22 describes the Horites as displaced by Esau's descendants, the [[edomites|Edomites]]. Several names in the genealogy fit a Semitic setting. Some have connected the ethnonym with a Hebrew word for a cave, and others treat it as the name of a local group. The derivation is uncertain.

Gen 34:2 and Josh 9:7 illustrate why the debate also includes Hivites. The Masoretic Text identifies Hamor and the Gibeonites as Hivites, while ancient Greek witnesses use a form conventionally rendered Horites in these passages. Differences among the texts show variation in the transmission of the names. They are insufficient to establish that the people named in either passage were Hurrian speakers.
