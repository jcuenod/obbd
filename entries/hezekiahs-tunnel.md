## Overview

The Siloam Tunnel is a rock-cut channel about 533 meters long that conducts water from the Gihon Spring beneath Jerusalem’s southeastern ridge toward the Siloam Pool. An inscription found near its lower end describes two groups of cutters meeting underground. Scientific dating supports an Iron Age date around 700 BCE, and the tunnel is commonly associated with waterworks attributed to Hezekiah. The date and biblical descriptions make the identification plausible. Neither the tunnel nor its inscription names the ruler who commissioned it.

## Location and Identification

The tunnel runs from the Gihon Spring on the eastern side of the City of David ridge to a lower outlet on its southern side. It is often called Hezekiah’s Tunnel, based on the comparison with biblical texts about his waterworks. The name Siloam Tunnel identifies the structure by its outlet. The later pool known from the Second Temple period is a distinct feature of the water system. Its surviving architecture cannot be used as the date of the underlying Iron Age tunnel.

The hill preserves more than one route for access to the Gihon and distribution of its water. Warren’s Shaft and the works close to the spring belong to a complex with several building phases. The earlier open or partly covered channel conventionally called Channel II followed the eastern slope. The Siloam Tunnel passes through the ridge and has its own construction history.

## Archaeological History

The tunnel follows a winding course through limestone. Its builders maintained a small overall fall that allows water to flow by gravity from the spring to the outlet. The [[siloam-inscription|Siloam Inscription]], discovered in 1880, describes workers cutting from opposite directions and meeting. That account explains how the separate cuts became a continuous passage. It does not give the surveying method. Geological studies suggest that natural features and guidance from the surface helped shape the route, but scholars differ on the role of natural cracks and conduits.

Radiocarbon analysis of organic material in plaster and uranium-thorium dating of mineral deposits support an Iron Age construction and a date around 700 BCE (Frumkin, Shimron, and Rosenbaum, 2003). The two methods date materials associated with the tunnel, with different relationships to its first cutting. Reich and Shukron proposed a date before Hezekiah on stratigraphic grounds, including pottery from deposits associated with access to the system. The dispute partly concerns which deposits and constructions date the cutting of the passage itself.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Second Kings 20:20 credits [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] with a pool and conduit that brought water into the city. Second Chronicles 32:2–4 describes obstructing water sources in anticipation of Assyrian attack, and 32:30 names the Gihon and a diversion toward the City of David. The archaeological tunnel gives a plausible physical referent for this interpretation of the notices. It remains possible that some passages refer to different stages or components of Jerusalem’s waterworks.

Isa 22:9–11 describes collecting water, considering the city’s defenses, and making a reservoir between walls. It evokes comparable concerns without identifying the tunnel. The gently flowing waters of Shiloah in Isa 8:6 can be understood against the local spring and channel system, even if the text is earlier than the tunnel’s usual date. Sir 48:17 remembers Hezekiah bringing water into Jerusalem and describes cutting through rock. John 9:7 directs the man born blind to wash in the Pool of Siloam. That passage concerns the pool familiar in the first century CE, many centuries after the Iron Age works.
