## Overview

Chemosh was the principal god of Moab, the Iron Age kingdom east of the Dead Sea. The clearest record of his role is a ninth-century BCE inscription in which King Mesha credits Chemosh with Moabite victories and explains Israelite domination as the result of the god's anger. Biblical writers likewise associate Chemosh with Moab and remember a place of worship established for him near Jerusalem. The evidence shows how royal writers used the language of divine anger, command and aid to explain national defeats and victories.

## Sources and Iconography

The [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]] found at Dhiban presents Chemosh as central to the fortunes of Moab. Mesha says that the god was angry with his land, accounting for Israel's control. He reports that Chemosh directed him to take Nebo and that its inhabitants were devoted to Ashtar-Chemosh. The latter is a compound divine name whose precise significance is debated. The language of [[herem|devotion to destruction]] and the portrayal of victory as divine favor have useful comparisons in biblical accounts, while each text speaks from a distinct political and religious position. [@kai, no. 181] [@cos2, 2.23] [@ahituv-echoes, 389–418]

A god named Kamish appears much earlier in texts from Ebla in Syria. The possible relation of that name to Chemosh remains uncertain. Proposed images of Chemosh include the figures on monuments from Shihan and Baluʿa. No secure inscription identifies those figures as Chemosh, so the god's appearance remains unknown. [@ddd, 186–189]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Num 21:29 calls the defeated Moabites the people of Chemosh. In Jer 48:7, 13, 46, the prophet imagines Chemosh and his priests going into exile and portrays Moab as disappointed in the god it trusted. The identification of Chemosh with Moab also stands behind 1 Kgs 11:7, 33 and 2 Kgs 23:13, which associate Solomon with a place of worship for the god and describe its later defilement under Josiah.

Judg 11:24 has Jephthah appeal to Chemosh in a speech to the Ammonite king. Elsewhere the Bible associates Ammon with [[milkom|Milkom]]. Some readers see a sign that the speech draws on traditions involving Moab, while others understand the use of Chemosh's name in light of the passage's concern with contested territory. The discrepancy limits what can confidently be said about Ammonite worship from this verse. [@ddd, 186–189] [@abd, 1:895–897]

## Scholarly Issues

In 2 Kgs 3:26–27 the king of Moab offers his heir on the city wall, and great wrath comes against Israel. Israel's forces then withdraw. The narrative supplies no name for the source of the wrath, and the sacrifice has often been understood as addressed to Chemosh in light of his place in Moab. Scholars have proposed that the wrath is that of Chemosh, that of Yahweh or an intense human reaction to the act. The verse establishes the order of events while leaving the source of the wrath open. [@ddd, 186–189] [@abd, 1:895–897]
