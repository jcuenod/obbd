## Overview

Darius I's Susa foundation inscription, conventionally called DSf, describes the construction of his palace at Susa. It lists timber, stone, metals, and other materials brought from across the Persian Empire and attributes particular work to groups of artisans. The composition is known from several inscribed witnesses, including an Old Persian clay tablet in the Louvre. The inscription gives context for the palace setting of Esther and illustrates the long-distance procurement that royal construction could require.

## Manuscripts and Date

The inscription belongs to the reign of [[darius-i|Darius I]] and survives in Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian versions. One witness is Louvre SB 2789, a clay tablet in Old Persian from the palace at [[susa]]. An early account places the find beneath a doorway threshold, though the details of the discovery differ between records [@louvre-sb2789] [@basello-old-persian-clay].

## Contents

Darius attributes his kingship to Ahuramazda and claims responsibility for the palace. The construction account describes preparing the ground, making bricks, bringing cedar from Lebanon through Babylon, and procuring stone and precious materials from several regions. It names peoples associated with tasks such as stonework, goldwork, and timber work. This organization of labor and materials serves a rhetorical purpose. The finished palace is presented as evidence of a king's ability to mobilize the resources of his empire [@kent-old-persian, DSf, 142–144].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Esth 1:2–7 places Ahasuerus at Susa and evokes court wealth through building materials, furnishings, and vessels. Darius's earlier account provides context for the palace complex and its display of imported resources. Identifying the furnishings of the banquet would require separate evidence. Ezra 3:7 describes payment and the transport of Lebanese cedar for the Jerusalem temple. The palace inscription offers a comparison for the logistics of moving construction materials, with a different destination, workforce, and sponsoring authority.

## Scholarly Issues

The inscription is a royal presentation of the project. Its lists are selective, and the origin assigned to a material or group of workers tells only part of the story of its procurement. The account records enough stages of transport and construction to show the scale of the undertaking. It gives fewer details about the obligations, compensation, or legal status of the people doing the work.
