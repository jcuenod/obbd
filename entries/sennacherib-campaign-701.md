## Overview

In 701 BCE Sennacherib of Assyria campaigned in the southern Levant to restore imperial control. His forces defeated opponents on the coast and in the lowlands, took Lachish, and devastated much of Judah. Jerusalem remained in Hezekiah’s hands, and he paid tribute. The campaign is documented by Assyrian inscriptions, palace reliefs, a destruction layer at Lachish, and biblical narratives. These sources agree on several events and differ in their emphases, chronology, and claims about why Jerusalem survived.

## Sources

The [[sennacherib-annals|Assyrian annals]] describe the western campaign as Sennacherib’s third. They recount victories, deportations, installation or restoration of rulers, and payments. Their tally of forty-six fortified Judahite towns and 200,150 people taken away represents the king’s reported figure. The text praises the campaign and associates its results with legitimate Assyrian power. It does not claim that Jerusalem was captured.

The [[lachish-reliefs|palace reliefs]] identify the city of Lachish and depict its capture. Excavations at [[lachish|Lachish]] document the destruction of Level III and a siege ramp associated with the attack. The inscription, reliefs, and excavated remains together provide firm evidence for the city’s fate. Each witness answers different questions. The royal images emphasize victory and the presentation of captives and goods. The excavated destruction records what happened at the site.

## Reconstruction

The annals portray rebellions and resistance across the region after Assyria changed kings. Sennacherib reasserted control over territories associated with Sidon and turned to the cities of the southern coast. He describes fighting Egyptian and Kushite forces at Eltekeh, punishing leaders at Ekron, and restoring its former ruler, whom Hezekiah had held. These accounts show overlapping conflicts. They need not imply that every regional opponent belonged to a single coordinated alliance.

Judah lost towns and territory. The distribution of [[lmlk-seal-impressions|lmlk stamped jars]], including examples in the destruction of Lachish, has often been linked to royal collection and provisioning before the attack. Disagreement concerns how long the system had operated and how much of its use was directed specifically toward the crisis. Changes in Jerusalem’s defenses and water supply are likewise often associated with preparation for Assyrian pressure. The physical remains do not independently name the year in which each project began.

## Biblical Accounts

Second Kings 18:13–16 dates the campaign in [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]]’s reign, acknowledges widespread conquest, and reports that he paid thirty talents of gold and three hundred of silver. The Assyrian annals report the same amount of gold but eight hundred talents of silver. They also describe tribute as sent to Nineveh. The textual accounts give different totals and locations. The difference cannot be settled by either source alone.

Second Kings 18:17–19:37 and Isa 36–37 emphasize the Assyrian threat, negotiations, prophecy, and the city’s survival. Many scholars distinguish overlapping narrative accounts in this material. Second Kings 19:9 names [[taharqa|Taharqa]] as king of Kush even though he took the throne later. Possible explanations include an earlier role attributed to him under his later title, or a chronological problem in the narrative. The deaths in the Assyrian camp in 2 Kgs 19:35 have no Assyrian equivalent. The story Herodotus tells about an Egyptian deliverance (*Histories* 2.141) takes place in a separate tradition and cannot independently establish what happened outside Jerusalem.

Second Chronicles 32:1–23 and 32:30 describe preparations, waterworks, and divine deliverance. Isa 22:8–11 depicts defensive measures and criticizes confidence in them. Some readers connect Mic 1:10–16 and Isa 1:7–9 with the impact of the campaign on towns and countryside. Those identifications remain less direct than the agreement about Lachish and the survival of Jerusalem.
