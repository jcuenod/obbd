## Overview

In 853 BCE, Shalmaneser III of Assyria fought a coalition of western rulers at Qarqar on the Orontes River in present-day Syria. An Assyrian monument names Ahab of Israel among the coalition's principal contributors, alongside the rulers of Damascus and Hamath. The Assyrian king claimed a victory, but later campaigns show that the coalition continued to oppose him. The Bible does not recount the battle. The dated Assyrian account is one of the earliest firm external anchors for the chronology of Israel's kings and shows Ahab working alongside a kingdom that biblical narratives often portray as his enemy.

## Sources

The fullest early account comes from the [[kurkh-monoliths|Kurkh Monolith]] of [[shalmaneser-iii|Shalmaneser III]], a stone monument covered with an account of his campaigns. The document names the army's participants and places the battle in a year identifiable through the Assyrian system of naming years after officials. Other inscriptions of Shalmaneser describe the campaign or subsequent fighting against groups of western rulers. All present the events from the Assyrian king's perspective [@rima3] [@cos2].

## Reconstruction

According to the inscription, Hadadezer of Damascus and Irhuleni of [[hamath|Hamath]] led a coalition designated as twelve kings. Its members came from different parts of the region. It credits [[ahab|Ahab]] with two thousand chariots and ten thousand troops, the largest chariot contribution in its list. Damascus and Hamath are also credited with substantial contingents of chariots, cavalry, and foot soldiers.

The size of Ahab's chariot force is disputed. Nadav Naʾaman argued that the figure might reflect a scribal error for two hundred. Others retain the stated total, suggesting it could have represented a force equipped by Ahab and supplemented by allies. Assyrian royal inscriptions can also use numerical claims to emphasize the scale of the opponent. The inscription securely establishes Ahab's participation, while the size and makeup of his force are less certain.

Shalmaneser reports victory in terms that magnify the losses he inflicted. Renewed fighting with the western alliance in 849, 848, and 845 BCE shows that it soon regained or retained the ability to resist. Many historians therefore judge Qarqar to have had no decisive outcome for Assyrian control of the region. Local rulers remained capable of coordinating resistance [@rima3].

## Biblical Accounts

Kings says that Ahab, son of Omri, reigned at Samaria and directs readers to a royal record for further deeds (1 Kgs 16:29; 22:39). It gives no account of the encounter at Qarqar. The Assyrian record locates Ahab in 853 BCE and therefore supplies an absolute date within his reign. This is an important external anchor for the wider [[chronology-of-kings|chronology of Israel's kings]].

In 1 Kgs 20:31–34, Ahab agrees to terms with [[ben-hadad|Ben-hadad]] after defeating him. Another passage mentions three years without war between Israel and Aram (1 Kgs 22:1). Some historians link the peace to their cooperation at Qarqar and identify the Ben-hadad of the story with the Hadadezer of the Assyrian account. The identification and the historical setting of the biblical episodes are disputed. The Assyrian text independently establishes that the rulers of Israel and Damascus could fight on the same side in 853 BCE.
