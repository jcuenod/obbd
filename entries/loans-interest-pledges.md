## Overview

Lending in the ancient Near East could sustain a struggling household or finance a transaction. Creditors could charge interest and ask for goods, land, or people as security. Babylonian laws and Persian-period Aramaic contracts document such arrangements, while biblical laws restrict what creditors may take and protect debtors in need. Biblical narratives, prophets, and proverbs also describe the effects of indebtedness. The comparison shows how the Hebrew texts distinguish a creditor's claim from a household's need to retain its means of living.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[laws-of-eshnunna|Laws of Eshnunna]] and provisions in the [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] attest standard rates of 20 percent for silver and a third for grain. Individual contracts and other periods could differ. Hammurabi's laws also address the unauthorized taking of assets and the handling of people seized for debts. The Persian-period [[elephantine-papyri|Elephantine contracts]] record loans, monthly interest terms, and obligations if the debtor failed to pay. They show that interest-bearing arrangements existed in a community that included Judeans [@roth-law, 98] [@tad, B4.2].

## In the Hebrew Bible

*Nešek* (“bite”) and *tarbît* or *marbît* (“increase”) are Hebrew terms for interest. Lev 25:35–37 uses both in protecting impoverished kin. Some interpreters assign them to amounts deducted at the outset or added at repayment. Others regard them as near-synonyms. Ezek 18:8, 13, 17 likewise treats such charges as moral evidence, while Ps 15:5 praises a person who does not lend at interest.

Exod 22:25–27 (MT 22:24–26) forbids charging interest to the poor and requires the return of a pledged cloak before night. Deut 23:19–20 (MT 23:20–21) distinguishes lending to an Israelite from lending to an outsider. Deut 24:6, 10–13, 17 protects the millstone needed for food preparation and the widow's garment, restricts a creditor's entry into a debtor's home, and requires the return of a cloak used for sleeping. These rules give practical weight to the household's need for shelter, food, and dignity.

Hebrew uses several words for security pledged. In Gen 38:17–18 Judah leaves his seal, cord, and staff as an *ʿērābôn*, a guarantee of payment. A related word appears in Greek as *arrabōn*, used figuratively for a guarantee in 2 Cor 1:22. Prov 22:26–27 warns against becoming surety for another person's debts. Job 24:3, 9 and Amos 2:8 present the effects of harsh pledges on the vulnerable. In 2 Kgs 4:1 the creditor threatens to take a widow's children into [[slavery|bondage]].

## Interpretive Significance

Neh 5:1–13 describes a crisis involving taxes, mortgages, and the loss of children and property. Nehemiah demands restoration and refers to a hundredth of silver and produce, sometimes understood as a monthly rate. The interval and precise interpretation remain disputed. The episode is especially close to the protection of needy kin in biblical law. Luke 19:23 presupposes that interest could be earned from deposited money in a different literary and social setting. Individual passages therefore need to be read in relation to the type of loan and the people concerned.
