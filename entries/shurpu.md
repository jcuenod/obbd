## Overview

*Šurpu* (“Burning”) is a Mesopotamian series of incantations and rituals for releasing a person from an afflicting ban or curse. It includes long lists of offenses because the cause of the suffering might be unknown. The series probably developed in the later second millennium BCE and survives mainly in first-millennium copies. Its concern with unnoticed faults makes it a useful comparison for biblical laws on unwitting wrongdoing and prayers for release from hidden faults. Its form and remedy differ from those of the biblical texts.

## Manuscripts and Date

The series consists of several cuneiform tablets, preserved in manuscripts from Nineveh and other scholarly collections. It uses Akkadian alongside some Sumerian material. Scholars often place its development in the Middle Babylonian period, though surviving copies are mostly Neo-Assyrian or later. The date when its sequence reached the form known from those copies is less certain. Its title also distinguishes it from [[maqlu|Maqlû]], a separate series especially concerned with countering witchcraft [@cos1].

## Contents

An *āšipu*, a ritual specialist, seeks release for a person affected by *māmītu*, an oath-related ban or curse. Tablet II surveys possible causes. These include moving boundaries, dishonoring parents, false statements, dishonest dealings, and failures connected with ritual obligations. The list does not require the sufferer to have recognized the original wrong. It accommodates a search for an offense in a situation where the consequences may be more evident than the cause.

Other parts of the series represent the curse as something that can be taken apart and removed. The texts compare its release with materials being stripped or separated and then consumed in fire. The spoken analogies belong to a ritual that seeks forgiveness or release through the action of divine powers [@cos1].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Lev 4:2, 13–14 addresses wrongdoing committed unwittingly and later recognized. Lev 5:1–6 includes cases involving an ignored adjuration, unnoticed impurity, and a rash oath. Lev 5:17–19 requires an offering even when the person did not know that an offense had occurred. These provisions concern recognized liabilities and sacrificial remedies. *Šurpu* offers a comparison for the possibility that culpability can precede awareness, with a different account of how the problem is removed.

Deut 27:15–26 lists offenses that bring curses, including dishonoring parents and moving a [[boundary-markers|boundary marker]]. The overlap illustrates social concerns that could matter in both settings. Ps 19:12 (MT 19:13) asks for clearance from hidden faults, giving the question a direct place in prayer.

## Scholarly Issues

Job 31:5–34 uses a list of possible offenses in a protest of innocence. Ezek 18:5–9 sets out the practices of a righteous person. The Egyptian [[book-of-the-dead|Book of the Dead]] includes a declaration of innocence before divine judges. The lists overlap with *Šurpu* in subject matter. Their purposes vary between defending oneself, defining right conduct, and seeking release from a curse whose cause may be unknown.
