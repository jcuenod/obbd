## Overview

The Baal Cycle is a series of mythological poems preserved on six clay tablets from Late Bronze Age Ugarit, a port on the northern Syrian coast. The poems tell how the storm god Baal defeats Sea, gains a palace on Mount Zaphon, and returns to power after his encounter with Death. They are the fullest surviving account of divine kingship from ancient Ugarit. In biblical studies the poems are especially valuable for interpreting language about raging waters, the divine mountain, and God's rule over a world threatened by forces pictured as monsters.

## Manuscripts and Date

The tablets were found at [[ugarit|Ugarit]], modern Ras Shamra. They are written in Ugaritic, a language related to Hebrew, in an alphabet whose signs were pressed into clay with a wedge-shaped tool. Modern editions conventionally number the six tablets 1.1–1.6 [@ktu3, 1.1–1.6]. A scribal note on the last identifies Ilimilku and names a king Niqmaddu.

Older studies often placed the writing of the tablets under Niqmaddu II in the fourteenth century BCE. Subsequent finds have strengthened a late-thirteenth-century dating under a later king named Niqmaddu, generally numbered III. This discussion concerns the surviving tablets. The stories may draw on older traditions transmitted in speech or performance, whose original form and date remain unknown.

## Contents

The usual reconstruction begins with the struggle between [[baal|Baal]], the storm god, and Yam, whose name means “Sea.” Kothar, the divine craftsman, provides weapons with which Baal overcomes Yam. Baal's victory displays his power, but a palace is still needed to express his standing among the gods. Anat and Athirat help secure El's consent for its construction [@parker-unp, 82, 94–141].

Kothar builds the palace on Mount Zaphon. A fire burns in the palace through a sequence of days, and on the seventh the work reaches its completion. The series of days marks the progress from construction to completion, a pattern worth comparing with other ancient stories of completed works and [[temple-building-accounts|temple building]]. The palace gives the victorious god a permanent seat from which to rule [@cos1, 1.86].

In the last part, Baal confronts Mot, whose name means “Death.” Baal is reported dead, and the gods mourn him. Athtar takes Baal's throne briefly, finds himself unequal to it, and descends. Anat attacks Mot, and Baal eventually returns to his throne. Baal and Mot later fight again, and Mot yields after receiving a warning from the sun goddess Shapshu [@parker-unp, 82, 141–164].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 27:1 describes [[leviathan|Leviathan]] as a fleeing and twisting serpent, closely matching terms used for a serpent in the Ugaritic poem. Ps 74:13–14 depicts God breaking the heads of sea monsters and Leviathan. Both texts use a West Semitic repertoire of divine conflict imagery, in which mastery of unruly waters can express divine rule. Exod 15:1–18 brings together a victory at the sea, a mountain sanctuary, and kingship, but its defeated foe is Pharaoh's army. Ps 93 joins divine kingship to mastery over rising waters.

In Ps 29, the divine voice thunders over waters and shakes Lebanon and Sirion, and YHWH is enthroned as king. Scholars have long proposed, following H. L. Ginsberg and Frank Moore Cross, that the psalm adapts an older hymn to a West Semitic storm god. Other scholars understand it as an Israelite composition using shared regional language. Its imagery is comparable to that of the cycle without establishing a direct relationship to any surviving tablet.

In Ps 48:2 (MT 48:3), the description of Zion can evoke Zaphon through a Hebrew expression for the far north. Ps 68:4 (MT 68:5) employs a title that can be compared to Baal's epithet “Rider of the Clouds,” although the Hebrew expression can also be understood as referring to the steppes. The unsuccessful ascent in Isa 14:12–15 has been compared to Athtar's failure, and the aged judge and cloud-borne figure in Dan 7:9–14 to El and Baal. Both are debated comparisons. Isa 25:8 pictures God swallowing Death, reversing the image of an all-devouring Death.

## Scholarly Issues

Damage to the tablets leaves gaps, especially in the earlier episodes. The sequence Sea, palace, and Death is a conventional reconstruction. The order of individual early fragments and whether the six tablets always belonged to one continuous work remain disputed.

Some older interpretations saw the cycle as a ritual script or read Baal's death and return as the regular passage of the agricultural seasons. Rain and fertility matter to Baal's character, but the tablets do not establish a calendar for the episodes or record how they were performed. The poems also explore how power becomes recognized kingship, through conflict, approval by other gods, and the establishment of a royal dwelling.
