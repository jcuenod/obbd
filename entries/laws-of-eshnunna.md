## Overview

The Laws of Eshnunna are a collection of legal rulings in Akkadian, a language of ancient Mesopotamia. They come from the kingdom of Eshnunna in what is now Iraq and probably took shape in the early eighteenth century BCE, before the better-known collection of Hammurabi. The laws deal with ordinary transactions and disputes, including theft, property entrusted for safekeeping, and injuries caused by animals. A rule for dividing the loss when one ox kills another closely resembles a law in Exodus, making it an important example of how biblical law compares with earlier Mesopotamian law.

## Manuscripts and Date

The collection is known mainly from two clay tablets excavated in the 1940s at Tell Harmal, ancient Shaduppum, in modern Baghdad. Another fragment was found at Tell Haddad, part of ancient Me-Turan. The copies are written in cuneiform, a script made by pressing wedge-shaped marks into clay. Albrecht Goetze published a full edition in 1956 after his earlier publication of the tablets.

The manuscripts belong to the Old Babylonian period, the early centuries of the second millennium BCE in Mesopotamia. The date of the surviving copies is not necessarily the date of the original collection. The damaged opening has been taken to point to the reign of Dadusha, king of Eshnunna, but the attribution is not certain [@roth-law].

## Contents

About sixty provisions survive, covering subjects such as prices, wages, marriage, and bodily injury. Many take the form of a particular case followed by the ruling that applies to it. This form is called casuistic law, or case law expressed as an if–then statement. Other [[ane-law-collections|ancient Near Eastern law collections]] use similar forms, including the Sumerian [[laws-of-lipit-ishtar|Laws of Lipit-Ishtar]] [@roth-law].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Section 53 and Exod 21:35 set out almost the same solution to a [[goring-ox|goring ox]] case: when one ox kills another, the owners divide the value of the surviving animal and the dead animal itself. Exodus explicitly calls for the live animal to be sold. In sections 54–55 and Exod 21:28–32, an owner who fails to restrain an ox known to gore can be held responsible when it kills a person. The remedies differ: Eshnunna prescribes payments in silver, whereas Exodus provides for the owner's death or a ransom in one case and payment to the enslaver when the victim is enslaved [@roth-law].

Sections 12–13 distinguish between daytime and nighttime intrusion, including someone caught in a house. They prescribe a silver payment for the daytime case and death for the nighttime case. Exod 22:2–3 (MT 22:1–2, the Hebrew numbering) likewise distinguishes night from day but asks whether the person who strikes and kills a thief incurs guilt for the death. Both laws make the time of the intrusion matter, but they use it to answer different legal questions [@roth-law].

The provisions on entrusted property in sections 36–37 include a sworn declaration before a god when property is lost together with the keeper's own goods. In Exod 22:7–8 (MT 22:6–7), the keeper comes before God if a thief cannot be found; the passage does not spell out the wording of any [[oaths-and-oath-formulas|oath]]. Another comparison concerns a structural hazard: section 58 assigns responsibility when a known dangerous wall collapses and kills someone, while Deut 22:8 requires a roof railing to prevent a fall and the resulting guilt. Section 26 and Deut 22:25–27 both prescribe death for a man who sexually assaults a woman pledged to another, but Deuteronomy explicitly clears the woman of guilt and explains why the setting matters [@roth-law].

## Scholarly Issues

The shared sequence and remedy in the ox case make it a particularly close parallel, but they do not show that an Israelite writer read a copy of the Eshnunna text. A common explanation is that recurring cases and rulings circulated among people who wrote and taught law. David P.
