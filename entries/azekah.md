## Overview

Azekah was a fortified town in the Shephelah, the foothills between Judah's central highlands and the coastal plain. Its mound, now called Tel ʿAzeqa, overlooks the Elah Valley. Archaeology documents occupation in the Bronze and Iron Ages, and both an Assyrian inscription and a Judahite letter name the town. Its position explains why it serves as a landmark in the biblical account of David and Goliath. It also helps explain why the town mattered in accounts of attacks on Judah by Assyria and Babylonia late in the period of the monarchy.

## Location and Identification

Tel ʿAzeqa stands on the northwestern side of the Elah Valley, above a route between the coastal plain and the hill country. Socoh lies farther along the valley, and the fortified site of [[khirbet-qeiyafa|Khirbet Qeiyafa]] stands above the valley as well. Biblical descriptions of the district and later records of the place-name support the identification of Azekah with the mound, also known as Tell Zakariya.

## Archaeological History

Frederick J. Bliss and R. A. Stewart Macalister excavated here in 1898–1899. The Lautenschläger Azekah Expedition, a collaboration involving Tel Aviv University and Heidelberg University, returned in 2012. Its excavations have exposed a building destroyed late in the twelfth century BCE with four people buried beneath the debris. The destruction belongs to the end of the settlement's Late Bronze Age occupation.

Evidence for the town in the era of the Judahite kingdom includes defensive remains and jar handles bearing royal stamps. The stamps show that goods in jars associated with the Judahite administration reached Azekah. Excavation on the summit has revealed successive Iron Age, Persian-period, and Hellenistic occupation levels. The sequence places different episodes of the town's history in the ground, although linking an individual wall or destruction deposit to a specific campaign requires additional evidence.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Joshua names Azekah in its list of foothill towns and in the account of the pursuit of the Amorite kings (Josh 15:33–35; 10:10–11). The story of David and Goliath locates the Philistine camp between Socoh and Azekah and puts Saul's troops in the Elah Valley (1 Sam 17:1–2). This geography makes sense of the setting without resolving the history of the battle. Chronicles includes Azekah among the towns fortified by Rehoboam (2 Chr 11:5–9).

The Azekah Inscription, preserved on two joined fragments from the British Museum's Assyrian tablet collections, describes an Assyrian attack on a town situated on a ridge. Nadav Naʾaman joined the fragments and attributed the attack to [[sennacherib-campaign-701|Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BCE]]. That widely followed interpretation would give a named example of the fortified towns whose capture is reported in 2 Kgs 18:13. Other scholars associate the tablet with [[sargon-ii-inscriptions|Sargon II]], for example with his campaign against Philistia in 712 BCE. The attribution and the original genre of the fragmentary document remain uncertain.

The last lines of [[lachish-letters|Lachish Letter 4]], a message written on a piece of pottery, report that its writer is watching for the signals of [[lachish|Lachish]] because Azekah cannot be seen [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 314–319]. Jer 34:7 names Azekah and Lachish as the only remaining fortified towns during the Babylonian attack. Some readers infer that Azekah had fallen by the time the letter was written. The writer's position may instead have prevented a view of Azekah or its signals. Azekah appears again among the Judahite settlements listed in Neh 11:25–30.
