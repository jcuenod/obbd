## Overview

The Siloam Inscription is a six-line Hebrew text cut into the rock inside a water tunnel in Jerusalem. It describes the meeting of two groups of workers and the flow of water from a spring to a pool. The inscription was found in 1880 and is now in Istanbul. Its writing and the tunnel's physical evidence place the work in the Iron Age. It gives a rare account of the engineering behind Jerusalem's water supply and is widely associated with the works attributed to Hezekiah in Kings and Chronicles.

## Description and Provenance

The inscription was discovered near the southern end of [[hezekiahs-tunnel|the Siloam Tunnel]], close to its outlet. The surface carrying the letters was cut from the wall in the nineteenth century and was broken in the removal. The pieces were taken to Constantinople. The Hebrew letters provide a prominent example of the [[paleo-hebrew-script|monarchic script]] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, Siloam Tunnel 1] [@ahituv-echoes, 19–24].

## Contents

The surviving text describes workers approaching each other from opposite directions. As the gap narrowed, a worker heard a voice calling from the other side. The teams then broke through. The last section gives figures for the length of the tunnel and height of rock above the workers, and reports the flow of water to the pool. The text names no ruler and emphasizes the action of the workers. Its position inside the tunnel differs from the prominent locations of many royal building inscriptions [@kai, no. 189] [@cos2, 2.28].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 20:20 gives [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] credit for bringing water into Jerusalem by a conduit. 2 Chr 32:30 describes diverting Gihon water toward the [[city-of-david|City of David]], and 2 Chr 32:2–4 connects water management with Assyrian pressure. Sir 48:17 recalls a tunnel cut in rock. These texts make the attribution to Hezekiah plausible. The inscription itself commemorates the breakthrough without specifying a royal sponsor or mentioning a siege.

Isa 22:9–11 describes the storage and collection of water in Jerusalem. Isa 8:6 refers to the gently flowing waters of Shiloah, often identified with an earlier channel. Neh 3:15 names the Pool of Shelah, and John 9:7 places a later episode at the [[pool-of-siloam|Pool of Siloam]]. These passages show the continuing importance of the area's water supply. The layout and use of individual pools and channels changed over time.

## Scholarly Issues

The reading of the script and the radiometric evidence for the tunnel converge on an Iron Age setting. One proposal dated the inscription to the Hasmonean period, while Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron argued from excavations that the tunnel may predate Hezekiah. The Hasmonean proposal has found little support. The fuller debate concerns when the conduit and inscription were made relative to late monarchic waterworks and what the inscriptions and samples can establish independently.
