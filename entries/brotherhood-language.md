## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern rulers could address one another as brothers to recognize a relationship between peers. The practice is well attested in diplomatic letters from the Late Bronze Age and in the treaty between Egypt and the Hittites. Biblical accounts use similar language when kings negotiate and when a prophet condemns Tyre for failing to honor a covenant of brothers. Other biblical uses concern peoples understood as kin, especially Israel and Edom. The setting of each passage helps establish whether brotherhood refers to political status, shared ancestry, or both.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] preserve correspondence between the king of Egypt and both major monarchs and local rulers. The kings of Babylonia and other great powers commonly address the pharaoh as brother. Local rulers who depended on Egypt employed phrases such as “my lord” and referred to themselves as servants. The terms marked the relative standing the sender claimed or conceded [@moran-amarna].

Brotherhood could imply duties of reciprocity, including sending gifts, exchanging envoys, or honoring agreements. The [[egyptian-hittite-peace-treaty|Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty]] speaks of peace and brotherhood between Ramesses II and Hattusili III. These were rulers whose treaty imposed mutual obligations. Calling one another brothers was a public claim to reciprocal standing even when their resources and interests differed [@beckman-hdt] [@cos2].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 1 Kgs 9:13 [[hiram-of-tyre|Hiram of Tyre]] calls Solomon his brother when questioning the quality of towns given to him. The term fits a relationship in which two rulers can negotiate and voice dissatisfaction. In 1 Kgs 20:32–33 the servants of Ben-hadad first report their master's plea for life using the language of service. Ahab's reference to Ben-hadad as a brother changes the terms of the encounter, and the servants promptly repeat the title.

In Amos 1:9 Tyre is condemned for handing people over to Edom and forgetting a covenant of brothers. Most interpreters understand this as a political agreement. A treaty between Israel and Tyre of the kind reflected in Kings is a possible setting, but Amos does not identify the parties more precisely. Whether the treaty originally concerned trade, protection, or another obligation remains open.

## Interpretive Significance

The comparison with Edom brings the meaning of kinship into focus. In Num 20:14, Moses calls Israel the brother of Edom while negotiating passage. Deut 2:4 describes the people of Seir as descendants of Esau and calls them Israel's brothers. Obad 10–12 condemns violence against a brother, Jacob, the ancestor who stands for Israel. These references present a political neighbor through a tradition of common descent.

Amos 1:11 also accuses Edom of pursuing its brother. The kinship language of other biblical passages favors understanding this as a reference to Israel or Judah as related peoples, though breach of a treaty has also been proposed. Much later, the claim in 1 Macc 12:21 that Jews and Spartans descended from Abraham uses shared ancestry as a reason for friendship between communities. It illustrates how diplomatic ties and assertions of kinship could be combined.
