## Overview

Families in Iron Age Judah sometimes buried their dead in chambers cut into bedrock. The bodies were laid on stone benches, some with shaped headrests. In many tombs, a cavity held bones gathered from earlier burials so that the benches could be used again. Examples around Jerusalem and in the Judahite countryside show how families used and revisited the same graves over generations. The tombs provide a setting for biblical stories about burial with parents and ancestors, while their varied forms and cost show that Judahites used more than one kind of grave.

## Archaeological Evidence

In a typical bench tomb, a doorway led into one or more chambers with raised platforms along the walls. Some benches had carved headrests, including examples at St. Étienne in Jerusalem with a shape compared to the Egyptian goddess Hathor's wig. After the flesh decayed, bones and associated objects could be moved into a repository, a cavity set aside for this purpose. This second placement is called secondary burial. Lamps, small jars, jewelry, and protective objects are among the furnishings found in such tombs. [@neaehl, 2:712–715] [@king-stager-life, 363–372]

A cemetery at Ketef Hinnom in Jerusalem included an extensive chamber and repository that yielded pottery, jewelry, and the rolled silver [[ketef-hinnom-amulets|Ketef Hinnom amulets]]. Other Jerusalem tombs, including that marked by the [[royal-steward-inscription|royal steward inscription]] in Silwan, display the resources some families or officials could devote to funerary architecture. The Silwan tombs vary in plan and include forms other than the standard bench chamber. Further examples of rock-cut tombs appear at Khirbet el-Qom west of Hebron, where the [[khirbet-el-qom-inscription|tomb inscriptions]] add personal names and blessing language to the architectural evidence. [@neaehl, 2:712–715] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 263–275, 507–510]

Creating a rock-cut chamber required access to suitable stone and labor, and furnished tombs give a partial view of the population. Lower-cost interments included simpler graves. In the later Second Temple period, rock-cut tombs often used narrow recesses extending into the chamber walls, a form known by the Hebrew name *kokh*. Later burials also include individual collections of bones in stone [[ossuaries|ossuaries]]. The Iron Age chambers often gathered remains in a shared cavity. [@mazar-archaeology, 520–526] [@neaehl, 2:749–755]

## In the Bible

Gen 23:1–20 tells of Abraham's purchase of a burial cave at Machpelah, and Jacob asks to be buried with his ancestors there in Gen 49:29–32. When Abraham dies, he is described as “gathered to his people” (Gen 25:8, ESV) before the text recounts his burial by his sons (Gen 25:9–10). Collecting relatives' bones in a common space makes the image concrete. The phrase itself is broader than the procedure, as the narrative uses it before describing what happened to Abraham's body.

The request that the old prophet's bones be laid beside the man of God's bones in 1 Kgs 13:30–31 illustrates the importance attached to proximity in death. In 2 Kgs 13:21 a body touches Elisha's bones after being thrown into his grave. Both scenes fit tombs that remained accessible after the first burial. Isa 22:16 describes Shebna carving out a tomb in the rock, whereas Jer 26:23 refers to the graves of the common people. The passages suggest differences in burial arrangements without describing a single standard for every family.
