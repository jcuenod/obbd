## Overview

Biblical narratives portray childbirth as an event involving women with practical knowledge, families with responsibilities for newborns, and risks to mother and child. They also draw on familiar acts of infant care to describe dependence and protection. Egyptian and Mesopotamian sources show that physical support and protective rituals could accompany labor and infancy. These sources clarify some practices behind the biblical allusions, while the customs and vocabulary of each place and period require separate attention.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

An excavated painted mudbrick from Abydos in Egypt has been interpreted as a birth brick. Its imagery associates a mother and child with protective figures. Egyptian evidence makes a delivery employing brick supports plausible. The excavated object and its images also show the importance of ritual protection. Mesopotamian amulets associated with [[lamashtu-pazuzu-amulets|Lamashtu and Pazuzu]] illustrate another response to dangers understood to threaten pregnant women and infants [@penn-abydos-birth-brick] [@mcgeough-birth-bricks] [@flynn-children].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 35:16–20 the midwife speaks to Rachel during a difficult labor. Rachel dies after the birth. Exod 1:15–21 portrays midwives as people whose access to women giving birth makes them central to Pharaoh's plan and to its failure. The rare expression in Exod 1:16 has been understood in connection with supports used during birth. Its relationship to terminology for a potter's equipment and the force of the Egyptian comparison are disputed [@mcgeough-birth-bricks].

Ezek 16:4–5 describes an abandoned newborn by listing care that was withheld. Cutting the cord, washing, rubbing with salt, and wrapping the infant mark what someone might ordinarily do for a child. The passage is an extended metaphor for Jerusalem, and its list cannot establish a uniform regimen. In 1 Sam 1:22–24 Hannah waits until Samuel is weaned before taking him to the sanctuary. Isa 49:15 uses the bond between a nursing woman and her child to express the strength of divine remembrance.

## Interpretive Significance

Stories and metaphors preserve different kinds of information. Rachel's story portrays the danger of a particular delivery, while Ezekiel uses familiar actions to make neglect visible. Infant care also ties the child to the household that raises and feeds it. The evidence for protective beliefs is clearest where an object or text gives those beliefs an explicit role.
