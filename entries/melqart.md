## Overview

Melqart, whose name means “king of the city,” was the principal god of Tyre. Phoenician trading and colonial networks carried his worship westward, while an Aramaic inscription also attests his cult inland near Aleppo. Greek speakers frequently identified him with Heracles. These identifications give context to references to Tyrian religion in Kings and 2 Maccabees and to imagery on later coins. The evidence establishes Melqart's importance at Tyre, while the proposal that he was the Baal worshipped by Ahab and Jezebel remains debated.

## Sources and Iconography

The Phoenician name expresses Melqart's relation to [[tyre|Tyre]]. His cult also appears at Carthage and Gades and in Phoenician inscriptions elsewhere. A bilingual dedication from Malta identifies the god called Melqart in Phoenician with Heracles in Greek. The [[melqart-stele|Aramaic stele from Breij]] shows that a king in inland Syria could dedicate a monument to the Tyrian god. Melqart's titles and representations vary with language and setting [@kai] [@ddd].

Herodotus 2.44 describes a temple of Heracles at Tyre with two notable pillars. Josephus quotes Menander of Ephesus about [[hiram-of-tyre|Hiram]] and the celebration of Heracles' awakening (*Against Apion* 1.119). The nature of this rite and how it changed over time remain uncertain. The later silver coinage of Tyre often bears the head of Melqart in a form associated with Heracles, alongside civic designs and inscriptions.

## In the Hebrew Bible

1 Kgs 16:31–32 depicts Ahab marrying [[jezebel|Jezebel]] and erecting an altar for Baal in Samaria. Her Tyrian connections make Melqart a plausible identification for this [[baal|Baal]]. Baal-Shamem, a god whose name means “lord of heaven,” and a local manifestation of Baal are other proposals. The wording in Kings does not provide a more specific title. In 1 Kgs 18:27 Elijah mocks the lack of a reply by suggesting that Baal is asleep. A link to Melqart's awakening festival is possible, but the joke also works as a broader objection to a god's silence.

In 1 Kgs 7:15–22, [[jachin-and-boaz|Jachin and Boaz]] flank the temple entrance in Jerusalem. Herodotus's report of paired pillars offers a comparison for Tyrian temple furnishings. The times, materials, and functions of the particular pillars cannot be assumed to match.

## Scholarly Issues

In 2 Macc 4:18–20, Jason sends a contribution intended for sacrifice to Heracles at games in Tyre. This is a clearer example of the Greek name used in a Tyrian cultic setting. Matt 17:24–27 describes payment of the temple tax. Tyrian silver bearing Melqart's portrait circulated widely and was used for temple payments. The passage identifies coin values and leaves the exact mint or imagery unspecified. These later uses provide evidence for the continuing recognition of the god, with little direct bearing on the identity of Ahab's Baal.
