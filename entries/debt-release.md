## Overview

Debt release in the Hebrew Bible includes several distinct laws. Deuteronomy calls for the periodic remission of loans and for releasing Hebrew servants after six years of service. Leviticus prescribes a Jubilee when inherited land returns and Israelite debt-servants go free. Mesopotamian kings also issued edicts canceling certain debts and undoing some consequences of financial distress, especially in the early second millennium BCE. These edicts provide a comparison for the problems the biblical laws address. The Bible gives their timing and rationale within its own account of the land and its people.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian rulers sometimes intervened when loans, taxes, or pledges had placed households under severe pressure. A reform attributed to Uru-inimgina of Lagash is an early example of royal claims to release people from burdens. In the Old Babylonian period, kings used terms such as *mīšarum* (“justice”) and *andurārum* (“release”) for acts that set aside specified obligations. The [[edict-of-ammi-saduqa|edict of Ammi-ṣaduqa]] canceled some debts and arrears and undid certain forms of dependency, while protecting genuine commercial transactions. Such edicts were issued at a king's discretion, often in connection with accession, and their provisions varied. [@cos2, 2.134, 2.152] [@anet, 526–528]

The Hebrew word *dĕrôr* (“release” or “liberty”) is commonly compared with Akkadian *andurārum*. The two terms show how related words could be used for restoring freedom or rights. The comparison leaves open how particular Israelite and Judahite laws took shape. It helps place the laws within a broader regional tradition of addressing the social consequences of indebtedness.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 15:1–11 calls on creditors to release claims on fellow Israelites every seventh year. It also anticipates reluctance to lend as the year approaches and addresses that problem directly. The [[sabbatical-year|sabbatical fallow]] is related by its seven-year rhythm and concerns the use of fields. The release of Hebrew servants in Exod 21:2–6 and Deut 15:12–18 counts six years from the beginning of service. Deuteronomy includes provisions for sending the released person away with supplies. These laws concern personal service and differ in timing and terms from the periodic release of loans.

Lev 25:8–17 calls for Jubilee after seven cycles of seven years. Holdings return to their families, and the price of land use reflects the number of remaining harvests. Lev 25:39–55 also limits dependence for Israelite debt-servants and presents release in the Jubilee. The chapter gives God ultimate ownership of the land and claims the Israelites as his servants. This rationale explains why land and personal service are subject to limits on permanent transfer. See [[land-redemption|land redemption]]. [@abd, 3:1025–1030]

## Interpretive Significance

Jer 34:8–22 describes a release of Hebrew slaves under Zedekiah and its subsequent reversal. Neh 5:1–13 depicts Nehemiah securing the restoration of fields and other property amid a debt crisis. These responses to immediate conditions resemble discretionary reform. Neither narrative securely documents a regular Jubilee. The extent to which the Jubilee was ever implemented as written remains debated. Later rabbinic law attributes to Hillel the *prozbul*, a procedure that transferred a lender's claim to a court so collection could continue after the year of release. The tradition illustrates concern that remission might discourage lending.

Isa 61:1–2 uses the language of release and a year of divine favor, and Luke 4:18–19 applies such language to Jesus' mission. The Qumran work usually called 11QMelchizedek also combines Jubilee and release language in its description of a future deliverance. These uses show how rules about property and freedom could furnish language for hopes extending beyond an immediate debt crisis.
