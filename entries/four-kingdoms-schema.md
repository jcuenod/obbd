## Overview

Daniel 2 and 7 present history as a succession of four kingdoms that ends when God establishes enduring rule. A statue made of different materials and a series of beasts from the sea tell this story in different ways. On the usual historical reading, the kingdoms are Babylon, Media, Persia, and Greece, with the final crisis focused on the Greek Seleucid king Antiochus IV. Later Jewish and Christian readers applied the fourth kingdom to Rome. Their interpretations show how the same pattern could explain successive experiences of imperial rule.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Ancient writers sometimes described the transfer of world rule through an ordered list of empires. Herodotus recounts how the Medes broke away from Assyrian rule and how Cyrus then defeated the Median king (Histories 1.95–130). Ctesias also recounts the succession of Assyria, Media, and Persia. Aemilius Sura, quoted in Velleius Paterculus 1.6.6, extends that sequence through Macedonia to Rome. The fourth Sibylline Oracle (4.49–151) places Assyria, Media, Persia, and Macedonia in ten generations before turning to Rome.

Other works offer comparanda for specific features. The Babylonian Dynastic Prophecy describes changes of rule in the guise of predictions, carrying the sequence into the rise of Macedonian power. Hesiod uses gold, silver, bronze, and iron to distinguish ages of humanity, with an age of heroes between bronze and iron (Works and Days 106–201). The Middle Persian *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* portrays eras as branches of a tree made of different metals. Its surviving form is much later than Daniel, and scholars debate the age of the underlying material and any direction of influence.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Nebuchadnezzar sees a statue with a gold head, silver chest and arms, bronze belly and thighs, iron legs, and feet of iron and clay (Dan 2:31–45). Daniel identifies Nebuchadnezzar with the head and describes three kingdoms to follow. A stone cut without human hands shatters the statue and becomes a mountain filling the earth. The dream ends with the God of heaven establishing a kingdom that cannot be destroyed.

In Dan 7:1–27, four beasts emerge from the sea. The final beast has iron teeth and horns, including a small horn that speaks arrogantly and attacks the holy ones. The heavenly court judges the beast, and rule passes to one like a human being and, in the interpretation, to the holy ones. The horn's persecution gives particular weight to the historical identification with [[antiochus-iv|Antiochus IV]]. The place of [[medes|Media]] as a distinct kingdom fits Daniel's presentation of [[darius-the-mede|Darius the Mede]]. Daniel 8:20–22 instead represents Media and Persia together as the horns of a ram and explicitly names Greece as the power that defeats it.

## Interpretive Significance

The imagery of [[composite-beasts-daniel|Daniel's beasts]] allows the text to speak of the violence of empire and also of its end. The schemes in chapters 2 and 7 place that end in God's hands. Readers living under Rome often paired Media and Persia, counted Greece third, and made Rome the fourth kingdom. In 2 Esd 12:10–12, the eagle vision reinterprets Daniel's fourth kingdom in imagery generally understood as Roman. Rev 13:1–2 combines features from Daniel's beasts into a single creature, applying the imagery to a new imperial setting.
