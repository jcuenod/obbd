## Overview

Greek inscriptions from Thessalonica and other Macedonian cities name officials called politarchs. One well-known example was preserved at the Vardar Gate of Thessalonica, and others come from different dates and civic settings. Acts uses the title for the officials who hear an accusation against Jason and other members of the community. The inscriptions establish that the author’s vocabulary fits Macedonian civic practice. They also show why different local titles can appear in neighboring cities within the same Roman province.

## Manuscripts and Date

The evidence consists chiefly of Greek inscriptions on stone. The best-known Thessalonian example is the inscribed block formerly set in the Vardar Gate, now in the British Museum. The gate was demolished in the nineteenth century and the block was preserved. Published proposals differ on its date, and a second-century CE assignment is common. Other inscriptions place the title in use before or during the first century CE. ## Contents

The gate inscription dates its record by naming a group of men serving as politarchs. It also names other civic officers, including a gymnasiarch, an official responsible for the gymnasium. The lists differ across inscriptions, and the number of politarchs could vary. The term literally evokes governing a city. Its precise responsibilities in each period must be recovered from the civic context and the actions attributed to the magistrates.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Acts 17:6, 8 calls the Thessalonian authorities *politarchai*. The accusation concerns sheltering men regarded as a threat to civic order and to Caesar’s decrees. In the account the city’s magistrates hear the charge and take security. The epigraphic evidence establishes that the title is an appropriate local designation. It supplies no record of Jason, the charge made in Acts, or the amount of any security given.

Acts 16:20–22, 35–38 uses a different term for magistrates at Philippi. Philippi was a Roman colony, and its civic arrangements and conventional terminology differed from those of Thessalonica. The contrast helps readers see how a Greek narrative can use context-specific titles within one region. First Thessalonians 2:14 recounts opposition experienced by the community without naming a civic office. It provides a different viewpoint on conflict in the city.

## Scholarly Issues

Inscriptions preserve the title even though surviving narrative literature uses it rarely. The distribution cautions against judging local practice from literary frequency alone. It also cautions against extrapolation from the Vardar Gate block. The inscriptions establish that a group of officials bore the title. Specific powers, the number of officers, and relationships among civic offices could change over time.
