## Overview

A Greek inscription on a marble statue base from Thyatira honors Claudia Ammion. The city's dyers paid tribute to a woman whose public roles included priesthood and the direction of contests. The monument is dated approximately to the middle of the first century CE. It gives a local example of an occupational group participating in civic recognition and of the connection between public office and imperial honors. It also supplies context for the Thyatira named in Acts and Revelation.

## Description and Provenance

The inscription comes from Thyatira in western Asia Minor and is published as TAM V 972. It belongs to the practice of marking public honors in durable inscriptions on statue bases. The designation of the dedicators as dyers identifies them by occupation. The commemorated woman is named, and the surviving text records the services for which she is praised [@agrw-129].

## Contents

Claudia Ammion is identified as a priestess of the Augusti and as high priestess of the city. The text praises the way she performed the office of director of contests, emphasizing the scale and character of her service. It gives the dyers a visible role in commemorating that service. For the wider patterns involved, see [[patronage-and-benefaction]] and [[imperial-cult]].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Acts 16:14 introduces Lydia as a seller of purple goods from Thyatira. The inscription illustrates the presence of organized dye workers in her city of origin. The occupational designation in Acts and the collective designation on the base are separate pieces of evidence. The base supplies no information about Lydia's own business or affiliations.

Rev 2:18–29 addresses the assembly at Thyatira and includes a dispute about food offered to idols. The base places a trade group within a network of civic and religious honors. It records no association meal and does not say that members had to participate in cult activity. Such requirements would need evidence beyond this monument.
