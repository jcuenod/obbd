## Overview

A Nazirite is a person set apart to God. The law in Numbers describes a vow that can be undertaken by a man or woman and marks it by abstention from grape products, uncut hair, and avoidance of contact with the dead. Samson's story presents a consecration from birth. Other biblical and later Jewish texts show how this practice was understood and adapted in different settings. The rules for the vow also provide background for accounts in Acts that associate cutting hair and temple offerings with a public act of dedication.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Ritual cutting or dedication of hair occurs in other cultures. Lucian's account of the sanctuary at Hierapolis describes offerings of young people's hair (On the Syrian Goddess 60). In this setting cutting and depositing hair marks a relationship with the sanctuary and a change in personal status. The place and date differ from those of the biblical material. The comparison helps explain how hair could serve as a visible sign of consecration and its removal as a ritual act.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Num 6:1–21 describes a [[vows-and-votive-offerings|vow]] undertaken for a period of dedication. The Nazirite must avoid wine and other products of the vine, allow the hair to grow, and avoid becoming unclean through proximity to the dead. Accidental defilement requires ritual repair and restarting the period. At its end the Nazirite brings offerings, shaves the consecrated hair, and puts it on the fire under the well-being offering. The hair marks the consecration over its duration and has a specified place in ending it.

In Judg 13:4–7 Samson's mother is instructed to abstain from certain things, and her son is to be a Nazirite from the womb. In Judg 16:17 Samson identifies his uncut hair as a mark of his dedication. Hannah promises that no razor will come upon Samuel's head (1 Sam 1:11). The Greek tradition adds language about abstention from wine, and the Qumran Samuel manuscript 4QSamᵃ explicitly calls Samuel a Nazirite in its form of 1 Sam 1:22. These accounts portray long-term consecration. The relation between these narrative traditions and the law in Numbers remains debated.

Amos 2:11–12 pairs Nazirites with prophets as people God has raised up and condemns forcing Nazirites to drink wine. In Jer 35:6–10 the Rechabites abstain from wine because of an ancestral command. The parallel illustrates the visibility of such abstention, while the Rechabites follow a group rule of a different kind.

## Interpretive Significance

1 Macc 3:49 refers to Nazirites who had completed their days, and [[josephus|Josephus]] recounts that Agrippa helped Nazirites shave their heads (Ant. 19.294). The Mishnah later records a range of regulations and questions in tractate Nazir. These sources illuminate the practical cost and obligations of the observance.

In Acts 18:18 Paul has his hair cut at Cenchreae because of a vow. Acts 21:23–26 describes him joining four men in purification and paying their costs so they can shave their heads. The second account in particular invites comparison with the completion rites in Numbers. Luke 1:15 links John's coming role with abstention from wine and strong drink. The passage shares language with biblical accounts of consecration and leaves John's precise status unstated.
