## Overview

The earliest history of YHWH's worship has to be reconstructed from scattered inscriptions and biblical traditions. Egyptian place lists may preserve a form related to the divine name, while the ninth-century BCE Mesha Stele clearly names YHWH. Some biblical poems portray the deity coming from regions south of Israel and Judah. Narratives connect Moses with a Midianite priest. The origin of the name, the routes by which worship spread and YHWH's relationship to the West Semitic god El are distinct questions, each with incomplete evidence.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Egyptian temple lists at [[soleb-shasu-inscription|Soleb]] under Amenhotep III and Amarah West under Ramesses II include a group or district of [[shasu|Shasu]] designated *yhwꜣ*. The similarity to YHWH is suggestive. The Egyptian entries are geographical and political designations. It remains uncertain whether this one contains a divine name and where the group or place was located. Proposed references in Eblaite personal names and an isolated Ugaritic spelling *yw* have not supplied secure attestations. [@ddd] [@ktu3]

The ninth-century BCE [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]] refers to vessels of YHWH removed from Nebo. The inscription clearly treats YHWH as a deity with objects belonging to his cult. Around the late ninth or early eighth century BCE, the [[kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions]] invoke YHWH of Samaria and YHWH of Teman. The titles establish that geographic associations could matter to worshipers. Teman provides evidence for a southern association by that time. [@cos2, 2.23, 2.47] [@ahituv-echoes]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 33:2 and Judg 5:4–5 depict YHWH appearing from Sinai, Seir, Paran or Edom. Hab 3:3, 7 pairs a coming from Teman and Paran with trembling in Cushan and Midian. Ps 68:7–8 (MT 68:8–9) depicts the God of Sinai marching through the wilderness. These poems use the imagery of a divine march to portray YHWH's relation to Israel. Scholars differ on whether they preserve a memory of a southern cult, refer to the wilderness deliverance or combine both associations. [@ddd] [@abd]

Exod 3:1, 13–15 places the revelation of the name at Horeb during Moses's association with Jethro. In Exod 18:1–12, Jethro blesses YHWH and presents sacrifices. Judg 1:16 and 4:11 connect Kenites with Moses's kin by marriage. Exod 6:2–3 portrays the patriarchs as knowing God as El Shaddai while Gen 4:26 places invocation of YHWH much earlier. These accounts explain knowledge of God and the name in different ways. [@abd] [@ddd]

## Interpretive Significance

The [[midianites-and-kenites|Midianite-Kenite hypothesis]] proposes that groups connected with the south transmitted YHWH's worship to Israel. Its evidence includes the literary traditions and the inscriptions, whose bearing on the routes or agents of transmission remains debated. Other reconstructions emphasize northern and central highland settings in the development of Israelite religion. Biblical texts also ascribe characteristics and titles associated with [[el|El]] to YHWH. The merging or sharing of these attributes is well documented at the literary level. The historical stages of the relationship remain difficult to reconstruct. [@ddd] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig]
