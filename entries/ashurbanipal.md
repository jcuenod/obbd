## Overview

Ashurbanipal became king of Assyria after Esarhaddon's death in 669 BCE and ruled from Nineveh into the later seventh century BCE. His inscriptions record campaigns in Egypt and Elam and a war against his brother, who ruled Babylon. His reign also produced carved palace reliefs and a major collection of clay tablets. He is generally identified with Osnappar, named as the ruler who resettled peoples in Samaria in Ezra. His conquest of Thebes and his records about Manasseh of Judah provide other points of contact with the Hebrew Bible.

## Extrabiblical Sources

[[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] arranged for Ashurbanipal to succeed him in Assyria and for his other son Shamash-shum-ukin to rule Babylon. The brothers held their respective thrones until Shamash-shum-ukin rebelled against Ashurbanipal. The revolt began in 652 BCE, and Babylon fell in 648 BCE. Ashurbanipal's royal accounts also claim victory over the army of the Kushite king ruling Egypt and the looting of [[thebes|Thebes]] around 664–663 BCE. They report campaigns against Elam in which Assyrian forces plundered [[susa|Susa]] and carried away statues and other valuables [@anet, 294–301].

The [[rassam-cylinder|Rassam Cylinder]] and other clay prisms preserve versions of the king's campaigns. His reliefs show events selected and arranged to celebrate royal power. The tablets assembled in the [[library-of-ashurbanipal|collections at Nineveh]] preserve literature, scholarly works, and texts used by ritual specialists. Ashurbanipal's inscriptions unusually claim training in scribal arts. The claims present an ideal of the learned king, while the extent of his own reading and writing is difficult to establish.

## Biblical Portrayal

In Ezra 4:9–10 the writers of a letter report that a ruler called Osnappar settled people in Samaria and elsewhere west of the Euphrates. The account includes people connected with Susa and Elam. Scholars usually identify Osnappar with Ashurbanipal because the form of the name is compatible with a transmitted version of his Assyrian name and the resettlement fits the setting of his Elamite campaigns. The letter refers back to the deportation from a Persian-period setting [@abd, 1:494–495].

Nah 3:8–10 invokes the defeat of the Egyptian city of Thebes to warn Nineveh. It gives no Assyrian royal name, but the sack recorded under Ashurbanipal supplies the usual historical reference. Ashurbanipal's inscriptions also list [[manasseh|Manasseh of Judah]], the ruler introduced in 2 Kgs 21:1, among western kings who supplied support for the Egyptian campaign [@anet, 294–295]. Esarhaddon had earlier named Manasseh among the rulers summoned to aid an Assyrian building project [@rinap4, no. 1 v 54–vi 1].

## Historical Assessment

In 2 Chr 33:10–13, Assyrian commanders take Manasseh to Babylon, and the king repents and is restored. Some have associated the reported detention with the Babylonian revolt. No known Assyrian account confirms that episode. The story's place in Chronicles highlights the connection the writer draws between repentance and restoration.

Business documents from Nippur attest Ashurbanipal's reign into 631 BCE. A later inscription from the family of the Babylonian king Nabonidus gives a longer reign that would extend it to 627 BCE. The sparse evidence for the last years and the succession leaves the date and circumstances of his death uncertain. Modern editions often use around 631 BCE as the end of his reign.
