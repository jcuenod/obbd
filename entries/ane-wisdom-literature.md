## Overview

“Wisdom literature” is a modern name for ancient texts that teach sound conduct or reflect on difficult questions about life. From the third millennium BCE onward, scribes in Egypt, Mesopotamia and the Levant copied instructions, proverbs and dialogues of this kind. These works help explain the forms and interests of Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes. Some comparisons involve a widely shared concern such as undeserved suffering, while the close wording and sequence of an Egyptian instruction and part of Proverbs point to a more direct literary relationship.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Sumerian scribes collected short proverbs and copied the *Instructions of Shuruppak*, in which a father advises his son. In Egypt, the [[instruction-of-ptahhotep|Instruction of Ptahhotep]] teaches restraint in speech and conduct in the company of others. The [[instruction-of-amenemope|Instruction of Amenemope]] likewise uses the voice of a teacher to commend self-control and warn against exploiting vulnerable people. A fifth-century BCE Aramaic copy of [[ahiqar|Ahiqar]] from Elephantine combines a court tale about a wise official with a collection of sayings. [@cos1, 1.174, 1.176] [@ael, 1:61–80, 2:146–163] [@tad, C1.1]

Other compositions test ideas through speeches or dialogue. The [[babylonian-theodicy|Babylonian Theodicy]] presents an exchange between a sufferer and a friend over how divine justice fits human experience. In [[ludlul-bel-nemeqi|Ludlul Bel Nemeqi]], a man passes through affliction and recovery, recounting his ordeal in a poem praising Marduk. The [[dialogue-of-pessimism|Dialogue of Pessimism]] has a servant argue for and against a series of activities proposed by his master. Scribes transmitted very different kinds of writing under conditions that often remain uncertain. [@lambert-bwl, 63–91]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Proverbs includes instruction in a parent's voice (Prov 1:8–9) as well as sayings arranged in named collections (Prov 25:1 and 30:1). The closest Egyptian comparison is with the sayings of the wise in Prov 22:17–24:22, especially the opening portion through 23:11. Amenemope and Proverbs both discuss treatment of poor people, caution around hot-tempered people and restraint at a ruler's table. Most scholars understand the Hebrew text as drawing on Amenemope, given the number, wording and sequence of the correspondences. How the Egyptian work reached a Hebrew writer is debated, as is the reading “thirty” in Prov 22:20, which would recall Amenemope's thirty chapters. [@ael, 2:146–163] [@abd, 5:515–519]

Job shares with the Babylonian Theodicy a dialogue about suffering and justice. The plot and speeches of Job develop that problem in their own ways, and the comparison establishes a shared question and literary form. Ecclesiastes can likewise be read alongside Near Eastern reflections on the limits of success and knowledge. Its advice to enjoy food, drink and life with a loved one (Eccl 9:7–9) resembles counsel given to Gilgamesh in an Old Babylonian version of the epic. [@lambert-bwl, 63–91] [@george-gilgamesh, 1:55–56, 278–79]

## Interpretive Significance

“Wisdom literature” groups works that differ in subject, setting and form. Will Kynes argues that applying the label to Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes can obscure their relationships with other biblical books. W. G. Lambert likewise draws attention to the difficulty of fitting Akkadian works within a category that modern scholars have developed partly from reading the Bible. The term remains useful for asking comparative questions when the particular form and evidence for each comparison are specified.

The figure of Wisdom speaking beside God at creation (Prov 8:22–31) has been compared with [[maat|Maat]], both a concept of right order and an Egyptian goddess. The comparison helps illustrate how an idea could be portrayed as a figure, while the proposal that Maat directly influenced the biblical portrayal remains debated. The close correspondences with Amenemope offer a different kind of evidence for contact. [@ddd, 534–535] [@ael]
