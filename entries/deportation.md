## Overview

The Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian empires forcibly moved people from conquered lands. The practice served several aims, including weakening centers of resistance, relocating skilled workers, and settling people where the state needed labor. Assyrian sources describe transfers into and out of conquered regions, while Babylonian evidence for Judah emphasizes removal to Babylonia. These policies provide the historical setting for accounts of Israel's fall and Judah's exile. Records from Babylonia also show how some displaced families acquired obligations, formed communities, and remained there after the end of Babylonian rule.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Assyrian kings describe transferring groups between provinces and into the imperial center. Royal texts count people and distinguish some of the roles assigned to them. Officials could settle people in farming areas, employ trained personnel, or incorporate soldiers into imperial forces. In some cases displaced people were brought into places from which others had been removed. This process both punished resistance and gave the state a way to redistribute people and skills. [@rinap1] [@rinap3-1] [@cos2]

Bustenay Oded extrapolated an empire-wide total of about 4.4 million people from figures in Assyrian records, with substantial uncertainty. The figure is a statistical estimate from a partial and uneven record. The numbers in the royal texts can themselves differ across copies. Sargon II's texts commonly give figures of 27,280 or 27,290 for people taken from Samaria, with other readings attested as well. These numbers describe a large removal but supply no census of those who remained. [@cos2]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 2 Kgs 15:29, Tiglath-pileser III takes northern and Transjordanian towns and deports their people. After the [[fall-of-samaria|fall of Samaria]], 2 Kgs 17:6 names destinations including settlements in Mesopotamia and Media. Second Kings also describes people brought into Samaria from elsewhere (2 Kgs 17:24). The Assyrian royal evidence and the biblical narratives agree on population transfers while emphasizing different details. Settlement and material evidence indicate that part of the local population remained. Later groups in the region also have histories beyond the account in Kings. See [[samaritan-origins|Samaritan origins]]. [@rinap1] [@cos2] [@mazar-archaeology]

The Babylonian conquest of Jerusalem in 597 BCE led to the removal of Jehoiachin and other members of the court, soldiers, and craft workers (2 Kgs 24:14–16). A further deportation followed the later fall of Jerusalem (2 Kgs 25:11). Jer 52:28–30 lists three transfers with a different set of figures. The differences may involve varying ways of counting or a different set of people covered by the lists. Accounts of the [[babylonian-exile|Babylonian exile]] concentrate on movement to Babylonia, and there is less evidence for replacing the population of Judah through large transfers into the province. [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles]

## Interpretive Significance

Jer 29:4–7 urges deportees to build homes, cultivate gardens, and seek the welfare of the place where they live. The [[al-yahudu-tablets|Āl-Yāhūdu tablets]] record transactions involving Judean families in rural Babylonia, and the later [[murashu-archive|Murašû archive]] provides further evidence of Judeans in the region. The [[jehoiachin-ration-tablets|Jehoiachin ration tablets]] document support for some members of the deported royal household. These records illuminate a range of experiences, from proximity to the court to work tied to local land and obligations. Archaeological evidence for damage and decline in Judah also differs by locality. Destruction and displacement could be severe while communities persisted in parts of the land. [@anet] [@neaehl]
