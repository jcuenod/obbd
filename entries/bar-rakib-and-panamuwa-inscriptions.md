## Overview

Panamuwa and Bar-Rakib were kings of Samʾal, an eighth-century BCE kingdom centered at Zincirli in what is now southern Türkiye. Their royal monuments include a statue of the storm god Hadad, a memorial to Panamuwa II, and inscriptions commissioned by his son Bar-Rakib about his reign and palace. The texts present kingship as a bond linking the local dynasty, its gods, and its Assyrian overlord. They offer a close regional comparison for the language of loyalty in King Ahaz's dealings with Assyria and for the use of monuments to preserve the memory of a dynasty.

## Description and Provenance

The monuments come from Samʾal and its surroundings. The inscribed statue of Hadad commissioned by Panamuwa I was found at Gerçin, outside the capital. Another statue preserves the memorial Bar-Rakib commissioned for his father Panamuwa II. Bar-Rakib also left inscriptions and reliefs associated with his own reign at Zincirli. Panamuwa I's statue inscription and the memorial of Panamuwa II are numbered 214 and 215 in the standard West Semitic corpus, while Bar-Rakib's own inscriptions begin at 216 [@kai, nos. 214–216].

## Contents

Panamuwa I's Hadad inscription directs a future ruler to remember him when offering sacrifice. The ruler is to invoke the soul of Panamuwa so that it may eat and drink with Hadad. The passage supplies evidence for rites that sustained the memory of a dead king in the presence of a god. The [[katumuwa-stele|Katumuwa Stele]] offers a comparison from the same kingdom for an offering made on behalf of a deceased person's soul.

Bar-Rakib's memorial describes the violent death of Panamuwa II's father Bar-Ṣur and seventy of his relatives. It credits the Assyrian king with putting Panamuwa II on his father's throne. Later, Panamuwa died in the camp of [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]], whose forces mourned him. His body was brought home. The episode probably belongs to the campaign against Damascus in 733–732 BCE, although the text gives no explicit date [@kai, nos. 214–216] [@cos2, 2.37].

In his own inscriptions, Bar-Rakib says that he ran at the wheel of the Assyrian king and credits his position to his own and his father's righteousness. His account claims distinction through faithful service. Assyrian evidence independently records the relationship between the two kingdoms. In a tribute list usually associated with 738 BCE, a king named Panammû of Samʾal appears alongside Rezin of Damascus and Menahem of Samaria [@rinap1, no. 47 rev. 6′–9′].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ahaz appeals to Tiglath-pileser for assistance, describes himself as the Assyrian king's servant and son, and sends him silver and gold (2 Kgs 16:5–10). The Samʾal texts show another local dynasty describing its survival and advancement in terms of service to the same imperial power. The comparison illustrates the language and political logic of [[tribute-and-vassalage|tribute and vassalage]], while the two sources describe different occasions.

Bar-Rakib's appeal to his father's righteousness also recalls Solomon's statement that the throne was given to David's son because of David's upright conduct (1 Kgs 3:6). The account of Bar-Ṣur's relatives being killed in a struggle for power supplies a comparison for the reports of dynastic violence in Judg 9:5 and 2 Kgs 10:6–7. The repeated number seventy may be a conventional way of portraying a large royal family.

## Scholarly Issues

Both Panamuwa inscriptions use a local written language usually called Samʾalian. Bar-Rakib's inscriptions about his own reign use a form of Old Aramaic closer to the written language used elsewhere in the region. The difference within the work of one dynasty suggests that scribes could choose among literary and political registers. It contributes to the history of [[aramaic-language|written Aramaic]] in the Levant.
