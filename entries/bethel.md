## Overview

Bethel was a town in the central highlands north of Jerusalem, generally identified with Beitin. The Bible associates it with Jacob's encounter with God and later with a royal sanctuary of the northern kingdom of Israel. By the eighth century BCE the book of Amos depicts it as a center of royal authority, while Hosea criticizes its worship. Excavations document settlement at Beitin at different times, but the location of the royal sanctuary remains unknown. The site brings together very different biblical portrayals of one place, from an ancestral site of worship to a target of prophetic criticism.

## Location and Identification

The identification of Bethel with Beitin rests on the survival of a similar name and on the geography of the central highlands. Gen 12:8 places Abram between Bethel and [[ai|Ai]], with Bethel to the west. This fits the usual pairing of Beitin with et-Tell to its east. Edward Robinson argued for the Beitin identification in the nineteenth century. El-Bireh and other candidates have been proposed, but Beitin remains the standard identification. [@abd, 1:651–652, 1:710–712] [@neaehl, 1:192–194]

## Archaeological History

William F. Albright and James L. Kelso directed excavations in 1934 and during later seasons in the 1950s and 1960. The excavations found remains from the Bronze and Iron Ages. The recorded relationships between finds and building layers have left questions about the size of the settlement in particular periods. Reanalysis by Israel Finkelstein and Lily Singer-Avitz identified stronger activity in the earlier Iron Age and the eighth century BCE, weaker settlement in some phases, and sparse evidence for Babylonian or Persian-period occupation. Other scholars have argued for more continuity, and the limits of excavation complicate claims that Bethel served as a center for scribal activity during the exile. No excavated structure can securely be identified as Jeroboam's sanctuary. [@neaehl, 1:192–194]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 28:10–22 relates that Jacob saw a connection between heaven and earth in a dream and named the site Bethel, meaning “house of God.” He set up the stone used at his head as a pillar. Gen 35:1–15 returns to the site and associates it with an altar and a standing stone. Such [[massebot|standing stones]] had several roles in the region, including marking sacred places. The Genesis stories present Bethel as a place of divine encounter before the biblical accounts of kings and royal sanctuaries.

In 1 Kgs 12:28–33, Jeroboam sets up a golden calf at Bethel and another at [[dan|Dan]], providing northern sites of worship. Whether such images represented the deity or served as a support for an unseen presence remains debated. See [[bull-iconography|bull and calf imagery]]. In Amos 7:10–13 the priest Amaziah calls Bethel a royal sanctuary, and Hos 10:5 uses the critical name Beth-aven in connection with its calf worship. Kings later links its altar to Josiah's reforms (2 Kgs 23:15–20). [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 190–195]

Jer 48:13 compares Moab's disappointment in Chemosh to Israel's disappointment in Bethel. Some read Bethel there as the sanctuary, while others take it as the name of a deity. The divine name occurs in Esarhaddon's treaty with Baal of Tyre, and related forms occur in the Aramaic texts from Elephantine. Those sources make the second reading possible without settling which sense Jeremiah intended. [@saa2, 26] [@tad, A2.1, C3.15] [@ddd, 173–175]
