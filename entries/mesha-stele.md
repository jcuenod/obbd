## Overview

The Mesha Stele is a basalt monument erected by Mesha, king of Moab, around the middle of the ninth century BCE. Found at Dhiban in 1868, it records Mesha's victories, building works, and devotion to Chemosh. It names Omri of Israel and describes Moab's recovery of territory that Israel had controlled. The inscription gives an independent Moabite perspective on conflicts also remembered in Kings. It is also a major source for the Moabite language, the worship of Chemosh, and an early written attestation of the name YHWH.

## Description and Provenance

The monument was reported by Frederick Augustus Klein at Dhiban, ancient [[dibon|Dibon]]. It was broken after its discovery. Substantial original fragments were recovered, and a paper impression prepared on behalf of Charles Clermont-Ganneau before the breakage helped reconstruct damaged or missing portions. The restored stone is in the Louvre. The state of the original basalt and the quality of the impression matter for several disputed readings [@kai] [@ahituv-echoes].

## Contents

Mesha describes himself as king of [[moabites|Moab]] and celebrates [[chemosh|Chemosh]]. The god's anger explains the earlier domination of Moab by Israel, and his renewed favor explains Mesha's success. The inscription names [[omri|Omri]], king of Israel, and refers to his successor. It describes the recovery or seizure of towns, the treatment of their populations, the dedication of goods, and building work that reshaped Moab's settlements.

In the account of Nebo, Mesha reports taking objects associated with YHWH and carrying them before Chemosh. The divine name is clear, while the exact identification of the objects as vessels is a conventional rendering of the damaged context. The text also describes devoting people to Ashtar-Chemosh with a verb related to the Hebrew term [[herem|ḥērem]], which can refer to placing people or things under a ban for a god [@cos2] [@kai].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 1:1 and 3:4–27 associate the rebellion with [[ahab|Ahab's]] death and narrate a counterattack against Mesha. The stele associates Mesha's success with the period of Omri's dynasty and speaks of forty years of Israelite control. The number may be schematic, and the sources do not yield a single chronology simply by combining their statements. The campaign in Kings ends after Mesha sacrifices his son and Israel withdraws. The stele presents victories and building projects through Mesha's own account and gives no account of that sacrifice.

Num 21:29 calls Moab the people of Chemosh, and Judg 11:24 associates a people's possession of land with its deity. Josh 6:17–21 supplies a Hebrew comparison for devoting people or property during war. Num 32:34–38, Isa 15–16, and Jer 48:18–24 include towns also named in the monument, such as Dibon, Nebo, and Kiriathaim. Judg 3:12–30 gives an earlier biblical story of Israel and Moab contending for power. These comparisons show the importance of territory and divine favor in the regional political vocabulary.

## Scholarly Issues

The term *ʾrʾl* at Ataroth has been compared with a difficult expression in 2 Sam 23:20. Its sense remains uncertain. In the damaged line 31, André Lemaire proposed “House of David,” a possible dynastic name with relevance to the tradition of David's power over Moab in 2 Sam 8:2. Other scholars have suggested a name such as Balak or have read too few letters to identify the word. Imaging has sharpened the discussion without securing agreement. The [[tel-dan-stele|Tel Dan Stele]] provides separate evidence for the dynastic name.
