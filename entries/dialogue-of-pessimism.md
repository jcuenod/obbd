## Overview

The Dialogue of Pessimism is an Akkadian literary composition preserved on first-millennium BCE tablets. A master proposes one activity after another, and his slave supplies reasons to pursue each one. When the master changes his mind, the slave finds equally persuasive reasons to abandon it. Their exchanges include amusement, sacrifice, and public service, and they end with a question about what is good. The work provides a Mesopotamian comparison for the examination of human projects in Ecclesiastes, although its speakers respond to uncertainty in their own way.

## Manuscripts and Date

The dialogue survives in manuscripts from Assur and Nineveh and in a Babylonian version. The versions differ in wording and detail, which shows that the text could be adapted in transmission. The titles Dialogue of Pessimism and the Obliging Slave are modern designations. The first surviving copies belong to the first millennium BCE, and the date of composition is uncertain [@lambert-bwl, 139–143] [@cos1, 1.155].

## Contents

Most episodes follow the same pattern. The master calls the slave and announces an intention, such as going hunting, offering sacrifice, or lending money. The slave agrees and explains the advantages. The master then reverses his decision, and the slave confirms that choice too. The reasons often sound like conventional wisdom, but the sequence shows how readily such advice can be turned to suit opposing plans.

Near the end the conversation turns to the question of what is good. The slave speaks about the limits of what a person can reach or encompass. The master threatens to kill him, and the slave replies that his master would survive him by only three days. That final reply calls attention to the dependence of the master on the person whose advice he seemed free to use or discard [@lambert-bwl, 149].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Eccl 2:1–11 examines pleasure, large building projects, and accumulated wealth before judging that the gains are fleeting. Eccl 2:14–16 considers how death overtakes the wise and the foolish alike. The dialogue's treatment of repeated proposals and its reminder of mortality make a useful thematic comparison. Eccl 9:7–9 also commends joy in food, drink, and companionship, a sustained response that the master's exchanges do not offer.

Prov 30:4 and Deut 30:12–13 use questions about reaching heaven or crossing great distances. The Akkadian text uses related images of human limits. In Deuteronomy, the question explains that the command is already accessible. The similarity can be considered within a wider stock of proverbial imagery, and a direct relationship remains debated.

## Scholarly Issues

Some scholars read the work as satire, particularly because the slave's quick reversals expose the master's indecision. Others give more weight to its serious examination of what action can achieve in the face of death. It has also been read as a display of a scribe's ability to make opposing arguments. The final exchange can support both a comic reading and a darker one, which is part of the reason the modern title is open to question.
