## Overview

The Famine Stele is a hieroglyphic inscription carved into rock on Sehel Island near Aswan, at the south of ancient Egypt. The inscription was made in the Ptolemaic period, many centuries after the reign of King Djoser in which it sets its story. It describes seven years of famine following low Nile floods and traces relief to the god Khnum. Its royal dream, famine, and concern with temple revenues provide comparisons with the story of Joseph. The inscription also shows how dependence on the river could be expressed through a story about a king's obligations to a god.

## Description and Provenance

The inscription is cut into a boulder on Sehel near the first rocky stretch of the Nile, known as the First Cataract. It belongs to the era of the Ptolemies, the dynasty that governed Egypt after Alexander the Great. It speaks in the voice of Djoser, a ruler of Egypt's Third Dynasty in the third millennium BCE. The contrast between the text's date and its dramatic setting is central to its interpretation [@ael].

## Contents

The inscription describes an insufficient rise of the [[nile|Nile]] over seven successive years. Lack of grain and general distress make the disaster a matter of royal concern. Djoser consults Imhotep, who finds information about the source of the river and the god associated with it. The search directs the king's attention to Khnum's sanctuary in the region of [[elephantine|Elephantine]].

The god [[khnum|Khnum]] appears to the king in a dream and promises renewed inundation. The inscription then gives Djoser's decree granting land and revenues to the temple. The resolution joins material relief for Egypt with royal recognition of Khnum's claims. The account moves from a practical problem to a divine response and a formal grant [@ael].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Gen 41:1–8, Pharaoh has dreams in which cattle emerge from the Nile and healthy examples are followed by emaciated ones. Gen 41:25–36 gives Joseph's interpretation of two dreams as one message about seven years of abundance and seven of famine. Joseph proposes storing grain against the coming shortage. The Famine Stele likewise uses seven years of hunger and a royal need for knowledge, but its dream promises relief and leads to a grant to a temple.

Egyptian agriculture depended on the Nile, so a long failure of its floods was an apt setting for a story about national distress. Gen 47:22, 26 also pictures priests as having a distinctive position in Egyptian land and revenue arrangements. The inscription supplies an example of how an Egyptian temple expressed such claims, while the two texts describe different institutions and episodes.

## Scholarly Issues

Egyptologists generally understand the text as a Ptolemaic claim to privileges attributed to an ancient king. It may reflect competition between Khnum's temple and other sanctuaries in the region, including that of Isis at Philae. That explanation concerns the interests the inscription served when it was carved. It does not establish a famine under Djoser or supply historical evidence for Joseph. The shared themes are best treated as comparisons within a region where long famines and royal responses were familiar subjects of storytelling.
