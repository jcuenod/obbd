## Overview

Ammi-ṣaduqa, a king of the First Dynasty of Babylon in the seventeenth century BCE, issued a decree that canceled certain debts and arrears and freed some people who had entered servitude because of debt. It is the best-preserved Old Babylonian example of a royal decree restoring economic equity. Its limits make it especially valuable for comparison with the biblical laws of debt release and Jubilee. In both settings, release could protect indebted households while preserving other obligations and forms of servitude.

## Manuscripts and Date

The Akkadian text survives on several cuneiform tablets, with damaged portions that can sometimes be read by comparing copies. Its wording refers to the first year of Ammi-ṣaduqa's reign. On the conventional Middle Chronology, that places the decree around 1646 BCE. Different absolute chronologies for Old Babylonian history give different calendar dates. Earlier rulers, including [[hammurabi|Hammurabi]], also proclaimed *mīšarum*, the restoration of equity by royal order [@anet] [@roth-law].

## Contents

The decree cancels specified arrears owed to the palace and obligations arising from loans of silver or barley. It voids qualifying claims and addresses attempts to conceal a consumer loan by presenting it in another legal form. Certain genuine commercial arrangements remain enforceable. The text distinguishes debts arising from economic distress from obligations that the king allows to continue.

Some people pledged or sold into servitude because of debt were entitled to freedom. The decree excluded people already held as slaves by family status, including house-born slaves. Its categories therefore matter for understanding how far the proclamation reached. The edict restricted some consequences of indebtedness while preserving the wider institution of [[slavery]] [@roth-law].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Lev 25:8–17 prescribes a proclamation of *dĕrôr* (“release”) in the fiftieth year and requires the return of ancestral property. Lev 25:39–46 gives release to impoverished kin who enter service and permits the continuing ownership of enslaved people from outside Israel. The shared feature with the Babylonian decree is the distinction between people whose loss of freedom can be reversed and people whom the laws continue to treat as slaves. Each society defines those categories differently.

Deut 15:1–11 commands a remission for Israelite debtors in the seventh year while allowing collection from a foreigner. This line distinguishes people by social membership. Ammi-ṣaduqa's exemptions turn in part on the nature of the obligation, including qualifying commercial loans. Both texts define the scope of [[debt-release|debt release]], but their criteria differ. Deuteronomy and Leviticus place release on recurring schedules under divine authority. The Babylonian decree takes effect through a royal proclamation.

Biblical narratives also describe responses to particular crises. In Jer 34:8–22, Zedekiah and the people agree to release Hebrew slaves and then reverse their decision. In Neh 5:1–13, Nehemiah calls for the restoration of lands and houses and the relinquishing of claims in an economic crisis. Those episodes provide parallels to the concentration of remedial action at a particular political moment.

## Scholarly Issues

Hebrew *dĕrôr* is related to Akkadian *andurārum*, a term for release that also appears in first-millennium Assyrian records. The term is used for the proclamation to captives in Isa 61:1–2 and for the year in which a prince's grant to a servant reverts in Ezek 46:17. Its use in these settings shows how language of release could extend from personal freedom to property and restoration. Scholars debate whether the Jubilee was observed as a regular national institution.
