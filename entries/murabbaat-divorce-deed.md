## Overview

Mur 19 is an Aramaic divorce deed issued at Masada and recovered from a cave at Wadi Murabbaʿat in the Judaean Desert. In it Joseph son of Naqsan releases Miriam daughter of Jonathan from their marriage, permits her to marry another Jewish man, and sets out obligations concerning her property. It is a surviving example of the kind of written release presupposed in Deuteronomy and discussed in the Gospels. The deed gives its date as “year six,” and the era from which the scribe counted remains disputed.

## Description and Provenance

The text survives on papyrus from Wadi Murabbaʿat, the site that gives the document its catalogue designation. Masada is named within the deed as the place of issue and the residence of both parties. Distinguishing the place of writing from the findspot matters for interpreting the documentary evidence from [[masada]] and the wider Judaean Desert [@fitzmyer-harrington-mpat, no. 40].

## Contents

Joseph declares that he divorces Miriam of his own accord. He gives her leave to marry any Jewish man she chooses and identifies the instrument as a writ of divorce. The text continues with provisions about her dowry and compensation for damaged property. The back names Joseph and three witnesses. The statement of release and the financial terms address different consequences of dissolving the marriage.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Deut 24:1–4 assumes that a man can give his wife a written certificate and that she can subsequently marry another man. Its ruling concerns a return to the first husband after a second marriage. Mur 19 shows what a written release could contain in a later Jewish setting. Matt 5:31–32 and 19:7–9 discuss the certificate in relation to divorce and remarriage. Mark 10:4–12 also addresses a wife divorcing her husband, whereas the surviving instrument is a release issued by the husband. See [[marriage-and-bride-price|Marriage, Bride Price, and Dowry]] and [[sealed-deeds]] for the broader legal context.

## Scholarly Issues

The heading dates the deed to the first of Marheshvan in year six. A count connected with the First Jewish Revolt places it around 71/72 CE. A count from the establishment of the Roman province of Arabia places it around 111/112 CE. The place-name alone cannot decide between these readings. Recent reassessment has reopened questions about the dates of several Murabbaʿat documents [@jacobus-brock-murabbaat].
