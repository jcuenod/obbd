## Overview

The Moabites inhabited the plateau east of the Dead Sea, where an Iron Age kingdom took shape. Its best-known ruler, Mesha, left a long inscription recounting battles, construction, and the favor of the god Chemosh. Excavations at several towns reveal local traditions of fortification, worship, and writing. The Bible depicts Moab in several settings, from a neighboring kingdom in conflict with Israel to Ruth's homeland and a place of refuge for an Israelite family. The sources preserve perspectives shaped by different political, religious, and literary concerns.

## Sources

Egyptian reliefs of Ramesses II at Luxor mention Moab in connection with military activity. The ninth-century BCE [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]], found at [[dibon|Dhiban]], is the longest surviving text by a ruler of Moab. Mesha celebrates victories over Israel and works undertaken with the help of [[chemosh|Chemosh]]. Assyrian inscriptions later name several kings of Moab among rulers who submitted or paid tribute.

The language of the Mesha inscription is closely related to Hebrew. It has its own spelling and grammatical features, yet much of its vocabulary is familiar to readers of biblical Hebrew. Shorter inscriptions from other sites add a wider range of settings beyond Mesha's royal account. The archaeological evidence includes remains of towns, shrines, and the objects people used in worship and daily life.

## History

Moab's borders changed, and written claims to territory sometimes describe the goals of a king as much as a permanent frontier. Archaeological work has traced fortified settlements and local centers at Dhiban and elsewhere on the plateau. Bruce Routledge has emphasized the way state power took shape through relations among communities and the kinds of administrative activity associated with them. The precise chronology and territorial extent of early Moab remain debated.

At Khirbat Ataruz, widely identified with Ataroth in the Mesha text, excavators found an Iron Age sanctuary and an inscribed stone incense altar. Khirbat al-Mudayna on Wadi ath-Thamad has yielded a fortified settlement with a shrine and evidence for specialized activity. Such finds help situate Moabite religion and administration in the life of towns. Mesha's account and the other inscriptions give different glimpses of political authority, war, and worship in the region.

## In the Bible

Gen 19:30–38 relates Moab's origin to Lot's family through an incest story. Num 21:26–30 describes the Amorite king Sihon's capture of Moabite land and quotes the Song of Heshbon. Balak's invitation to Balaam in Num 22–24 makes Moab the setting for oracles about Israel. In Judg 3:12–30 Moab's king Eglon exacts tribute until Ehud kills him, while 2 Sam 8:2 reports David's subjugation of Moab.

In 2 Kgs 3:4–27 Mesha rebels after Ahab's death, and a campaign against Moab ends when Mesha offers his heir on the city wall. The Mesha Stele remembers liberation from Israel under different circumstances and in the language of Chemosh's favor. The two accounts agree on conflict involving Mesha and Israel. Their order of events and presentation of the campaigns reflect their own aims.

Deut 23:3–6 (MT 23:4–7) excludes Ammonites and Moabites from the assembly, remembering refusal of aid and the hiring of Balaam. Ruth 1:1–4 introduces a Moabite woman whose character is later central to the story of David's family. Jer 48 laments or condemns a long list of Moabite towns. In Amos 2:1–3 Moab is condemned for burning the bones of the king of Edom. That charge concerns a wrong against another neighbor and illustrates the wider regional relationships of the kingdom.
