## Overview

The Bull Site figurine is a bronze model of a humped bull found on a ridge in the northern Samarian hills. Archaeologists associate it with a nearby stone enclosure containing early Iron Age pottery. The figurine is one of the clearest examples of cattle imagery from the hill country in this period. It supplies a regional comparison for biblical descriptions of calves in worship. Its original place in the enclosure, its function, and the identity of its owners remain questions for interpretation.

## Description and Provenance

The figure was found accidentally on the surface at Dhahrat et-Tawileh before Amihai Mazar excavated there. The investigations documented an open stone enclosure and a small pottery assemblage. Mazar dated the principal material to Iron Age I, the first part of the Iron Age. The figurine was outside a sealed excavation layer, so the association with a particular phase of use rests on its find area and comparison with other objects [@mazar-archaeology, 348–353].

## Contents

The bronze figure shows a bull standing on four legs. A hump rises in front of its back, resembling the anatomy of a zebu, a type of humped cattle. Fine details on the face and body show careful workmanship. There is no identifying inscription, and the surviving object alone does not show whether it was used with another figure or set out separately [@keel-uehlinger-ggig].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The figure provides a material counterpart for the general practice of shaping cattle in metal. The calf made by Aaron in Exod 32:1–6 and those erected by Jeroboam in 1 Kgs 12:28–30 belong to their own literary and religious settings. The bronze bull is earlier than the conventional date assigned to Jeroboam's reign. Its find location also differs from those of the royal sanctuaries named in Kings.

Hosea's criticism of the calf made by a craftsman (Hos 8:5–6) shows that animal images could become a subject of dispute. The [[bull-iconography|wider imagery of bulls]] shows that an animal could stand for a divine power or support a god's figure. These options help explain the disputed function of biblical calves, but none can be established for the figurine from the Bull Site. Num 23:22 likewise compares strength to that of a wild ox, a literary image with no demonstrable relationship to this object.

## Scholarly Issues

Mazar interpreted the enclosure as an open-air place of worship used by early highland settlers, whom he associated with early Israel. On this view the bronze bull may have been an offering or a sacred emblem. Gösta Ahlström emphasized northern parallels and questioned the attribution to Israelite settlers. Other scholars have reconsidered the date and whether the enclosure was necessarily a place of worship. The identification of the site's users and the identification of a god such as El or Baal require evidence beyond the shape of the figurine.
