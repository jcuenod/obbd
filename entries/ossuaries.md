## Overview

Ossuaries are containers for bones gathered after a body had decomposed. Limestone examples are characteristic of Jewish rock-cut tombs around Jerusalem from the late first century BCE to 70 CE. The practice continued in some places after Jerusalem’s destruction. The chests and their inscriptions provide evidence for family burial, personal names, and the languages used around the time of the New Testament. They also clarify the difference between placing a body in a tomb and the later collection of bones in a family burial chamber.

## Archaeological Evidence

Many tombs had a chamber with benches or narrow recesses cut into the rock. A body could be laid in the tomb and left to decompose. The bones were later collected, a practice known as secondary burial. Limestone ossuaries kept sets of bones in relatively small spaces and could make room for additional burials. Some chests contained more than one person’s remains. The custom belongs to a larger history of gathering bones in family tombs, including earlier [[bench-tombs|bench tombs]] with repositories.

Some chests are plain, while others bear carved rosettes and geometric decoration. Inscriptions in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek may name a person, a parent, or another relative. Names shed light on naming practices and language use. Many were common, and the occurrence of several familiar names cannot by itself identify a biblical family. That is a major difficulty with efforts to identify the Talpiot tomb as the burial place of Jesus’ family.

L. Y. Rahmani connected the rise of ossuary burial with belief in bodily resurrection. The evidence allows other explanations, including changing tomb design and the management of family space. The practice itself gives no direct statement of belief. The privately held object called the James ossuary raises a different question. Its inscription names James, Joseph, and Jesus, but the object lacked an excavated context and scholars have disputed the antiquity of part of the inscription. The 2012 acquittal in the related criminal trial did not establish the inscription’s authenticity.

## In the Bible

Matt 27:57–60 and John 19:38–42 describe an initial burial in a rock-cut tomb. Mark 16:3–5 has the women expect difficulty moving the tomb’s stone. The wording does not require a circular disk. Excavated early Roman tombs at Jerusalem more commonly had fitted rectangular or squared blocking stones, and large circular rolling stones are attested in a few exceptional tombs. John 11:38–44 likewise pictures the opening of a tomb and a recently buried body, rather than the collection of bones. The archaeological distinction helps place these narratives within a sequence of burial actions.

Matt 23:27 compares outward appearance and inward impurity to whitewashed tombs containing bones. The image assumes familiarity with burial places and the ritual significance of human remains, without specifying an ossuary. Some interpreters take the request to bury one’s father in Matt 8:21–22 as a reference to a later stage of burial. The text does not specify the interval, and an ordinary request for initial burial remains possible. In 2 Kgs 22:20 the promise that Josiah will be gathered to his fathers uses much older language for ancestral burial or death. It does not describe the late Second Temple custom of using individual limestone chests.
