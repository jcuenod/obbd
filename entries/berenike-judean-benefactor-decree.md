## Overview

A Greek inscription from Berenike in Cyrenaica records honors voted by a Judean corporate body for Decimus Valerius Dionysios. He had paid for improvements to a building called an amphitheater. The decree calls for recurring honors and makes provision for the text to be displayed. It gives evidence for how a Jewish diaspora community publicly recognized a benefactor and supplies a comparison for benefaction and public status in the Gospel of Luke.

## Description and Provenance

The inscription comes from Berenike on the North African coast and is dated to the late first century BCE. It survives on a marble slab with a molded gable and a damaged upper corner. It is known as IBerenike 18 and as number 305 in the catalogue of Greco-Roman association inscriptions. Its Greek calls the corporate body a *politeuma*. The designation shows that the Judeans acted collectively under leaders. The decree by itself leaves the body's exact constitutional status open [@agrw-305].

## Contents

The decree praises the donor for past service to the community and its members and specifies work on the building's flooring and walls. It votes exemption from public services and recurring honors at meetings and new moons, including an olive crown. The text orders that the decree be set up in a conspicuous place in the building. “Amphitheater” translates the designation in the inscription. The word alone does not securely identify the building as a Roman arena or a synagogue.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Luke 7:4–5 Jewish elders cite a centurion's contribution to their synagogue in making an appeal on his behalf. The Berenike decree illustrates comparable public acknowledgment of a gift in a different Jewish community. The Roman name of the donor leaves his religious identity unknown. Luke 22:25–27 concerns the title “benefactors” and its association with power. The decree offers an example of the honor and continuing recognition that could follow a public gift.
