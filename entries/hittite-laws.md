## Overview

The Hittite Laws are a collection of about two hundred provisions preserved on clay tablets from Anatolia. The collection took shape in the early Hittite kingdom and continued to be copied and revised in the Late Bronze Age. It addresses injuries, property, marriage, and prohibited sexual relations. Some provisions describe how penalties changed over time. These laws give a useful comparison for biblical rules on compensation, family duty, and responsibility when a body is found outside a settlement. Both collections classify similar problems while prescribing different responses.

## Manuscripts and Date

The laws are arranged in two series of about a hundred provisions each, identified in antiquity by their opening words. Copies from Ḫattuša include versions of different ages. Older language points to an early Hittite core, while later manuscripts preserve revised wording and, in some cases, different penalties. Modern paragraph numbers help readers compare variants across editions [@roth-law, 213–217] [@cos2, 2.19].

## Contents

Many provisions set compensation for injuries or losses. Some explicitly give an earlier penalty followed by the current one, evidence that scribes preserved the memory of legal change. Death appears for certain offenses, including some prohibited sexual acts and offenses against royal authority. The term *ḫurkel* marks especially serious forms of prohibited sexual conduct. The surviving copies provide evidence of legal classification and scribal practice, and actual proceedings are only partly known.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The laws and the Bible both treat injury to a pregnant woman as a case requiring legal remedy. Hittite provisions 17–18 concern the loss of a pregnancy and specify payments in circumstances defined by status or stage of pregnancy. Exod 21:22 imposes a fine in a case where fighting men strike a pregnant woman. The meaning of the Hebrew description of the birth or loss of the fetus is debated.

A late version of Hittite provision 6 concerns a dead body found on another person's land or in an uncultivated area. It assigns compensation according to where the body is found and considers distance to nearby settlements. Deut 21:1–9 likewise measures distance to towns when an unidentified killer has left a body in the open. The nearest town in Deuteronomy has a ritual obligation involving elders and a declaration of innocence. The Hittite text addresses payment [@roth-law, 217–219].

Hittite provisions 189–195 distinguish permitted and prohibited unions within a family. Provision 193 describes successive marriages to the relatives of a dead husband, including his brother and father. Deut 25:5–10 concerns a brother's obligation to the widow, while Gen 38:8–26 narrates Tamar's claim within her late husband's household. These cases are comparisons for [[levirate-marriage|levirate duty]], with different accounts of who is eligible and what happens if the duty is refused.

Lev 18:6–18 sets out forbidden close-kin relations, and Lev 18:23 and 20:15–16 prohibit sexual acts with animals. Hittite provisions 187–188 and 199–200 distinguish different animals and penalties. Hittite provision 197 also distinguishes situations by location and assigns responsibility accordingly. Deut 22:23–27 distinguishes a case in a town from one in open country, partly through the possibility of a cry for help. The comparisons illuminate differently formulated legal assumptions about place, coercion, and liability.

## Scholarly Issues

Comparison with other [[ane-law-collections|ancient law collections]] helps identify recurring kinds of cases. Direct transmission from the Hittite collection to the biblical laws remains unestablished. The Anatolian archives also belong to a different historical setting from most biblical references to [[hittites-and-hethites|Hittites or the sons of Heth]].
