## Overview

The Book of Jubilees is a Jewish work from the second century BCE that retells Genesis and much of the first half of Exodus. It presents the account as revelation to Moses on Sinai through an angel. The retelling arranges history into periods of forty-nine years and explains how laws and observances were already important in the time of Israel’s ancestors. Preserved in Hebrew fragments and a complete Ethiopic version, it shows how Second Temple Jewish writers interpreted inherited stories and used them to support a distinctive calendar and religious practice.

## Manuscripts and Date

The work was composed in Hebrew, as demonstrated by fragments of about fourteen or fifteen manuscripts among the [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]], including 4Q216. The complete text survives in Geʿez, the classical Ethiopic language. Portions survive in Latin, and ancient authors preserve traces of the Greek version. Jubilees remains canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox tradition. Many scholars date it to the middle decades of the second century BCE, while some proposals place its composition before the Maccabean revolt. [@abd, 3:1030–1032]

## Contents

The revelation frame takes its setting from Exod 24:12–18, where Moses stays on the mountain and receives the tablets. In Jubilees the Angel of the Presence recounts events and laws drawn from heavenly tablets. History is organized by weeks of years and jubilees of forty-nine years. The retelling inserts precise dates, traditions about ancestors and explanations of commandments. It treats the [[sabbath|Sabbath]], festivals and covenant obligations as grounded in the world’s earliest history. [@abd, 3:1030–1032]

The book insists on a year of 364 days, exactly fifty-two weeks. In such a system dates and recurring festivals fall on the same weekdays. Jubilees criticizes a system based on observing the moon because it would displace appointed times. Related calendrical assumptions occur in other Second Temple writings, including texts from Qumran and the [[temple-scroll|Temple Scroll]]. The degree of practical observance and the relationship among these writings remain matters of investigation. [@abd, 3:1030–1032]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The creation account in Gen 1:1–2:3 provides the sequence from which Jubilees enumerates the acts of creation and develops its Sabbath teaching. It also extends Sabbath provisions comparable with Exod 31:12–17. The retelling combines the angelic rebellion and giants suggested by Gen 6:1–4 with traditions also found in [[first-enoch|1 Enoch]], and it places the flood and its resolution from Gen 7:11–8:14 within a more explicit calendar. The stories connect Israel’s later observances with early humanity. [@abd, 3:1030–1032]

In the retelling of Gen 22:1–19, Prince Mastema proposes the test of Abraham. Jubilees also assigns to Mastema the danger to Moses that in Exod 4:24–26 comes from the Lord. This redistribution of agency is comparable with the use of an adversary in 1 Chr 21:1 and helps illuminate Second Temple accounts of the causes of evil. Gal 3:19 also knows a tradition of angelic mediation of the law, without requiring direct use of Jubilees. [@abd, 3:1030–1032]

## Scholarly Issues

The shared calendar and the number of Hebrew copies indicate that readers at Qumran valued the work. They do not establish that the Qumran community composed it. Jubilees reworks the ancestral stories with distinctive laws and claims authority for the resulting account. The evidence for how one community used it still leaves its standing among other ancient Jewish readers open. [@abd, 3:1030–1032]
