## Overview

The Khirbet el-Qom inscription is a late Iron Age Hebrew text from a rock-cut tomb west of Hebron. It invokes the Lord’s blessing on a man named Uriyahu. Many readings also find a reference to protection from enemies involving his asherah, a word whose meaning here is debated. An incised hand accompanies the inscription. The find provides evidence for local Judahite religious language and is often compared with blessings from Kuntillet ʿAjrud and with biblical references to worship and protection.

## Description and Provenance

William G. Dever investigated tombs at Khirbet el-Qom in 1967 after they had been disturbed by looting. The principal text was cut on part of an interior support in one of the tombs. Other inscriptions from the group identify people or burial spaces and place the blessing within a local practice of inscribing tombs. The writing is assigned to the late eighth century BCE on the basis of its letter forms. The damaged surface and overlapping strokes complicate the reading. [@cos2, 2.52] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions] [@neaehl]

## Contents

The text is generally read as naming Uriyahu and blessing him by the Lord. Several reconstructions identify a statement about deliverance from enemies followed or accompanied by a reference to his *asherah*. Some of the lower words, their order and their relation to the main blessing are disputed. An engraved human hand appears beneath the text. It has been interpreted as an image intended to avert harm, or as a memorial sign connected with the Hebrew word *yād*, which can mean hand or monument. Its function remains uncertain. [@cos2, 2.52] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The blessing formula can be compared with Samuel’s reception by Saul in 1 Sam 15:13. The widely read appeal for rescue from enemies has a biblical counterpart in Ps 18:3 (MT 18:4). Deut 16:21 prohibits setting an asherah beside the Lord’s altar, and 2 Kgs 23:6 depicts its removal from the Jerusalem temple. These passages show that biblical writers associated an asherah with worship of the Lord in order to reject the practice. The tomb inscription suggests that an asherah could also appear positively in a local appeal for protection. [@cos2, 2.52] [@ddd] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

References to the Lord and his *asherah* also occur in the [[kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions]]. The wording is generally clearer there, which is one reason the texts are compared. Scholars differ over whether [[asherah|Asherah]] here denotes a goddess, a cult object that could represent her or the Lord, or another feature of the local cult. Grammatical and contextual arguments apply differently at a tomb and at a settlement along desert routes. The inscription records a particular instance of religious language. The range of practice throughout Judah cannot be inferred from a single tomb. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig] [@ddd]
