## Overview

Ophir is a place named in the Hebrew Bible for its gold. Kings describes ships setting out from the northern Red Sea in the time of Solomon and bringing gold back from Ophir. A short Hebrew inscription associated with Tell Qasile uses the name in connection with a weighed amount of gold. These sources show that Ophir gold was a recognized expression. The route and ultimate source remain uncertain, and proposals in Arabia, northeastern Africa, and South Asia rest on different readings of the geographical evidence.

## Location and Identification

The harbor named for Solomon’s fleet in 1 Kgs 9:26–28 is [[ezion-geber|Ezion-geber]], near Eloth at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba. The point of departure makes Red Sea routes an obvious place to start, but it does not locate the destination. Ships could call at an emporium that handled gold from farther away. Even if Ophir were securely located, the place from which the fleet obtained gold could differ from the region where it was mined.

Southwestern Arabia is a longstanding proposal, partly because Gen 10:29 places Ophir among the descendants of Joktan, a grouping often associated with Arabia. The Horn of Africa has been proposed in light of contacts across the Red Sea and comparisons with Egyptian references to Punt. Other readings place Ophir farther east, including South Asia. Josephus identifies it with a land he associates with India. That identification gives evidence for ancient interpretation, with no secure evidence of the actual location. Attempts to identify Ophir with Great Zimbabwe or destinations across the Atlantic lack support from the Hebrew records and material evidence.

## Archaeological History

A short incised Hebrew [[ostraca|potsherd inscription]] associated with Tell Qasile, near the mouth of the Yarkon River, lists gold of Ophir, Beth-horon, and a weight usually read as thirty shekels. It is generally dated to about the eighth century BCE on the basis of its script. The wording and the object’s provenance do not reveal the route by which the gold traveled. The text is evidence for use of the label in a transaction or record outside the literary accounts of the kings. It leaves open whether the label had become a recognized grade of gold as well as indicating provenance.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 1 Kgs 9:26–28 Hiram provides experienced seafarers for Solomon’s fleet. First Kings 10:11 associates the same movement of goods with gold, precious stones, and almug wood. Individual goods could have different ultimate sources in a trading network. The later enterprise in 1 Kgs 22:48 fails when the ships break up at Ezion-geber, so the passage contributes evidence for the imagined route and objective without an additional arrival. The expression for the vessels has prompted comparison with [[tarshish|ships of Tarshish]], whose designation need not identify the destination in every passage.

Ophir also functions as a measure of value. First Chronicles 29:4 names Ophir gold among the gifts for the temple, while Job 28:16 invokes it to convey the inestimable worth of wisdom. Ps 45:9 (MT 45:10) uses the gold in a royal setting, and Isa 13:12 uses it as a standard of rarity. These passages help explain how the name could retain meaning even for readers with no precise map of the trade.
