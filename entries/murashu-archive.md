## Overview

The Murašû archive contains several hundred cuneiform tablets from a business firm at Nippur in Persian-period Babylonia. It records leases, payments, credit, and other transactions involving land whose holders owed taxes or service. The firm dealt with people from many backgrounds, including individuals whose names invoke the God of Israel. The tablets show Judeans taking part in a regional economy across generations after the Babylonian exile. They also attest a canal usually identified with the Kebar of the book of Ezekiel.

## Manuscripts and Date

A University of Pennsylvania expedition found the main group of tablets at Nippur in 1893. The earliest and latest documents are conventionally dated to about 454 and 404 BCE. The dates span much of the reigns of Artaxerxes I and Darius II, with the end of the archive extending to around the accession of Artaxerxes II. Most documents are in Babylonian Akkadian. Some incorporate Aramaic dockets or other short annotations. The archive is named for the family of entrepreneurs whose affairs its documents record.

## Contents

The firm leased land, arranged payments, and supplied credit or other resources. Many participants held land associated with a *ḫaṭru*, an administrative grouping whose members could owe taxes or service. Military service and the dues associated with such holdings gave tenants and titleholders reasons to contract with a firm that could manage cultivation and payments. The documents record these dealings from the firm's perspective and preserve the names of holders, witnesses, and other participants.

West Semitic names occur among the people named in the documents. Some have a Yahwistic element, giving evidence of a continuing Judean or Israelite tradition of naming. The names can give clues to family backgrounds. A name alone cannot establish every person's religion, place of origin, or degree of connection to a particular community.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The tablets refer to the Kabaru canal, usually identified with the Kebar where Ezekiel describes being among the exiles and receiving visions (Ezek 1:1, 3 and 3:15). The archive is later than the setting of the book. Its canal references support a geographical connection with the region around Nippur. The [[al-yahudu-tablets|Āl-Yāhūdu tablets]] come from other archives and supply independent evidence about Judean families and communities in Babylonia.

Neh 5:4 describes borrowers pledging fields and vineyards to pay the king's tax. Ezra 4:13 mentions several royal levies in a letter warning against the rebuilding of Jerusalem. The Nippur tablets show ways agricultural resources and credit could be connected with state obligations elsewhere in the empire. Details from Babylonia provide comparisons for Judah, where landholding and taxation may have worked differently. Ezra 8:15–17 also portrays a group in Babylonia from which Ezra recruits people before departing for Jerusalem.

## Scholarly Issues

The documents have survived as a record of transactions concerning one firm. Their clients and witnesses therefore represent a particular network. The archive gives detailed evidence about economic arrangements and individual names while leaving questions about the size and religious life of diaspora communities open.
