## Overview

Biblical laws set places of refuge apart for people who caused a death without intending it. Some biblical texts connect refuge with an altar, and others designate towns where a person could remain under protection. The laws address the risk that a relative of the deceased, called the avenger of blood, would kill the person who caused the death before the circumstances were judged. Evidence for protected sanctuaries in the ancient Mediterranean provides a broad comparison. The biblical texts lay out more specific rules for distinguishing intentional from unintentional killing.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Ancient legal collections also distinguish between different circumstances of homicide. The Laws of Hammurabi, for example, describe an oath about intent after a blow in a quarrel and prescribe payment if the injured person dies. Some Hittite laws likewise distinguish deaths that result from intentional and accidental blows. These laws illustrate the different legal results that could follow a death, but they supply no close parallel for a network of towns designated for asylum [@roth-law, 104].

Greek inscriptions recognize *asylia*, the protection of a place or person from forcible interference. In the Hellenistic period some decrees declared a sanctuary inviolable. Grants for some temples in Ptolemaic Egypt could protect people who took refuge within them. These cases are comparable to the use of a sanctuary as a protected space, while their dates, legal terms, and purposes differ from those of Israel's refuge towns.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 21:12–14 distinguishes deliberate killing from a death that the killer did not plan. It promises a place to which the second person may flee and orders that a deliberate killer be taken even from God's altar. Accounts in Kings depict appeals for protection made by grasping the [[horned-altars|horns, or projections, of an altar]]. Solomon grants Adonijah conditional safety in 1 Kgs 1:50–53. Joab seeks the same protection in 1 Kgs 2:28–34, but Solomon orders his death and invokes the blood Joab had shed.

Deut 19:1–13 provides for towns within reach of someone fleeing the relative entitled to avenge the death. The example of a loose axe head killing a neighbor illustrates an accidental death. The law instructs the killer's town elders to hand over a deliberate murderer who has fled. Deut 4:41–43 names three towns east of the Jordan. Josh 20 includes them alongside Kedesh, [[shechem|Shechem]], and [[hebron|Hebron]] and describes a person presenting the case to the elders at the city gate.

Num 35:9–34 designates six cities and assigns judgment to the community. After a finding of unintentional killing, it restores the person to the town of refuge. The protected residence lasts until the death of the high priest. The passage rejects paying a ransom to return home sooner. At the priest's death the person may return to the family's land.

## Interpretive Significance

Many scholars have interpreted the towns as a way to preserve protection after reforms concentrated authorized worship at a single sanctuary. Others question whether the altar and town laws belong to so simple a sequence. The literary relationship and relative dates of the laws and the town list remain debated, as does whether the list reflects a working system or an ideal arrangement.

Some interpreters see the high priest's death as expiatory, meaning that it removes the liability or pollution left by the shedding of blood. Others see it as a public boundary ending the period of confinement and making return possible, without treating the priest's death as a substitute sacrifice. Numbers prescribes the endpoint without explaining directly why it has this effect [@abd].
