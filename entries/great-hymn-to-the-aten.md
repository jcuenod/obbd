## Overview

The Great Hymn to the Aten is an Egyptian poem from the reign of Akhenaten in the fourteenth century BCE. Its main surviving witness is carved in the tomb of the official Ay at Amarna. The hymn addresses the sun disk as the giver of life and presents the king as having special knowledge of it. Its descriptions of darkness, wild animals, human labor, and living things sustained by divine power resemble passages in Psalm 104. The likenesses are substantial, although the relationship between the two poems remains debated.

## Manuscripts and Date

The long inscription is on a wall of Ay's tomb at Akhetaten, the city founded by [[akhenaten|Akhenaten]] at modern Amarna. The inscription belongs to the period when Aten received exceptional royal patronage. Ay appears as the speaker in the inscription, although the identity of the composer is uncertain. Parts of the wall were damaged after early recording. The copies of Urbain Bouriant and Norman de Garis Davies help recover readings that are difficult or impossible to see there now. [@ael, 2:96–100] [@cos1, 1.28]

## Contents

The hymn traces the effects of the sun's appearance and disappearance. At sunset people are unable to see or work, and lions and other threats emerge. With sunrise people get up and go about their work, and animals and plants respond. The poem attributes life in distant lands as well as in Egypt to Aten. It also celebrates the formation of children and the growth of life before birth. [@ael, 2:96–100] [@cos1, 1.28]

At the same time, the hymn gives a central role to the royal household. Aten sustains the whole earth, while the king is singled out as the one who knows the god and his purposes. This claim belongs to the setting of the court and its worship. Whether Akhenaten's program is best described as monotheism, belief in only one god, or as an exclusive form of royal worship has long been debated. [@ael, 2:96–100] [@anet, 369–371]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ps 104:20–23 similarly moves from darkness and the activity of lions to sunrise and human work. Ps 104:24–30 praises a creator who sustains creatures and gives or withdraws their breath. The combination and sequence are more suggestive than a shared reference to sunlight would be on its own. The psalm also depicts the sea, ships, and [[leviathan|Leviathan]], a creature with its own West Semitic background. Its opening imagery of clouds, wind, and the retreat of waters in Ps 104:3–4, 7–9 has comparanda in traditions of the storm god. [@cos1, 1.28] [@day-dragon, 28–35]

## Scholarly Issues

Some scholars have proposed that the psalm draws on the Great Hymn itself. The long chronological gap, the dismantling of Akhenaten's cult, and the lack of a traceable chain of copies complicate that proposal. A wider Egyptian tradition of solar hymns, including hymns to Amun, supplies comparable ways of praising the god as a giver of life. Elements of that tradition could have reached Levantine writers by several routes, though the route cannot be demonstrated from the poems. [@ael, 2:96–100] [@cos1, 1.28]

Sigmund Freud once proposed that the religious program of Akhenaten shaped Mosaic monotheism. The hymn is primary evidence for the court's theology, whereas the necessary historical link to Moses lacks primary evidence. The comparison with Psalm 104 can be assessed through the language and themes of the surviving poems independently of that larger proposal.
