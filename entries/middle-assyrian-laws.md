## Overview

The Middle Assyrian Laws are legal collections on cuneiform tablets from the city of Assur. Their best-preserved tablet covers property, marriage, offenses by or against women, and rules about veiling. Other tablets deal with subjects including land and obligations between households. Several provisions resemble laws in Exodus and Deuteronomy in the situations they address, while the remedies and assumptions can differ sharply. The collections show how ancient scribes framed legal problems concerning bodily harm, sexual offenses, marriage arrangements, and the authority of a household.

## Manuscripts and Date

German excavations at Assur recovered the tablets in the early twentieth century. Most surviving copies are usually placed around the reign of Tiglath-pileser I in the late twelfth and early eleventh centuries BCE. The material they transmit may be older. Modern editors designate the tablets by letters, and Tablet A preserves the longest continuous series of provisions. The tablets and sections have been numbered in part for ease of comparison in modern editions.

## Contents

Much of Tablet A deals with women in relation to households, marriage arrangements, and sexual and bodily offenses. A typical provision begins by describing a hypothetical case and then gives the required response. The penalties include fines, labor, flogging, mutilation, and death. A section about the widow in her father's house can assign her to her father-in-law's household and also describes conditions under which she may go where she wishes (MAL A §33).

MAL A §40 sets out who must or may wear a veil in public, and bars enslaved women and prostitutes from veiling. Clothing thus marked legal categories and distinctions of rank. The next provision describes a man giving a concubine the public status of a wife by veiling her before witnesses. Tablet A also distinguishes situations involving married, betrothed, and unmarried women in the remedies it prescribes for sexual offenses.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Deut 25:11–12 and MAL A §8 both impose mutilation on a woman who injures a man's genitals in a fight. The provisions specify different injuries or remedies. Deut 25:1–3 caps flogging at forty blows, while Assyrian provisions assign a fixed number of blows for particular offenses and may combine them with other punishments. The rules on injuries during pregnancy in Exod 21:22–25 and MAL A §§21 and 50–52 likewise invite comparison. Their wording and penalties depend on the circumstances specified in each law.

Deut 22:22 and MAL A §§13–15 deal with adultery and allow severe punishment. Judicial assessment of location and consent appears in both Deut 22:23–27 and MAL A §12. Deut 22:28–29 and MAL A §55 both address a sexual offense involving an unbetrothed young woman, with consequences involving her placement in a household and a payment under some circumstances. The legal solutions differ, and the Assyrian provision includes a retaliatory penalty if the offender already has a wife. MAL A §56 and Exod 22:16–17 (MT 22:15–16) provide relevant comparisons where consent and a father's decision figure in the remedy.

Deut 25:5–10 gives a widow a procedure for pursuing [[levirate-marriage|marriage to her late husband's brother]] to preserve the deceased's name. The Assyrian provisions likewise concern the disposition of a widow within her husband's family, but depend on the circumstances of her residence and household. In Gen 38:14–15 Tamar covers herself and Judah takes her for a prostitute. MAL A §40 prohibits prostitutes from veiling in public. These texts indicate that a veil could carry different meanings in different settings. Rebekah's veiling in Gen 24:65 supplies another example of the significance of occasion.

## Scholarly Issues

The tablets are collections of cases. How they relate to royal or local administration and what courts did in a particular case are separate questions. The biblical and Assyrian similarities help clarify common legal problems and differences among their solutions. Establishing use of the Assyrian tablets by a biblical writer would require additional evidence of transmission.
