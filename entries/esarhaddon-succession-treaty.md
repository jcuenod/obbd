## Overview

In 672 BCE the Assyrian king Esarhaddon required officials and subjects to swear loyalty to his designated successor, Ashurbanipal. Copies of the oath were found at Nimrud in Assyria and at Tell Tayinat in southern Turkey. The text demands active defense of the succession and threatens oath breakers with curses. Some of its requirements and curses closely resemble passages in Deuteronomy. Scholars debate whether Judean writers adapted this particular oath or drew on treaty language that circulated more widely.

## Manuscripts and Date

The Assyrian term *adê* refers in this setting to sworn obligations. Copies found in the temple of Nabu at Nimrud in 1955 name rulers from the Iranian borderlands among those bound by the oath. A tablet found in 2009 in a temple at Tell Tayinat shows that the same program reached an Assyrian center in the west. That find broadens the known distribution of the oath and makes familiarity with it in the Levant more plausible. [@saa2, xv, xxxv–xlii, 28–30] [@lauinger-tayinat, 87–92]

## Contents

[[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] requires those who swear the oath to protect [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]] and report statements or activities threatening his succession. The obligations apply even when disloyal conduct comes from those to whom the oath takers would ordinarily be close. The text invokes gods as witnesses and sets out extensive curses. These express consequences for broken loyalty in terms of illness, loss, failed harvests and exposure to enemies. [@saa2, 28–58]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Deut 13:1–18 sets loyalty to Yahweh against attempts to induce the worship of other gods, including enticement by close relatives. The Assyrian oath also considers situations in which a person receives a disloyal message from a close associate. In Deut 28:23, the sky and ground turn to metal. The Assyrian curses use related metal imagery for the effects of drought. Deut 28:26–30 likewise includes curses on bodies and households that can be compared in wording or sequence with Assyrian sanctions. [@saa2, 33–34, 45–46, 51] [@abd, 1:1179–1184, 2:169–171]

The oath also uses the language of love for committed loyalty to the designated ruler. This supports the diplomatic comparison William Moran drew for Deut 6:5, which commands Israel to love Yahweh. The wider evidence for [[covenant-love-language|love as a treaty term]] helps explain how language of personal attachment could carry public obligations. It need not exhaust the meaning of love in Deuteronomy. [@saa2, 39]

## Scholarly Issues

Eckart Otto, Bernard Levinson and Hans Ulrich Steymans have argued in different ways for a relationship between the oath and Deuteronomy. One influential reading sees a deliberate reassignment of the loyalty due an emperor to Israel's God. C. L. Crouch questions whether the parallels are distinctive enough to establish direct use and argues that several can be explained from the wider treaty tradition. The Tayinat copy establishes western circulation, while links to a specific copy in Judah or to customs such as the teaching of words in Deut 6:6–9 remain disputed. [@saa2, 28–58] [@abd, 1:1179–1184, 2:169–171]
