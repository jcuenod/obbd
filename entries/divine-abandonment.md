## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern texts sometimes explained a city's defeat by saying that its god had withdrawn protection or left the sanctuary. The idea appears in Mesopotamian laments and royal inscriptions and has a close parallel in a royal text from Moab. A god's absence could end when the deity relented and the shrine was restored. In Ezekiel, the glory of Israel's God leaves Jerusalem and later returns. The comparison explains how defeat could be interpreted as the action of a city's own deity and how restoration could express renewed favor.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In Sumerian [[city-laments|laments for ruined cities]], their gods leave or lose their places as disaster overtakes the people. In the Akkadian text called the Marduk Prophecy, Babylon's god describes journeys to Hatti, Assyria, and Elam and anticipates restoration. These journeys are generally understood in relation to the removal and return of Marduk's statue. In [[erra-and-ishum|the story of Erra and Ishum]], Marduk's departure makes way for Erra's destructive activity [@cos1].

Esarhaddon's inscriptions say that Marduk condemned Babylon to seventy years of desolation and then shortened the period to eleven. This makes the king's rebuilding an expression of the god's decision [@rinap4]. The later [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]] likewise portrays Marduk as choosing Cyrus after turning against the rule of Nabonidus. In Moab, the [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]] explains Israel's domination in terms of Chemosh's anger with his land. The statement gives a Levantine comparison for attributing defeat to a people's own god [@cos2].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ezek 8:6 connects offenses at the sanctuary with God's departure. In Ezek 10:18–19 and 11:22–23, the glory leaves the temple and then the city, moving east. In Ezek 43:1–5 it returns by the eastern gate and fills the sanctuary. Daniel Block interprets these scenes in light of the ancient abandonment motif. The vision represents the movement of the [[divine-glory-and-melammu|divine glory]], which leaves by choice in judgment without the transport of an image.

Jer 12:7 describes God leaving his house and handing his people over. Lam 2:1, 7 presents God as rejecting his sanctuary and altar during Jerusalem's destruction. Both passages place responsibility for abandonment with Israel's God. Jer 25:11–12 also uses a period of seventy years, a detail that can be compared with the Assyrian account of Babylon without establishing literary dependence.

## Interpretive Significance

In 1 Sam 4:1–7:2 the Philistines capture the [[ark-of-the-covenant|ark]], carry it into their territory, and eventually return it. Patrick Miller and J. J. M. Roberts compared the story with accounts of the seizure and restoration of Mesopotamian divine statues. The comparison helps explain the importance of possessing the sacred object. In Samuel, the affliction of the Philistines gives the story a different emphasis from a political account in which a captor boasts of taking a defeated city's god. Scholars continue to debate the date and literary shape of the ark account.

The actual removal of [[cult-images|divine images]] and the literary motif of divine withdrawal are related. An ancient author could present a statue taken in war as a deity who chose to leave. Ezekiel's account shows how the pattern could also be expressed through a visionary movement of divine glory. In either case the explanation places defeat and restoration within a story about divine action.
