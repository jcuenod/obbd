## Overview

Nabopolassar founded the Neo-Babylonian dynasty and ruled Babylon from 626 to 605 BCE. He took power during the decline of Assyrian rule in Babylonia and joined the Medes in campaigns that destroyed the Assyrian capital of Nineveh. His royal inscriptions describe temple construction and divine favor, while the Babylonian chronicles record campaigns and alliances. He is unnamed in the Bible, but his reign provides the historical setting for prophecies concerning Nineveh, the rise of the Chaldeans, and the battles at Megiddo and Carchemish.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[babylonian-chronicles|Babylonian chronicles]] give the sequence of Nabopolassar's accession and campaigns. They trace fighting in Babylonia and later attacks on the Assyrian heartland. The Medes took Assur in 614 BCE. Nabopolassar and the Median king met near the captured city and formed an alliance. Their forces took [[nineveh|Nineveh]] in 612 BCE, and the coalition went on to take Harran in 610 BCE.

Nabopolassar's own inscriptions describe him as the son of a nobody, a formula that highlights his rise through divine favor and leaves his family origins obscure. It has been proposed that his family had connections with Uruk. His building inscriptions emphasize work for the gods, including the restoration of Babylon's ziggurat, [[etemenanki|Etemenanki]]. They present him as a ruler who restores sacred buildings and secures the gods' support for the kingdom.

## Biblical Portrayal

Nah 2:1–3:19 (MT 2:2–3:19) depicts an attack on Nineveh and condemns the violence associated with the city. Zeph 2:13–15 pictures Nineveh desolate. These oracles reflect the historical importance of the continuing threat or collapse of Assyrian power. Hab 1:5–11 announces that God is raising up the Chaldeans, whose speed and reach make them a frightening new force. The growth of Babylonian power under Nabopolassar gives these portrayals a historical setting.

In 2 Kgs 23:29, Pharaoh Necho goes toward the Euphrates and Josiah meets him at Megiddo, where Josiah dies. The Babylonian chronicle shows that Egypt was supporting Assyria against Babylon around the time of the campaign in 609 BCE. Jer 46:2 reports that Nebuchadnezzar defeated Necho's forces at [[battle-of-carchemish|Carchemish]] in 605 BCE. Nebuchadnezzar led the campaign as crown prince while Nabopolassar was still king.

## Historical Assessment

Nabopolassar's alliances and campaigns helped replace one imperial power with another. Assyrian resistance continued after Nineveh's fall, aided by Egyptian forces, before Babylon defeated Egypt at Carchemish. The chronicle notes that Nabopolassar died in August 605 BCE shortly after that victory. [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar]] then hurried to Babylon to take the throne. The events described in the biblical texts thus span several stages of the change in power.

“Chaldean” can denote groups in southern Babylonia and is later used for the Neo-Babylonian dynasty or its subjects in biblical and classical usage. The label has a wider history than the ancestry of Nabopolassar himself. The chronicles and inscriptions give the strongest evidence for what he did and how he represented his rule.
