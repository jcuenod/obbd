## Overview

Purple and blue-violet dyes produced from marine snails were valued in the ancient Mediterranean and Levant. Their preparation required labor and specialized knowledge, and dyed textiles could signal status or mark objects for sacred use. Stained vessels from Tel Shiqmona and colored fibers from Timna provide direct evidence for production and use. Biblical texts place valuable dyed fabrics in the sanctuary, royal dress, and long-distance trade. The exact relationship between ancient color names, the biological source of a dye, and the finished shade remains debated.

## Archaeological Evidence

Several marine snail species contain dye precursors in a gland beneath the gill region. Important examples include the banded dye-murex, *Hexaplex trunculus*, and the spiny dye-murex, *Bolinus brandaris*. Treatment of material from the gland can yield blue, violet, or reddish-purple shades. The resulting color depends on species, processing, exposure to light, and the fiber used. Ancient color names need not map exactly onto modern color categories or identify a single species.

Broken shells near coastal settlements can indicate collection or processing, though shell remains alone can have several explanations. At Tel Shiqmona, stained vessels and chemical evidence identify an Iron Age facility associated with purple dye. At Timna in the southern Arabah, chemical analysis of textile fragments from the late eleventh or tenth century BCE identified compounds characteristic of true shellfish dye. The coastal installations show production. The textiles show transport or use far from the coast. The evidence does not identify the settlement at which those particular textiles were dyed.

## In the Bible

Exod 25:4 lists dyed materials among the supplies for the sanctuary, and Exod 28:5–6 uses them in priestly clothing. Num 4:6–7 describes coverings for sacred objects, and Num 15:38 prescribes a colored cord on garment fringes. The terms *tĕkēlet* and *ʾargāmān* are usually understood as a blue or blue-violet and a purple, respectively. Related words occur in other ancient languages used in textile trade. Proposals that both colors could be produced from the same snail under different treatment are plausible, although the biblical words do not specify a recipe or species.

Second Chronicles 2:7, 14 names dyed textiles among the skills required for royal construction. Ezek 27:7, 16 includes colored fabrics in its portrayal of [[tyre|Tyre]] and its trade. Judg 8:26 and Esth 8:15 associate rich color with court clothing, while Prov 31:22 depicts it in a prosperous household. In Acts 16:14 Lydia deals in purple goods. The description places her in a specialized trade. It supplies no evidence that she dyed the products herself or that every item she handled used marine dyes.

The etymology connecting the Greek name for [[phoenicians|Phoenicians]] with a word for reddish purple is frequently proposed. The coast was certainly associated with dye production, but that association does not settle the direction or origin of the linguistic connection.
