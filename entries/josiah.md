## Overview

Josiah, son of Amon, ruled Judah in the late seventh century BCE. Kings remembers him for responding to a law book discovered in the Jerusalem temple and removing installations and practices it treats as illicit. He died in an encounter with Pharaoh Necho II at Megiddo in 609 BCE. Babylonian records illuminate Necho’s campaign, while archaeology and inscriptions provide a setting for Judah during Josiah’s reign. The scope of his reforms and the extent of his influence beyond Judah remain debated.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[fall-of-nineveh-chronicle|Babylonian record of the fall of Assyria]] reports that an Egyptian army cooperated with the remaining Assyrian forces in the campaign to retake Harran in 609 BCE. It supplies independent evidence for Egypt’s intervention in support of Assyria. No surviving contemporary inscription names Josiah securely. At Megiddo, Egyptian and Greek pottery has been interpreted as evidence for an Egyptian military presence around the relevant period. Both the chronology of the finds and the garrison interpretation have been challenged. The pottery provides no direct evidence for Josiah’s death. [@abc, 96] [@glassner-chronicles, no. 22, 218–223] [@neaehl]

Clay seal impressions help document literate offices at the end of the monarchy. A bulla from Jerusalem names Nathan-melech with a title identifying him as a servant of the king. The name also occurs in 2 Kgs 23:11 for an official at the temple. The find makes an identification plausible, while the absence of a patronymic and the difference in recorded titles leave room for uncertainty.

## Biblical Portrayal

In 2 Kgs 22:8–13 the priest Hilkiah finds a law book, and the king orders an inquiry after hearing its contents. Its relationship to some form of Deuteronomy has been an influential proposal since Wilhelm de Wette. 2 Kgs 23:4–20 describes removal of cult objects in Jerusalem and Judah and action at Bethel. Zeph 1:1, 4–6 names Josiah’s reign and denounces worship associated with Baal, the heavenly bodies and divided religious allegiance. Jer 22:15–16 recalls a ruler who practiced justice for the poor, in context the father of the king being addressed. [@abd, 3:1015–1019] [@ddd, 100–104, 581–585]

2 Kgs 23:29–30 says [[necho-ii|Necho]] went toward the king of Assyria and killed Josiah at Megiddo. 2 Chr 35:20–24 adds a warning from Necho, a battle and Josiah’s death after removal from the field. The Babylonian chronicle clarifies Egypt’s support for Assyria. It gives no details about the encounter at Megiddo. [@abc, 96] [@abd, 3:1015–1019]

## Historical Assessment

Some scholars accept a substantial reorganization of Judahite worship under Josiah and see the notice about Bethel as evidence of northern expansion. Others consider significant portions of the account later expressions of ideals for Israel’s worship. The persistence of [[judean-pillar-figurines|pillar figurines]] and other household objects through the late monarchy shows the difficulty of inferring changes in domestic practice from official action. Changes in imagery on seals are also open to different explanations. Comparisons between Deuteronomy and [[esarhaddon-succession-treaty|Esarhaddon’s succession treaty]] illuminate arguments about how the book took shape. They do not identify the book found in the temple or establish what Josiah enacted. [@saa2, 28–58] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 329–339] [@abd, 3:1015–1019]
