## Overview

A silver bowl with an Aramaic inscription names Qaynu son of Gashmu, king of Qedar, in a dedication to the goddess Han-ʾIlat. It belongs to a group of vessels reported from Tell el-Maskhuta in the eastern Nile Delta and published in 1956. The dedication places Qedarite royal connections within the Persian-period world. The name Gashmu resembles the name of Nehemiah’s opponent Geshem the Arab, allowing a plausible but unproved identification. The group’s undocumented acquisition limits conclusions about the original shrine and the territory ruled by the dynasty.

## Description and Provenance

The bowl is kept with related silver vessels in the Brooklyn Museum collection. Isaac Rabinowitz published the inscriptions as a group attributed to Tell el-Maskhuta in the Wadi Tumilat. The pieces were acquired through the antiquities market. The reported findspot is important but lacks a record of controlled excavation. The exact deposit, placement of the vessels, and relationship to a particular sanctuary building therefore remain uncertain.

## Contents

The Aramaic legend says that Qaynu, son of Gashmu, king of Qedar, presented the offering to Han-ʾIlat. The goddess’s name is associated with an Arabian religious tradition. The title gives evidence for named kingship among the [[qedarites-and-arabs|Qedarites]], while the language of the inscription reflects a widely used means of writing across the Achaemenid world. The text names a donor and a recipient. It provides no list of the places controlled by either Qaynu or Gashmu.

The link with Geshem rests on the similarity of names, the general date, and the fact that Nehemiah identifies his opponent as Arab. A separate inscription from Dedan naming a Gashm son of Shahr has sometimes entered the discussion. Identifying all the individuals across the records requires accepting further similarities of time and setting. The inscriptions do not connect their bearers directly to Jerusalem.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Neh 2:19 presents Geshem the Arab among those objecting to work in Jerusalem. Neh 6:1–2 includes him in a proposed meeting, and 6:6 gives the form Gashmu in the letter quoted there. The name’s form makes a connection with the bowl attractive. Identifications based on names and broad chronology can be plausible without being demonstrable, particularly when the biblical account gives no title or father for the opponent.

Gen 25:13 locates Qedar among the descendants of Ishmael. Isa 21:16–17 refers to the end of Qedar’s glory, and Ezek 27:21 associates rulers of Qedar and Arabia with livestock trade. Ps 120:5 invokes the tents of Qedar as a distant and difficult setting. These passages represent Qedar differently as a kin group, a political name, and an economic partner. The dedication gives independent evidence of a ruler associated with that name and of movement or religious patronage reaching the eastern Delta.

## Scholarly Issues

The reported find association fits evidence for connections between northern Arabia and Egypt. It cannot by itself draw the boundaries of a Qedarite kingdom or establish uninterrupted rule along the routes between them. In the Persian period, royal families, merchants, and ritual offerings could cross regions governed in different ways. The political importance of Geshem’s opposition to Jerusalem can therefore be considered without making the proposed identification certain.
