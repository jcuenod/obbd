## Overview

The Letter of King Adon is an Aramaic papyrus in which a ruler appeals to Pharaoh for military assistance against a Babylonian advance. The letter invokes the sender's loyalty and presents the situation as urgent. Its damaged lines leave the ruler's city uncertain, and scholars have proposed different settings for both the appeal and the surviving copy. The text provides a useful comparison for biblical accounts of rulers looking to Egypt for military protection when threatened by Babylon.

## Description and Provenance

The papyrus is associated with Saqqara in Egypt and is kept in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. It is damaged, especially where a place-name would help identify the sender's territory. Its Aramaic is part of the wider use of [[aramaic-language|Aramaic]] for communication across political boundaries [@tad, A1.1] [@kai, no. 266].

## Contents

Adon addresses Pharaoh as his superior and speaks of his own obligations as a loyal subordinate. He reports a Babylonian force advancing and asks Pharaoh to send assistance. Those features make the letter a concrete example of appeals for protection in an unequal relationship. Other details about the town and campaign depend on how damaged wording is restored.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ezek 17:15 describes Judah's ruler sending to Egypt for horses and troops. Jer 37:5–7 presents an Egyptian army coming out while the Babylonians are besieging Jerusalem, then returning to Egypt. Adon's appeal shows why a threatened ruler could expect military assistance from Pharaoh. The comparison concerns the practice of seeking aid, with different people and circumstances in the biblical accounts. The larger shift in power behind 2 Kgs 24:7 is a possible historical setting for the letter.

## Scholarly Issues

A widely discussed reading places the appeal near the end of Egyptian predominance in the Levant and associates the unnamed Pharaoh with [[necho-ii|Necho II]]. The sender's city has been restored in different ways. Identification with [[ekron|Ekron]] remains a proposal. A newer edition also considers whether the surviving papyrus was a later writing exercise. The date of a possible historical appeal and the date or purpose of the surviving copy are separate questions [@porten-adon] [@moore-deaps-adon].
