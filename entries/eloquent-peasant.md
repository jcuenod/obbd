## Overview

The *Tale of the Eloquent Peasant* is an Egyptian literary work from the Middle Kingdom, preserved on papyrus manuscripts. A peasant named Khunanup is robbed by a man connected with a high official and petitions for redress. The king orders that the petitions be written down and delays the decision, giving the peasant occasion to speak at length about justice. The speeches offer comparisons for biblical portrayals of officials as protectors of vulnerable people and for the language of honest judgment.

## Manuscripts and Date

The story is written in Middle Egyptian and survives in several manuscripts. Two papyri now in Berlin preserve much of the narrative and petitions, while a papyrus known from the Ramesseum collection in London preserves part of the opening. The surviving witnesses have gaps. The time of the king portrayed in the narrative and the period when the work was composed need to be distinguished. [@ael, 1:169–170] [@anet, 407–410]

## Contents

Khunanup is traveling with goods when a man named Nemtynakht seizes his donkeys and cargo. The peasant takes his complaint to the high steward Rensi. The steward reports his skill as a speaker to the king, who orders that his petitions be recorded and that the petitioner and his family receive provisions without his knowledge. The story gives Khunanup nine petitions, and a final judgment restores his position. [@ael, 1:170–173]

The speeches appeal to [[maat|maat]], the Egyptian ideal of truth, justice and right order. They compare a good official to a balance or a steering oar, images of a device that must work reliably if others are to be safe. They also call on the steward in terms that give him a familial duty to the orphan and widow. The speaker uses these ideals to press a person with authority to act on them. [@ael, 1:173–183] [@anet, 407–410]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 23:6–8 and Lev 19:15 require just handling of cases without regard to advantage or social standing. Prov 16:11 presents honest weights as belonging to Yahweh, using the balance in a different context with related associations of right measure. In Job 29:12–17, Job describes his former practice of rescuing those without help, including the orphan. The Egyptian petitions place principles of judgment and protection beside one another in much the same way that these biblical texts do. The comparison concerns a shared regional ideal. [@ael, 1:173–183] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

The delay in justice is central to interpretation. Some readings emphasize the story's interest in the power and preservation of eloquent speech. Others find assurance in the king's eventual correction of an official's wrongdoing. The provisions given secretly to the family moderate the immediate harm but leave the structure of the story in place. Khunanup has to keep exposing failures of justice in order for the court to record his words. [@ael, 1:173–183]
