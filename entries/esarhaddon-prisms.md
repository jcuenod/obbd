## Overview

The Esarhaddon prisms are inscribed clay prisms from the reign of Esarhaddon, king of Assyria in the seventh century BCE. Examples from Nineveh describe his disputed accession after Sennacherib's death and celebrate work on a royal arsenal. A list of rulers supplying materials includes Manasseh of Judah. The prisms provide evidence for Judah's obligations to Assyria and can be read alongside the biblical notices of Sennacherib's death and Manasseh's reign. They present those matters from the interests of the Assyrian court.

## Description and Provenance

Royal inscriptions occur on prisms, clay objects with several flat faces suitable for columns of writing. The texts known as Nineveh A and B survive in multiple copies and fragments. Editions identify them by text designation so that a reading can be traced across duplicates. Their archaeological and literary setting is the program of royal construction and commemoration at [[nineveh|Nineveh]]. [@rinap4]

## Contents

[[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] describes himself as the son chosen to succeed [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]]. He portrays his brothers as hostile, recounts his withdrawal from danger and represents his eventual march on Nineveh as divinely supported. The account leaves the circumstances of his father's killing largely outside the narrative. The Babylonian chronicle separately reports that Sennacherib was killed by a son. The sources thus supply different details about the same transfer of power. [@rinap4] [@abc]

One list groups rulers of the mainland and islands who were called upon to provide material for construction, including timber and stone objects. It names [[manasseh|Manasseh]] as king of Judah alongside other western rulers. The inscription's wording directly attests the status of Manasseh and the service claimed from the kings. It illustrates [[tribute-and-vassalage|the obligations of subject kingdoms]] under Assyria. [@rinap4]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 2 Kgs 19:37 and Isa 37:38, Adrammelech and Sharezer kill Sennacherib, flee to Ararat and are followed on the throne by Esarhaddon. An identification of the first name with an Assyrian royal son called Urdu-Mullissu has been widely proposed. The second name is harder to identify securely. Neither the prism narrative nor the Babylonian chronicle provides the complete account given in the biblical books. [@rinap4] [@abc]

Manasseh's presence in the list gives an external point of reference for the reign narrated in 2 Kgs 21:1–18. It shows Judah participating in Assyrian imperial arrangements during that reign. Second Chronicles 33:11 reports that Assyrian officials took Manasseh to Babylon. The prisms supply no independent confirmation of the detention. The building list and the detention story address distinct issues.

## Scholarly Issues

The accession narrative is a defense of Esarhaddon's succession. Its attention to divine approval and the hostility of his brothers helps explain the way it assigns responsibility and emphasizes the king's legitimacy. In the building record, the inclusion of Manasseh establishes that the palace claimed his contribution, while it does not establish the full course of Judah's relationship with Assyria. [@rinap4]
