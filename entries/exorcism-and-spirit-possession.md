## Overview

Ancient Jewish and Greco-Roman texts sometimes explain distress or disruptive behavior through the activity of a hostile spirit. Practices for removing a spirit could involve spoken orders, inherited prayers, divine names, or material objects. Josephus's account of an exorcist and ritual texts among the Dead Sea Scrolls show some of the variety within Jewish practice. These sources help readers see what the Gospel and Acts narratives share with their environment and how they present the authority of Jesus and his followers.

## Jewish and Greco-Roman Evidence

Josephus tells of Eleazar performing an exorcism before Vespasian and others. Eleazar employs a ring and a root, invokes Solomon, and orders the spirit to overturn a vessel as a sign that it has departed. Josephus introduces the episode as evidence of Solomon's wisdom and of its continuing effectiveness [@josephus-antiquities, 8.45–49].

The [[11q11-apocryphal-psalms|Qumran manuscript 11Q11]] places compositions used against harmful spirits alongside Psalm 91. Its fragmentary spells and the psalm give evidence for spoken protection and attempts to drive away spirits. Protective and expulsive language can occur in the same collection. Their functions need to be assessed from the wording and setting of each composition [@pajunen-exorcism].

## In the Bible

In Mark 1:21–28 Jesus commands an unclean spirit to leave a man in a synagogue, and the witnesses respond to his authority. Mark 5:1–20 expands the confrontation into a story about a man living among tombs, a group of spirits, and a herd of pigs. The man's restoration, including his return to his community, is central to the outcome. Matt 12:22–29 uses an accusation about the source of Jesus' power to interpret the act as binding a strong man and taking his property.

Acts 19:13–20 portrays itinerant exorcists trying to invoke the name of Jesus whom Paul proclaims. Their failure is part of the narrative's distinction between an adopted formula and the authority it claims. The story assumes that invoking a powerful name was recognizable as an exorcistic practice.

## Interpretive Significance

Ps 91 promises protection from dangers and gives angels a role in guarding the faithful. Its use in 11Q11 shows how a biblical psalm could be read in relation to hostile spirits. That evidence concerns the psalm's reception and the practice of the text's users. The biblical stories describe illness or distress in the terms of their own settings, which do not supply modern clinical evidence.
