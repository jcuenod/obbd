## Overview

A Greek papyrus from Tebtynis in Roman Egypt records an agreement among salt merchants in 47 CE. It appoints a supervisor, sets terms for sales and public payments, and prescribes a monthly drinking gathering. The text is unusually detailed evidence for a group's combined economic and social obligations. It gives a regional comparison for the organization of trades and communal gatherings in Acts and 1 Corinthians.

## Description and Provenance

The document is published as P.Mich. V 245 and is preserved at the University of Michigan under inventory number 657. It records decisions adopted by named men in Tebtynis, a village in the Fayum. Its precise date rests on its own regnal dating, which places the agreement in the reign of Claudius [@agrw-302].

## Contents

The merchants appoint one of their number to supervise their group and collect public taxes on the trade. The agreement distributes rights to sell salt and gypsum, fixes prices for grades of salt, and sets penalties for breaking sales rules. Some fines are payable to the common fund as well as to the public treasury. It also sets a monthly occasion for drinking beer and assigns fines for missing meetings. The financial and social rules occur in the same agreement.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Acts 19:24–27 portrays artisans acting together when they believe their trade is threatened. The Tebtynis merchants offer a documented example of collective action over commercial interests, from another province and occupation. Its terms do not define the organization of the silversmiths of Ephesus.

In 1 Cor 11:17–22 Paul criticizes conduct at a communal meal. The merchant agreement shows how taking part in a scheduled occasion could carry obligations to other group members. It gives no account of a Christian meal or of compulsory participation in another cult. See [[meals-and-banqueting]] and [[greco-roman-associations]] for the range of purposes and arrangements represented by similar gatherings.
