## Overview

Several biblical texts picture waters above the sky and beneath the inhabited land. Genesis describes the separation of waters at creation and the release of upper and lower waters at the flood. Other passages associate water beneath the land with springs, fertility, and blessing. Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts also relate the ordered world to waters around or below it, although they describe these relations differently. The comparison helps explain why water can support human life in one passage and threaten the stability of creation in another.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian texts associate Enki or Ea with the *apsû*, a body of subterranean freshwater. It could be understood as a source of springs and rivers. The word can designate a cosmic region or, in other contexts, a basin associated with a temple. Mesopotamian compositions give different accounts of the relation between freshwater below, the sea, and other regions of the world. Those differences matter when comparing a particular text with Genesis.

Egyptian accounts picture Nun as the primeval waters out of which the ordered world emerged. Some descriptions continue to place these waters around or below the land. The first mound and the renewal of created life give the water a place in the story of order as well as a potential to overwhelm it. Egyptian and Mesopotamian sources furnish conceptual comparisons. Their gods and spatial arrangements cannot be combined into a single scheme.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 1:6–7 separates waters below from waters above by means of a [[firmament|firmament]], an expanse described as dividing the waters. Ps 148:4 calls on waters above the heavens to praise God. Ps 104:3 uses upper waters in an image of God’s dwelling. These passages give the waters different roles in creation, praise, and royal imagery. They do not address every question about their physical relation to one another.

Gen 7:11 describes the opening of the springs of the deep and the windows of the heavens at the beginning of the flood. Gen 8:2 describes those openings being closed. Many interpreters see a reversal of the ordering in Genesis 1, as boundaries that make land habitable are breached and then restored. The narrative explicitly names both sources. The exact physical arrangement readers infer from them belongs to interpretation.

Exod 20:4 and Deut 4:18 include the waters below the earth in descriptions of creatures and images. Ps 24:2 and Ps 136:6 associate the establishment of land with waters. Gen 49:25 invokes blessings of heaven above and of the deep below. The last passage shows a beneficent aspect of subterranean water. The [[tehom|deep]] need not function as a personified enemy whenever it is mentioned.

## Interpretive Significance

The images convey the dependence of ordinary life on the proper provision and restraint of water. Biblical poets may vary the same vocabulary according to whether they describe fertility, danger, or praise. Ancient comparanda show why surrounding waters were intelligible as a way to represent the ordered world. Reading individual passages in context avoids treating all water as chaos or assigning one diagram to every source.
