## Overview

Biblical texts do not present a single account of life after death. Much of the Hebrew Bible describes Sheol, the realm of the dead, while other passages express hope that God can rescue people from death. Explicit resurrection expectations appear especially in second-century BCE Jewish writings of the Second Temple period, the era of Jerusalem's rebuilt temple before its destruction in 70 CE. Resurrection means the dead being raised to renewed life, not simply the continued existence of a soul. Egyptian, Iranian, Canaanite, and Greek traditions provide comparisons, but similarities alone do not establish how Jewish beliefs developed.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian funerary literature combines hopes for continued life with judgment of the deceased. The [[book-of-the-dead]] includes declarations of innocence and the weighing of the heart against the standard of truth and order. This is not identical to the expectation that God will raise the dead together at history's end. More broadly, practices of [[care-for-the-dead]] must be distinguished from an expectation of resurrection. [@ael]

In the [[baal-cycle]], the storm god's death and return form part of a struggle with Death. Such divine narratives differ from promises of resurrection for human beings. [[zoroastrianism]] contains expectations of resurrection, judgment, and the final defeat of evil. Iranian influence on Jewish beliefs is possible, but the fullest surviving accounts are later than the earliest Jewish resurrection texts. Older Iranian material exists, yet its dating and interpretation make the timing and direction of influence difficult to establish. Greek philosophical accounts of an immortal soul offer another comparison, although Greek views were diverse and cannot be reduced to one doctrine.

## In the Hebrew Bible

The common picture of [[sheol-and-the-netherworld]] is continued, diminished existence rather than resurrection to judgment. Gen 5:24 says that God took Enoch, and 2 Kgs 2:11 describes Elijah's ascent in a whirlwind. These exceptional departures do not themselves announce a resurrection of ordinary people. They nevertheless broaden the range of biblical language about the limits of death. [@abd]

Ps 49:15 (MT 49:16, the numbering in the Hebrew Masoretic Text) expresses confidence that God will redeem the speaker from Sheol and receive him. Whether this means rescue from premature death or reception beyond death is debated. Ps 73:24 likewise permits competing readings of divine reception and honor. Job 19:25-27 anticipates vindication by a living redeemer, meaning a defender of the speaker's claim, but its difficult wording leaves disputed whether this occurs before or after death.

Ezek 37:1-14 explicitly identifies the dry bones as Israel and explains their revival as restoration to the land. Hos 6:1-2 similarly speaks of the community's healing and renewed life after divine punishment. Its two-day and third-day language does not by itself establish a timetable for individual resurrection. A proposed background in dying-god rites remains debated, not a demonstrated explanation.

Isa 26:19 depicts the dead rising to life. Its date and the relationship between individual revival and communal restoration remain disputed. Dan 12:2-3, generally dated to c. 165 BCE in its present literary setting, is the clearest Hebrew Bible statement of individual resurrection to differing destinies. Some rise to live forever, others to disgrace, and the wise are likened to stars. Whether the passage refers to all the dead or a selected group remains debated.

## Interpretive Significance

Second Temple writings develop different answers rather than a uniform doctrine. In the persecution setting associated with the [[maccabean-revolt]], 2 Macc 7:9, 14, 23 presents martyrs trusting God to restore their lives. Wis 3:1-4 emphasizes righteous souls in God's care and their hope of immortality. In [[first-enoch]], 1 En 22 describes separated places where dead spirits await judgment; this is not itself a description of universal bodily resurrection.

Paul's argument in 1 Cor 15:12-57 makes Christ's resurrection the basis for the future resurrection of those who belong to him. His contrast between a perishable and an imperishable body describes transformation rather than mere return to ordinary mortal life. A spiritual body remains a body, although its mode of existence differs from the present one. These distinctions prevent either soul survival or national restoration imagery from being treated automatically as an explicit resurrection doctrine.
