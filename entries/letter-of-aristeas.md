## Overview

The *Letter of Aristeas* is a Jewish work written in Greek, probably in Alexandria in the second century BCE. It looks back to the reign of Ptolemy II Philadelphus and tells how seventy-two elders from Jerusalem translated the Jewish Law into Greek for the king's library. The story concerns the Pentateuch, the first five books of the Bible, rather than the whole collection of Greek Jewish scriptures later known as the Septuagint. The letter shows how a Greek-speaking Jewish author could present a translation as authoritative scripture and explain Jewish law to readers familiar with Greek culture.

## Manuscripts and Date

The work survives in medieval Greek manuscripts, not as a document from Ptolemy's court. The narrator calls himself Aristeas, a royal courtier writing to Philocrates, but scholars generally regard the letter as pseudepigraphic: its actual author wrote under someone else's name. A common dating is c. 150–100 BCE, although some scholars place it earlier. <!-- VERIFY: preferred range and the case for earlier dating in recent scholarship --> One reason to distinguish the composition from its third-century BCE setting is the prominent role of Demetrius of Phalerum under Ptolemy II, which is hard to reconcile with ancient accounts of his fall from favor at that king's accession.

## Contents

In the story, the king asks Eleazar, the high priest in [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]], to send translators, and Eleazar selects six elders from each of Israel's twelve tribes. The narrator describes the royal gifts for the temple, the city, and the priests at work. At a symposium, a banquet with formal discussion, the elders answer the king's questions about justice, self-control, and good government. The translators then compare their work to reach agreement and finish in seventy-two days (sections 301–307).

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The Alexandrian Jewish community hears and approves the translation in a public reading (sections 308–311). This resembles the assembly hearing the Law in Neh 8:1–8 and the people's assent after Moses reads the covenant book in Exod 24:7. The curse on changing the wording also recalls the prohibition on adding to or subtracting from the command in Deut 4:2. The scenes are comparable without assuming that each biblical passage served as a direct model.

Eleazar gives the food laws a symbolic meaning: the split hoof teaches people to distinguish right from wrong, and chewing the cud teaches them to keep God in mind (sections 128–171). Lev 11:1–47 distinguishes animals that may be eaten from those that may not and sets out rules concerning contact with carcasses. These provisions belong to the wider subjects of [[pig-avoidance|pork avoidance]] and [[purity-and-impurity|ritual purity]], the rules governing what is clean or unclean. Eleazar's moral explanation shows how one later Jewish author interpreted the rules; it does not establish why the biblical laws were first given.

The royal table has the dimensions of the table for the bread set before God in Exod 25:23–30, but the letter describes solid gold where Exodus specifies wood overlaid with gold. Eleazar's sacred garments include gold bells and a breastpiece with twelve stones, features also found in the instructions for Aaron's garments in Exod 28:2–43. These details show how the author describes temple furnishings and priestly dress in terms familiar from scripture, not that the objects themselves have been independently identified.

## Scholarly Issues

The probable historical core is that the Hebrew Pentateuch was translated into Greek in third-century BCE Alexandria, forming the earliest part of what is now called the [[septuagint|Septuagint]] [@abd, 1:379–384]. This does not establish that the king commissioned it or that the work took seventy-two days. Philo of Alexandria later portrays the translators' agreement as divinely inspired, whereas the letter describes their reaching agreement by comparison (Philo, *Life of Moses* 2.25–44). [[josephus|Josephus]] retells much of the letter in *Jewish Antiquities* 12.11–118; his retelling shows the story's circulation rather than independently confirming it.

One reading sees the work as a defense of Jewish law and way of life in a Greek-speaking setting. Another emphasizes the authority of the Greek translation for Jewish readers and takes the curse against alterations as a possible response to efforts to revise it. <!-- VERIFY: whether the proposed link to an actual revision program can be stated more specifically --> The two aims need not exclude each other, and the curse itself does not identify any group of revisers.
