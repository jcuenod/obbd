## Overview

Lamps that burned vegetable oil supplied portable light in homes, public spaces, and sanctuaries throughout the ancient Levant. Potters made many Bronze and Iron Age examples as open bowls with a pinched rim for the wick. Closed lamps became common in later periods, and distinctive wheel-made lamps circulated in early Roman Judea. Changes in shape help archaeologists date deposits. The familiar experience of tending a flame also informs biblical references to divine guidance, the survival of a household or dynasty, and readiness for a delayed arrival.

## Archaeological Evidence

Many Bronze and Iron Age clay lamps have a shallow open reservoir and a wick channel formed by pinching the rim. Some Iron Age II forms have a thick disc base. They would hold oil, often olive oil, and a wick of plant fiber. Soot at the wick outlet can show use even when the organic materials are gone. Multiple outlets on some lamps allowed more than one flame from the same reservoir. Finds in sanctuaries give evidence for ceremonial lighting. Lamps accompanied by bowls in deposits associated with buildings have also been interpreted as foundation offerings, especially where their placement appears deliberate.

Closed vessels with filling holes and separate wick outlets became more common in the Hellenistic period. Both wheel-made and mold-made forms circulated, and the ratio differs across sites and periods. A characteristic early Roman type known as the Herodian lamp had a wheel-made body with a nozzle pared with a blade. Its conventional name refers to a period and style. Lamp fragments are useful for relative dating because changes in body shape, manufacture, and the design of the nozzle can be traced in excavated assemblages.

## In the Bible

Exod 25:37 describes seven lamps for the [[menorah|sanctuary lampstand]]. Exod 27:20–21 calls for pure oil and assigns responsibility for tending the light. First Samuel 3:3 locates Samuel’s call before the lamp of God goes out. Zech 4:2 gives a visionary description of a lampstand with a bowl and seven lamps. Multi-spouted clay lamps offer evidence that a single reservoir could supply several flames in actual use. The vision’s arrangement and meaning require attention to the text, since an excavated vessel cannot identify the objects in a vision.

In 2 Sam 21:17 David’s men fear the loss of Israel’s lamp if the king is killed. In 1 Kgs 11:36 the conventional reading of *nîr* as “lamp” evokes the continuation of David’s line in Jerusalem. Some scholars instead associate the word with a term for dominion or a yoke. The alternative translation remains disputed. Prov 20:27 uses a lamp to picture examination of the inner person, and Ps 119:105 compares divine instruction to a light for one’s path. In Prov 31:18 a lamp kept burning at night accompanies productive household work. Job 18:5–6 reverses the image, making an extinguished lamp a sign of the collapse of the wicked person’s household.

In Matt 25:1–13 members of a wedding party wait with lights, some bringing more oil than others. The Greek word *lampas* can designate a torch. The outdoor procession and need for a supply of oil have led some interpreters to picture oil-fed torches, while others retain the familiar image of portable lamps. The precise implement remains debated. The narrative depends on the lights requiring fuel and becoming unusable without it.
