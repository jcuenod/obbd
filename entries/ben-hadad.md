## Overview

Ben-hadad is the biblical form of a royal name meaning “son of Hadad,” the Aramean storm god. The books of Kings use it for rulers of Damascus in accounts set in the ninth and early eighth centuries BCE. An Aramaic inscription securely names Bar-Hadad son of Hazael, but Assyrian records call an earlier Damascene king Hadadezer, and another inscription naming a Bar-Hadad is hard to place. These sources help explain the prominence of Damascus in Israel's history. They also leave open how many rulers named Ben-hadad appear in Kings.

## Extrabiblical Sources

On the [[zakkur-stele|Zakkur Stele]], the king of Hamath and Luʿash names Bar-Hadad son of [[hazael|Hazael]] as a leader of a coalition that besieged him. The inscription confirms the royal name and father for this king of [[damascus|Damascus]]. The [[tell-al-rimah-stele|Tell al-Rimah Stele]] of Adad-nirari III also names a Damascene ruler, Mariʾ, conventionally explained as an Aramaic title meaning “my lord.” Many scholars identify him with Hazael's son, though the Assyrian inscription gives no patronymic, a name identifying his father. [@kai, no. 202] [@rima3, A.0.104.7 lines 4–13]

For an earlier period, Shalmaneser III names Hadadezer of Damascus as a coalition leader at the [[battle-of-qarqar|battle of Qarqar]] in 853 BCE. He later names Hazael as his opponent there. The name Hadadezer has a different meaning from Ben-hadad. It could have been another name for the same ruler, but the Assyrian account leaves the question open. The [[melqart-stele|Melqart Stele]] names a Bir-Hadad, another form of the same Aramaic name. Its damaged wording has supported competing identifications involving Damascus and the northern Syrian kingdom of Arpad. [@rima3, A.0.102.2 ii 78–102, A.0.102.14 lines 92–102] [@kai, no. 202]

## Biblical Portrayal

In 1 Kgs 15:18–20, Asa of Judah pays Ben-hadad son of Tabrimmon, son of Hezion, to break with Baasha of Israel and attack him. In 1 Kgs 20:1–34, Ben-hadad besieges Samaria and later makes terms with [[ahab|Ahab]]. The identification of the king in that story and in the siege notice of 2 Kgs 6:24 remains debated. In 2 Kgs 8:7–15, Hazael comes to the throne after the death of a king named Ben-hadad, complicating comparison with the Assyrian sequence.

The clearest match with an external inscription is 2 Kgs 13:3, 24–25. There Ben-hadad succeeds his father Hazael, and Joash of Israel recovers from Ben-hadad towns that Hazael had taken. Amos 1:4 pairs Hazael with the strongholds of Ben-hadad. The expression recurs in the oracle against Damascus in Jer 49:27. These uses show the name's association with the capital and its ruling house without supplying a full sequence of kings.

## Historical Assessment

One reconstruction distinguishes the ruler in Asa's time, another in Ahab's time, and Hazael's son. A shorter reconstruction questions whether the war stories set in Ahab's time originally dealt with him. Some scholars place the events of 1 Kings 20, and sometimes traditions in 1 Kings 22, under later rulers such as Jehoahaz or Joash of Israel and Ben-hadad son of Hazael. Others retain an earlier historical setting. The Assyrian and Aramaic inscriptions establish part of the sequence, while the literary history of the biblical narratives leaves the full count uncertain.
