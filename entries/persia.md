## Overview

The Achaemenid Persian Empire ruled much of western Asia and Egypt from the sixth to the late fourth century BCE. Cyrus II captured Babylon in 539 BCE, bringing Judah into a new imperial setting. Under Persian rule Judah became the small province of Yehud. Royal inscriptions, administrative tablets, and documents from provincial communities show how the empire governed across languages and traditions. Isaiah, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, and Daniel use that political world in distinct ways, from accounts of the Jerusalem temple to narratives of life at foreign courts.

## Sources

Achaemenid kings commissioned inscriptions that record building, titles, dynastic claims, and military success. The [[behistun-inscription|Behistun Inscription]], for example, sets out Darius I’s account of his accession and suppression of rivals. Its scale and multiple languages reveal a program of royal communication. Its account is also a claim about who had the right to rule. The [[persepolis|Persepolis archives]] document administration through supplies, rations, and seals. Aramaic documents from communities such as Elephantine and from regions as distant as Bactria provide evidence at other levels of government.

Greek authors including Herodotus, Xenophon, and Ctesias describe kings and customs from a range of perspectives. Their accounts sometimes preserve information unavailable elsewhere. Their interests in war, court behavior, or ideal kingship affect what they narrate and how they arrange it. Comparison among literary, inscriptional, and administrative witnesses is especially important when reconstructing events in distant provinces.

## History

[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus II]] defeated the Median kingdom, expanded westward, and captured Babylon in 539 BCE. Cambyses added Egypt. [[darius-i|Darius I]] took power amid conflict and consolidated the empire. Under him and his successors, provincial officials collected revenues, maintained communications, and negotiated with local elites. The title satrap names a high provincial office, but jurisdictions and chains of authority varied. Royal centers included Susa, Persepolis, and others used at different times. Alexander’s campaigns ended Achaemenid rule in the late fourth century BCE.

[[yehud-province|Yehud]] was a small administrative unit centered on Jerusalem. Governor and priestly offices mattered locally, and the rebuilt temple was a center of worship and administration. The empire could support local institutions when doing so served its purposes. The [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]] describes restoration of cult places and communities in Babylonian terms, without mentioning Jerusalem or the Judeans. Its modern description as a charter of human rights gives it a purpose and categories foreign to the ancient text.

## In the Bible

Isa 44:28–45:7 presents Cyrus as the ruler through whom Israel’s God acts. Ezra 1:1–4 assigns him a proclamation concerning Jerusalem’s temple. Ezra 4:7–24 presents correspondence about opposition to rebuilding, and Ezra 6:1–12 describes Darius confirming authorization and supplies. These passages give accounts of royal action with different subjects and settings. The Cylinder provides comparable royal language about restoration without confirming the text of a decree for Jerusalem.

Ezra 7:11–26 depicts Artaxerxes equipping Ezra to act in accordance with divine and royal requirements. The proposal that such acts made local law binding throughout an imperial legal system belongs to the [[persian-imperial-authorization|debate over imperial authorization]]. Neh 2:1–9 illustrates the importance of access, letters, and supplies. Hag 1:1 names a governor and high priest under Darius, showing how local leadership is presented within imperial dating.

Esth 1 and Dan 6 dramatize the workings of royal courts. Persian-period vocabulary in such texts includes terms for law or decree and high provincial officials. A narrator’s familiarity with such language provides background for the story without settling whether every court detail is historical. Dan 11:2 looks back on the succession of Persian rulers in a literary scheme that leads toward conflict with Greece.
