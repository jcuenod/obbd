## Overview

Resheph was a West Semitic god associated with warfare, pestilence and protective power. His name occurs in sources from Ebla and Ugarit to Egypt and Cyprus, where some inscriptions identify him with Apollo. In Egyptian representations he can appear armed and ready to strike. The Hebrew Bible uses the related word *rešep* in descriptions of plague, flashes or fiery projectiles. Habakkuk pictures Resheph as an attendant in YHWH's march, an unusually vivid instance of the biblical use of a name that also belonged to a deity.

## Sources and Iconography

Resheph occurs in third-millennium records from Ebla. At Late Bronze Age Ugarit he received offerings in several local forms. A bilingual god list equates him with [[gods-of-2-kings-17|Nergal]], the Mesopotamian god associated with plague, war and the netherworld. That equivalence indicates how scribes understood him in one setting and gives context to the protective and dangerous powers attributed to him elsewhere. [@ddd, 700–703] [@ktu3, 1.47:27, 1.118:26, 1.148:8, 32]

Egyptian New Kingdom stelae portray Resheph as an armed god. The gazelle head or protome fitted to his crown is a distinctive identifying feature. His weapons and his raised arm in some images belong to the wider [[smiting-the-enemy-motif|smiting pose]] used for deities and kings. First-millennium Phoenician inscriptions from Cyprus include a title conventionally read as Resheph of the Arrow. Bilingual inscriptions link versions of Resheph with Apollo, whose archery and capacity to send or avert affliction made him a suitable counterpart. [@ddd, 700–703] [@kai, nos. 38, 41] [@anep, nos. 473–474, 476]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Hab 3:5 places Deber and Resheph, personified powers of disease, before and behind YHWH. They accompany the advance of a [[divine-warrior|divine warrior]] and are subordinated to him. Deut 32:24 groups *rešep* with hunger and destructive afflictions sent in judgment. The verse can be read with pestilence personified or with an ordinary term for burning disease. Ps 78:48 describes flocks handed over to *rəšāpîm*, variously understood as lightning, burning bolts or a plague. [@ddd, 700–703]

Ps 76:3 (MT 76:4) uses the plural for the fiery arrows of a bow that God breaks. Song 8:6 uses it for love's fiery flashes. In Job 5:7, the literal expression “sons of Resheph” or “sons of flame” describes things that fly upward. Interpretations include sparks and birds. The occurrence of Resheph in an Ephraimite genealogy in 1 Chr 7:25 shows that the form could also serve as a personal name. [@ddd, 700–703]

## Scholarly Issues

The god's association with burning and striking makes sense of a word used for both sudden affliction and projectiles. Habakkuk offers the clearest biblical case of a figure with an identifiable role in a divine retinue. Elsewhere grammar and context can favor an ordinary noun, and understanding an arrow or spark in a poem does not require taking it as a divine being. The personal name in Chronicles establishes usage of the form and leaves the circumstances of its adoption unknown. [@ddd, 700–703]
