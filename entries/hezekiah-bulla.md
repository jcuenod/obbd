## Overview

A clay seal impression from the Ophel excavations in Jerusalem bears the name of Hezekiah, son of Ahaz, king of Judah. It was recovered from excavated soil in 2009 and published in 2015. A winged solar disk flanked by ankh signs appears beside the royal inscription. The excavated context gives this small object particular value among seals associated with biblical kings. It independently attests the royal name and offers evidence for the use of internationally recognizable symbols by the late eighth-century Judahite court.

## Description and Provenance

The bulla is a small lump of clay stamped while it was soft. It was recovered through wet sifting of material from a controlled excavation directed by Eilat Mazar in the Ophel, south of the Temple Mount. The deposit was secondary. Its contents were moved there in antiquity, so the findspot does not identify the exact room or office where the impression was made. It was the first published seal impression naming a Judahite king to come from controlled excavation.

Marks left on the reverse have been interpreted as traces of the string and material to which the clay was attached. They fit the use of sealings to secure a tied document. The clay and its impression survive because they can resist decay under conditions that destroy papyrus and other writing materials. The object preserves the act of sealing but no recoverable message from the attached document.

## Contents

The Hebrew legend names Hezekiah, Ahaz, and the kingship of Judah. The usual translation is “Belonging to Hezekiah, [son of] Ahaz, king of Judah.” The bracketed wording supplies the relationship understood from the construction. A disk with downturned wings occupies the central field, with an ankh at either side. Ankh signs are Egyptian symbols of life. Winged disks circulated more broadly in the Near East and were also used in Assyrian royal imagery.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Second Kings 18:1 identifies [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] son of Ahaz as king of Judah. The bulla provides independent material evidence for those names and the title. First Kings 21:8 describes Jezebel writing in Ahab’s name and sealing the letters with his seal. The story illustrates the association between seals and royal authority. A bulla can document the seal used, while authorship and responsibility for a particular communication depend on further information.

Mal 4:2 (MT 3:20) pictures a sun of righteousness rising with healing in its wings, and Ps 84:11 (MT 84:12) uses the sun as an image for God. The bulla shows that winged solar imagery was visible in Judahite royal culture. It cannot tell us how either biblical author encountered the image or which figure the bulla’s designer meant it to signify.

## Scholarly Issues

Other impressions attributed to Hezekiah show a winged scarab, but those examples and an impression attributed to Ahaz appeared on the antiquities market. Their original archaeological settings are unknown. Similar motifs on excavated [[lmlk-seal-impressions|lmlk jar handles]] provide an independent comparison. The evidence leaves open whether Hezekiah changed his royal device, employed several devices, or adapted Egyptian and Assyrian motifs for different purposes.

A separate impression from the same excavations preserves a personal name that can be read as Isaiah. Damage near the following word or name keeps the reading “the prophet” and the identification with Isaiah of Jerusalem uncertain. Proximity to the royal impression cannot settle the missing letters.
