## Overview

The Fall of Nineveh Chronicle is an Akkadian clay tablet recording campaigns in the reign of Nabopolassar, the founder of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. It covers the years from 616 to 609 BCE and provides the principal written evidence for the sack of Nineveh in 612 BCE. It also records later fighting at Harran, when Egypt supported the remaining Assyrian forces. The tablet anchors the history behind biblical oracles against Nineveh and clarifies the wider campaign during which the books of Kings and Chronicles place the death of King Josiah.

## Description and Provenance

The clay tablet is preserved in the British Museum as BM 21901. C. J. Gadd published it in 1923. Its Akkadian text was impressed in cuneiform, a script formed by wedges pressed into clay. The original find context is uncertain, and damage has removed portions of the record. Modern editions number it Chronicle 3 in Grayson and Chronicle 22 in Glassner [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles].

## Contents

The tablet organizes campaigns by Nabopolassar's years of rule. It reports that the Medes took Assur in 614 BCE and that Nabopolassar and the Median king met and made an agreement. In Nabopolassar's fourteenth year, the two armies besieged Nineveh for about three months. The chronicle dates the capture to the month of Abu, in the summer of 612 BCE. It reports plunder and the city's reduction to ruins [@abc].

The later entries distinguish the loss of the capital from the end of Assyrian resistance. Aššur-uballiṭ II held power at [[harran|Harran]] after Nineveh fell. The chronicle records the loss of Harran and an attempt by Assyrian and Egyptian forces to regain it. The surviving account places that effort in 609 BCE and gives the wider military setting for the biblical report of the Egyptian army's movement northward.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Nah 2:1–13 (MT 2:2–14) portrays the assault and plunder of [[nineveh|Nineveh]], while Zeph 2:13–15 pictures the city as desolate. The chronicle supplies a date and identifies the allied forces that took the city. The biblical texts do different work, interpreting Nineveh's ruin as divine judgment. Scholars disagree over how much of Nahum takes shape before the event and how much reflects it afterward.

The Egyptian intervention in 609 BCE helps explain 2 Kgs 23:29, which reports that [[necho-ii|Necho]] went north and that [[josiah|Josiah]] died in an encounter with him at Megiddo. The Hebrew can be understood to mean that Necho marched against the Assyrian king or went to join him. The chronicle favors the latter interpretation, reflected in the NIV's “to help the king of Assyria.” The tablet gives no account of Josiah or the encounter at Megiddo.

## Scholarly Issues

The chronicle reports the siege and the sack but does not explain how the armies breached the walls. A story preserved by the later Greek writer Diodorus describes a flood. The Hebrew poem's image of opened river gates in Nah 2:6 (MT 2:7) is compatible with several readings. The sources do not establish that the poem describes a particular breach witnessed by the author.
