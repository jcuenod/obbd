## Overview

Judean pillar figurines are small clay figures from the kingdom of Judah, especially common in finds from Jerusalem. Most belong to the eighth through early sixth centuries BCE. Their characteristic forms combine a pillar-like lower body with a female torso and hands held at the breasts. Their identities and uses remain debated. They provide physical evidence for images used in Judahite households and offer a setting for biblical discussions of domestic rites, divine images and the limits of royal religious reform.

## Archaeological Evidence

The figures have a solid cylindrical base that allows them to stand. The upper bodies generally depict hands supporting or touching the breasts. Some heads were shaped by hand, with a pinched face, while others were pressed in molds and have more defined facial features and hair. The figures are typically found as fragments. They differ from the flat molds used for some earlier Levantine plaques, although female figures were widespread in the region. [@neaehl] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 329–339]

Many pieces come from disturbed deposits such as fills or refuse, including contexts associated with dwellings. Such deposits can preserve evidence for regional distribution and disposal more clearly than for the objects’ original placement. Proposed uses include votive offerings, healing, protection from harm and assistance in nursing or childbirth. Some scholars interpret them as images of a particular goddess such as [[asherah|Asherah]]. Others treat the female form as a more general expression of care or protection. No accompanying inscription names the figure or explains its use. [@neaehl, 5:1761–1763] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 329–339] [@king-stager-life, 348–352]

## In the Bible

Jer 7:18 and 44:15–19 portray family participation in offerings to the [[queen-of-heaven|Queen of Heaven]]. These accounts provide evidence of religious activity involving households and women. The preparation of offerings described by Jeremiah cannot securely be associated with the figurines. Gen 31:19, 34–35 gives Rachel a role involving household objects called [[teraphim]], but their form in the story is unspecified. The identification of either kind of object with the clay figures is a proposal. [@abd, 3:377–381] [@ddd, 678–680]

The removal of an Asherah object and associated activities in 2 Kgs 23:6–7 takes place in the Jerusalem temple. It offers a possible comparative context for public religious images. The passage gives no description that would match the household finds. Pillar figurines continued to circulate into the final years of Judah, after Josiah’s reign. This persistence helps explain the difficulty of using the account of temple reform to reconstruct how it affected household practice. [@neaehl, 5:1761–1763] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 329–339]

Deut 4:16 prohibits making images in male or female form. The finds help readers understand the kinds of representation that existed in the society addressed by such prohibitions. The wording does not identify pillar figurines specifically. The contrast between normal use of small figures and the biblical critique is a reason to distinguish the varied practices of ancient households from the normative interests of a text. [@abd, 3:377–381] [@king-stager-life, 348–352]
