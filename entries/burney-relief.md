## Overview

The Burney Relief is a fired-clay plaque generally dated to the Old Babylonian period in the early second millennium BCE. It shows a winged female figure with bird talons, standing on lions between two owls. The findspot is unknown, and no inscription identifies the figure. Scholars have proposed Ishtar, the underworld goddess Ereshkigal, or a being associated with Lilith. The relief helps illustrate how these identifications are made and why its familiar association with the night creature in Isaiah remains weak.

## Description and Provenance

The relief became known through the antiquities market. It was named for Burney, one of its modern owners, and entered the British Museum in 2003. The museum calls it the Queen of the Night, a modern descriptive title. Its lack of excavation history initially contributed to doubts about authenticity. Study of the material and stylistic comparisons have led to general acceptance of an ancient origin, although its original location and purpose remain unknown.

## Contents

The plaque shows a naked female figure wearing a horned headdress. She holds a rod and ring in each raised hand. Her feet are talons, and her wings hang on either side of her body. Two lions beneath her and two owls beside her form a balanced composition. The horned crown and emblems are consistent with a prominent divine figure, while the combination of animals and bird features makes identification difficult [@british-museum-burney-relief, no. 2003,0718.1] [@ddd]. Traces of paint show that the relief originally presented more color than it does today, though the appearance of the whole plaque must be reconstructed.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 34:14 describes the desolation of Edom as a place where unusual creatures dwell. One of the Hebrew terms is *lîlît*, rendered “night bird” (ESV). Henri Frankfort linked the figure to Lilith in an early study of the relief, drawing on comparisons with Mesopotamian texts and the biblical term. Neither the biblical verse nor later stories about [[lilith|Lilith]] identify which figure the maker of the plaque intended.

Jer 44:17–19 depicts Judean worshipers offering incense, drink offerings, and cakes to the [[queen-of-heaven|Queen of Heaven]]. Ishtar is one proposed identification of that goddess, and the relief has also been interpreted as showing Ishtar. Both identifications require their own argument. The modern museum title supplies no evidence for the biblical identification.

## Scholarly Issues

The lions and insignia support comparison with [[ishtar|Ishtar]], a goddess of sexual power and war whose images could feature lions. Others associate the downward wings and owls with the underworld and favor Ereshkigal. The two interpretations place different weight on the same physical details. The older proposal that the figure is a Lilith-like demon finds less support today, in part because the headdress and regalia suggest a deity of high standing. Without an inscription or find context, the choice remains uncertain.
