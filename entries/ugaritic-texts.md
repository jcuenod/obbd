## Overview

Ugaritic texts are documents written in the local West Semitic language of Ugarit on Syria's coast. Most surviving examples date to the later centuries of the Late Bronze Age and were written with wedge-shaped signs on clay. The documents include stories about gods and heroes, ritual prescriptions, letters, and accounts. Their language is closely related to biblical Hebrew and helps illuminate some words and poetic forms. The texts also provide a comparable but locally distinct body of religious imagery and literature.

## Manuscripts and Date

Excavations at [[ugarit|Ras Shamra]] beginning in 1929 uncovered clay tablets in the palace, private houses, and other buildings. Tablets were also recovered at nearby Ras Ibn Hani. The main alphabet uses thirty signs, most representing consonants. Abecedaries, or practice texts listing the signs in order, document the sequence scribes learned. The broader archives also contain texts in Akkadian, Hurrian, Hittite, and other languages. This shows that alphabetic writing formed part of a multilingual scribal setting.

## Contents

The [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]] relates struggles involving the storm god Baal, Sea, and Death. The [[aqhat-epic|Aqhat Epic]] and [[kirta-epic|Kirta Epic]] concern leading figures and their families. Some tablets bear a scribe's name, including Ilimilku, who is associated with several major works. Ritual texts prescribe offerings and ceremonies, and administrative documents record transactions and obligations. These genres give different kinds of evidence about beliefs and practices.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 27:1 has a close parallel in the epithets of a twisting and fleeing serpent known from the Ugaritic material. Ps 29 uses storm language often compared with descriptions of [[baal|Baal]], and Ps 68:4 (MT 68:5) uses imagery sometimes translated as riding on clouds, comparable with one of Baal's titles. Ps 92:9 (MT 92:10) uses a repeated phrase to build up a statement about the destruction of enemies. A passage in the Baal Cycle uses a closely comparable poetic pattern. Ps 82:1 likewise uses imagery of a divine assembly, an institution widely attested in the region.

Ugaritic vocabulary can suggest how to read an obscure Hebrew word. In Prov 26:23 some scholars propose reading a word for glaze comparable to Ugaritic *spsg*. The proposal requires analyzing the Hebrew consonants differently from the traditional division. Other proposed readings, such as an emendation affecting the fields in 2 Sam 1:21, also depend on comparison and remain debated. Ezek 14:14, 20 and 28:3 names a figure sometimes compared with the Danel of Ugaritic tradition.

## Scholarly Issues

The surviving tablets belong to a north Syrian city centuries earlier than much of the Hebrew Bible. The closest similarities can reflect linguistic kinship, regional traditions, or particular histories of transmission. The strength of each comparison depends on the form and context of the words or images involved. An older reading of KTU 1.23 was once taken to parallel the prohibition in Exod 23:19 against boiling a kid in its mother's milk. The wording used to support that comparison has generally been abandoned.
