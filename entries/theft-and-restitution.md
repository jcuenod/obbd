## Overview

Biblical laws address theft through repayment, while treating kidnapping, burglary, and disputes over entrusted goods according to their circumstances. The amount required can depend on what was stolen, what happened to it, and how the case came to light. Mesopotamian and Hittite collections address similar problems and also prescribe payments above the value of stolen goods. Their provisions differ in important ways. Comparing the laws helps explain when restitution was a remedy and when a breach of trust or an act of violence brought additional consequences.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] assign death for certain thefts involving a palace or temple (§6). For theft of some kinds of property, §8 sets much higher repayment, thirtyfold or tenfold depending on ownership, and provides for death if the offender cannot pay. Other provisions address breaking into a house and robbery (§§21–22). Sections 120–126 concern goods entrusted to someone else and sometimes require double repayment for fraudulent denial. The conditions attached to each case matter to the comparison.

The [[laws-of-eshnunna|Laws of Eshnunna]] §§12–13 distinguish offenses in a field or house by day and by night, giving a comparison for Israelite law concerning a burglar. The [[hittite-laws|Hittite Laws]] §§57–70 prescribe multiple compensation for stolen animals, with several provisions referring to penalties higher in an earlier formulation. These collections reveal distinct ways of valuing property, accounting for loss, and responding to the danger a thief posed.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 20:15 and Deut 5:19 prohibit stealing. Albrecht Alt proposed that the command originally referred to kidnapping, which incurs death in Exod 21:16 and, in a case concerning the seizure and sale of another Israelite, in Deut 24:7. The brief command gives no object, and the proposed restriction by original context remains debated.

Exod 22:1–4 (MT 21:37–22:3) requires five cattle for an ox and four sheep for a sheep when the animal has been stolen and slaughtered or sold. If a stolen animal is recovered alive, the thief pays double. Between these rules are provisions about a burglar killed during an intrusion and the significance of whether the sun has risen. They concern liability for the killing as well as the thief's obligation to repay. In this sequence a person unable to repay can be sold to satisfy the obligation.

Exod 22:7–15 (MT 22:6–14) concerns entrusted or borrowed property, [[oaths-and-oath-formulas|oaths]], and responsibility for loss. The expression conventionally rendered “before God” has been understood as presentation before God at a sanctuary, an appearance before judges, or recourse to household gods. Lev 6:1–7 (MT 5:20–26) and Num 5:5–8 provide for restoring what was taken plus one fifth in cases of acknowledged wrongdoing and require a reparation offering. Numbers also addresses the case in which repayment must go to the priest for lack of a relative who can receive it.

## Interpretive Significance

Proposed death sentences for theft in Gen 31:32 and 44:9–10 occur in stories where characters make pledges or negotiate penalties. Biblical case laws generally use payment for theft of ordinary property. Moshe Greenberg associated that practice with a particular valuation of property and life. Comparisons must also account for the status of the parties and the particular offense in each ancient collection.

David's order of fourfold repayment for a lamb in 2 Sam 12:6 evokes known rules in response to a parable. Prov 6:30–31 uses sevenfold repayment and the loss of household goods to make a rhetorical point about the cost of theft. Zacchaeus offers fourfold restitution in Luke 19:8. These passages invoke repayment to express different judgments about wrongdoing and restoration.
