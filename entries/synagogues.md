## Overview

In the late Second Temple period, Jewish communities gathered in assemblies commonly called synagogues. The name could refer to the group, the meeting, or its building. Greek sources also use *proseuchē*, a place of prayer. Inscriptions, buildings, and literary texts place such gatherings in Judea, Galilee, and the diaspora. They attest scripture reading and teaching as well as hospitality and communal business. The New Testament describes Jesus and Paul speaking in synagogues. Before 70 CE, these local assemblies coexisted with the sacrificial worship centered on the Jerusalem temple.

## Archaeological Evidence

The origins of the synagogue remain debated. Egyptian dedicatory inscriptions from the third century BCE attest Jewish *proseuchai*. They provide early evidence for communal places of prayer, while leaving their activities and institutional connection with later synagogues open. An excavated building on Delos has often been identified as a synagogue, but its identification and earliest use are disputed. No surviving evidence links the beginning of the institution securely to one event such as the Babylonian exile [@abd].

In Judea and Galilee, buildings commonly identified as pre-70 synagogues include assembly rooms at Gamla, Magdala, and [Masada](masada.md). Benches around the walls and an open central space suited communal assembly. Benches alone cannot determine a room's function, and in places like Masada the conversion of an existing space requires attention to occupation phases. At [Capernaum](capernaum.md) the extant limestone synagogue is later than the Gospel period. Earlier basalt remains beneath it have been interpreted as a predecessor, but their date and function are disputed [@neaehl].

The Greek dedication of Theodotos from Jerusalem is usually dated before 70 CE. It names a synagogue leader who built a synagogue for reading the law and teaching the commandments, along with lodging and water installations for travelers. The inscription gives direct evidence for teaching and hospitality at one institution. Local situations could support different combinations of uses.

## In the Bible

Luke 4:16–30 describes Jesus reading Isaiah and commenting on it in Nazareth. Mark presents him teaching at Capernaum and later names Jairus as one of the synagogue leaders (Mark 1:21; 5:22). A centurion's sponsorship of a synagogue in Luke 7:5 illustrates a tie between an assembly and a local patron. The reference to preferred synagogue seats in Matt 23:6 shows how public assembly could also give visible expression to social status.

Acts 13:14–15 depicts leaders inviting visitors to speak after the reading of the Law and the Prophets. Acts 15:21 takes the regular Sabbath reading of Moses for granted. At Philippi the gathering by the river in Acts 16:13 is described with a form of the word *proseuchē*. Whether this was a building or an open meeting place cannot be established from the narrative. The gathering places mentioned in some translations of Ps 74:8 have sometimes been read as early synagogues. The disputed expression is too uncertain to date the institution.

The decades after the temple's destruction gave local assemblies continuing importance. Later practices and architectural features should be dated from their own evidence when interpreting the earlier buildings and New Testament scenes.
