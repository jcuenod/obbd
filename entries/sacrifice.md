## Overview

Sacrifice is the ritual presentation of animals or other goods to a deity. Across the ancient Near East, offerings could honor the gods, mark shared meals, accompany vows or address ritual and social harm. The Hebrew Bible describes whole offerings, meals, blood rites and other forms of presentation, especially in Leviticus. Ugaritic and Punic texts provide close parallels for some categories and terms. Biblical narratives, prayers and prophetic speeches also ask what makes an offering acceptable and what obligations it places on the people who bring it.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian temple institutions prepared meals for divine images. Offerings included animals, grain preparations, oil and liquids. The service fed the gods in the idiom of the temple household, and institutions could redistribute portions among personnel. Burning an entire victim was much less typical in Mesopotamia than presentation as food. Ritual handling of a slaughtered animal could also serve other purposes, including [[extispicy|divination]]. [@cos1, 1.34] [@anet, 331–334]

West Semitic texts offer closer comparisons for particular categories. Ugaritic rituals distinguish *šrp*, a kind of burned offering, and *šlmm*, a category related in form to Hebrew *šĕlāmîm* and associated in some texts with consumption by participants. The Punic [[marseilles-tariff|Marseilles Tariff]] names categories including *kll* and *šlm kll* and allocates fees and portions among priests and offerers. The words and divisions help clarify choices faced by people presenting an offering. Details varied by sanctuary and occasion. [@ktu3, 1.39, 1.115] [@cos1, 1.98] [@kai, no. 69]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Lev 1–7 distinguishes the whole burnt offering (*ʿōlâ*), the grain offering (*minḥâ*), the well-being or communion offering (*šĕlāmîm*), the purification offering (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*) and the reparation offering (*ʾāšām*). Some portions ascend in smoke, some are assigned to priests and some are eaten by the offerers. The treatment of the blood depends on the rite. Lev 17:10–14 connects blood with life, reserves it for atonement at the altar in sacrificial settings and prohibits its consumption. [@abd, 5:871–881]

Exod 20:24 allows burnt and well-being offerings on an earthen [[altars|altar]]. In Exod 24:5–8, the same categories accompany covenant-making, and blood is applied to altar and people. In 1 Sam 2:12–17, Eli's sons attempt to take a priestly portion before the fat is offered, a narrative about misuse of entitlement. The burning of offerings and the deity's response to their aroma in Gen 8:20–21 can be compared with the post-flood sacrifice in *Gilgamesh* XI. [@abd, 5:871–881] [@george-gilgamesh, 1:518, 712–13]

## Interpretive Significance

Explanations of sacrifice as gift, shared meal, substitution or purification account for different features. Robertson Smith stressed communion, Hubert and Mauss examined mediation through the consecrated victim, and Girard foregrounded the handling of violence. A model that explains one form may have less purchase on another. [@abd, 5:871–881]

Ps 50:8–15 denies that God needs animal food and calls for thanksgiving. Ps 51:16–17 (MT 51:18–19) calls a broken spirit an acceptable offering. 1 Sam 15:22 gives precedence to obedience. Hos 6:6 emphasizes faithful love and knowledge of God, and Amos 5:21–24 demands justice. Isa 1:11–17, Mic 6:6–8 and Jer 7:21–23 press related critiques. Many scholars read these as arguments about priorities and the conditions of acceptable worship. More radical critiques of sacrificial claims are also possible in particular passages, especially where a prophet denies that God commanded or required such offerings in the circumstances evoked. [@abd, 5:871–881]
