## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern texts describe gods meeting in assemblies to decide the fate of kings, cities, and peoples. These gatherings appear in Mesopotamian epics, Late Bronze Age texts from Ugarit, and Iron Age inscriptions from the Levant. Biblical writers likewise depict an assembly around Israel's God, sometimes likening it to a royal court. The comparison helps explain what prophets claim to have seen or heard in God's presence and why some biblical poems can speak of other heavenly powers while proclaiming YHWH's supreme authority.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The tablets from Ugarit use several expressions for gatherings of gods. The Kirta epic refers to an assembly of gods (*ʿdt ʾilm*), while other texts speak of the circle of the sons of [[el|El]]. In the Baal Cycle, El presides over a gathering in which the Sea's envoys demand that Baal be handed over. The participants and powers of the gathering vary with the story, as does the relationship of its presiding figure to the other gods [@parker-unp] [@ktu3].

In Akkadian, *puḫrum* means an assembly. In [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]], the gods gather and grant Marduk extraordinary authority to confront Tiamat. In Atrahasis they deliberate over measures to control the human population. In the Iron Age Levant, the Phoenician inscription from Karatepe invokes a whole circle of gods. The fragmentary [[deir-alla-inscription|Deir ʿAlla inscription]] tells of heavenly beings called *šaddayin* meeting in a vision reported by Balaam son of Beor. The reading of individual lines at Deir ʿAlla remains contested [@lambert-bcm] [@lambert-millard-atrahasis] [@kai] [@cos2].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 1 Kgs 22:19–23, Micaiah sees YHWH enthroned with a host on either side. A spirit proposes a plan to entice Ahab into battle and receives permission to carry it out. In Job 1:6–12, the [[sons-of-god|sons of God]] present themselves before YHWH and [[satan-the-adversary|the accuser]] challenges Job's motives. YHWH sets the limits of the test. Zech 3:1–7 stages a similar legal scene in which the accuser is rebuked and Joshua the high priest is cleared of guilt.

The court also provides a setting for [[prophecy-in-the-ane|prophetic commissioning]]. In Isa 6:1–8 the prophet sees the enthroned Lord among the seraphim and volunteers to go when the Lord asks who will go for him and those around him. Jer 23:18, 22 treats standing in YHWH's confidential council (*sôd*) as a mark of genuine prophetic access. Frank Moore Cross read the plural commands and answering voices of Isa 40:1–8 as a heavenly commission. The passage leaves the addressees unidentified, and interpreters differ over the speakers.

In Ps 89:5–7 (MT 89:6–8), the holy ones praise YHWH, who has no equal among them. Ps 82 depicts God judging other beings called gods in the assembly of El. The prevalent historical reading understands them as heavenly beings condemned for failing to uphold justice. A longstanding interpretation understands them as human judges, drawing on their judicial duties. John 10:34–36 quotes the psalm in a dispute about Jesus' claim to be God's Son, and its use of the verse has played a role in the human-judges reading. The application in John leaves the original identity of the figures open.

## Interpretive Significance

A reading preserved in [[4qdeut-j|a Dead Sea Scroll]] and reflected in the ancient Greek text of Deut 32:8–9 connects the division of nations with heavenly beings. The Masoretic Text instead relates the boundaries of the peoples to the number of Israel's descendants. The song identifies Jacob as YHWH's portion. The relationship of YHWH to [[elyon|the Most High]] in the passage is debated. The variant gives textual substance to comparisons with the nations and their assigned powers in Ps 82.

A divine assembly is one common explanation of the divine plurals in Gen 1:26, 3:22, and 11:7. Other readings understand these as forms of divine deliberation or majesty. None of the three speeches identifies a named council. Comparison with Ugarit and Mesopotamia clarifies why an ancient audience might imagine a court behind such language, while the particular scenes in Kings, Job, and Isaiah give varying roles to the beings around YHWH.
