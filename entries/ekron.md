## Overview

Ekron was a Philistine city in the southern Levant, identified with Tel Miqne on the inland edge of the coastal plain. Excavations uncovered an early Iron Age town with Aegean-style pottery and a much later industrial center that produced olive oil on a large scale under Assyrian domination. A royal inscription found in a temple names the city and two of its rulers. The site gives historical and material context to Ekron's appearances in Joshua, the ark narrative in Samuel and accounts of Israel's dealings with Philistia.

## Location and Identification

Ekron stood near the transition from the coastal plain to the low hills west of Judah. It was one of the five leading cities of the [[philistines|Philistines]]. A limestone block found in the sanctuary of a temple in 1996 provides direct evidence for the identification of Tel Miqne. The [[ekron-royal-dedicatory-inscription|dedicatory inscription]] names Achish, son of Padi, as ruler of Ekron and credits him with building the temple for a goddess. The reading and identity of the goddess are debated. [@neaehl, 5:1952–1958] [@ahituv-echoes, 335–345]

## Archaeological History

Excavations directed by Trude Dothan and Seymour Gitin documented an extensive early Iron Age settlement. Locally made pottery uses forms and decoration related to the Aegean, and excavators found hearths in domestic contexts. These remains help explain why Ekron is important in studying the formation of early Philistine communities. The finds combine practices with different regional histories, while individual artifacts seldom identify the origins of the people who used them. [@neaehl, 5:1952–1958] [@mazar-archaeology, 300–318]

During the seventh century BCE, the city expanded and developed a large [[olive-oil-production|olive oil industry]], with more than a hundred production installations identified. Excavations found presses and related equipment in an industrial district. Assyrian inscriptions give a political setting for this growth. Sennacherib names Padi in his account of the campaign of 701 BCE, and the later Assyrian records also name Ekron's Achish. The late seventh-century town ended in a destruction generally associated with the Babylonian campaigns around 604 BCE. [@neaehl, 5:1952–1958] [@rinap3-1, no. 22 ii 37–iii 49] [@rinap4, no. 1]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 13:3 includes Ekron among the centers of Philistia, while Josh 15:45–46 includes it in the description of Judah's allotment. These notices belong to different ways of mapping the land. In 1 Sam 5:10–12, Ekron receives the captured ark after other towns have suffered, and its residents protest the danger. The list of offerings sent back with the ark in 1 Sam 6:16–17 represents Ekron alongside the other Philistine cities.

Second Kings 1:2–16 describes Ahaziah sending to inquire of [[baal-zebub|Baal-zebub]], the god of Ekron. The name can be understood as “lord of flies,” and some scholars take its form as a hostile modification of another title. The Ekron temple inscription names a goddess and so provides independent evidence of the city's religious life without identifying the god in Kings. Zeph 2:4 includes the city in a judgment against the Philistine towns. [@ddd, 154–155] [@ahituv-echoes, 335–345]
