## Overview

A Greco-Roman household was a residential and economic unit that could include relatives, enslaved people, and other dependents. Household labor, property, religious activity, and authority were distributed according to wealth and status. Ancient writers described these arrangements in different ways, and legal documents show how the position of an individual could change. This evidence supplies a background for New Testament instructions addressed to wives and husbands, children and parents, enslaved people and owners, and people responsible for leading assemblies.

## Jewish and Greco-Roman Evidence

Aristotle begins his discussion of household management with three relationships, those between owner and slave, husband and wife, and father and children. His account reflects the viewpoint of an elite Greek writer. It helps identify categories that structured ancient discussions, while giving only a partial picture of actual households. A family's needs and resources shaped who lived, worked, and exercised authority in it [@aristotle-politics, 1.3, 1253b].

Documents reveal changes that prescriptive discussions can conceal. The [[murabbaat-divorce-deed|Murabbaʿat deed]] ends a marriage and describes obligations regarding property. The [[delphic-manumission-antigona|Delphic inscription for Antigona and her daughters]] records a change from slavery to freedom. They come from different places and periods. Both make visible the legal and economic ties within which household members acted.

## In the Bible

Col 3:18–4:1 and Eph 5:21–6:9 address paired groups within the household. These instructions are often called household codes because they organize exhortation around domestic relationships. Their addressees include people with very different degrees of power. Their appeals to the Lord give the relationships a theological frame. First Peter 2:18–3:7 addresses household servants and spouses within an argument about conduct under pressure.

In 1 Tim 3:4–5, 12 managing a household becomes a criterion for leadership in the congregation. Titus 2:1–10 distributes instructions among groups distinguished by age, sex, and status. Acts 16:14–15, 30–34 reports baptisms associated with Lydia's household and with the Philippian jailer's household. The accounts leave the composition and ages of each household unstated.

## Interpretive Significance

Households could provide property and meeting space and could connect people who held unequal legal positions. The household codes address relationships within that social world. The individual addresses and the reasons given for conduct deserve attention alongside the broad similarities with ancient discussions of household management. For related legal categories see [[slavery]], [[adoption]], and [[marriage-and-bride-price]].
