## Overview

Name theology is a modern term for biblical statements that God chooses a place to put his name there or to make it dwell there. Deuteronomy uses the language in laws governing offerings, tithes, and festivals. The books of Kings apply it to the temple in Jerusalem, while Jeremiah also uses it of Shiloh. Ancient texts in Akkadian show that placing a name could express a ruler's claim over a place. Scholars debate how the biblical formulas relate such a claim to God's presence and to restrictions on where Israel could worship.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]], Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem petitions the king of Egypt and says that the king has placed his name in the land of Jerusalem forever (EA 287:60–63). The writer assumes a continuing royal claim over a city where the pharaoh is absent. Mesopotamian royal inscriptions also describe the placement of names in connection with buildings, monuments, or territory. The expression *šuma šakānu* can make a claim endure through an inscription or other act associated with a place.

These usages provide a comparison for the biblical formulas because both connect a name with a chosen location. The royal statements occur in different political settings and can have different purposes. The analogy identifies a wider idiom available to the biblical writers and leaves open how closely they adapted any particular formulation.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 12:5 calls Israel to seek the place God will choose to put his name there. Deut 12:11 uses the closely related expression traditionally translated as making his name dwell there. The latter language recurs in rules about tithes and the distance to the chosen place (Deut 14:23–24) and about Passover and the Festival of Weeks (Deut 16:2, 6, 11). The passages make divine choice central to the place where the prescribed worship occurs.

Exod 20:24 speaks of the places where God causes his name to be remembered in a law about altars. In 2 Sam 7:13 God promises that David's heir will build a house for his name. Solomon similarly speaks of the place where God's name will be while appealing to God to hear from heaven (1 Kgs 8:16, 29). 1 Kgs 9:3 associates the placement of the name with God's eyes and heart. The construction of altars and installation of an Asherah image in the house carrying God's name makes Manasseh's action especially grievous in 2 Kgs 21:4, 7.

## Interpretive Significance

One longstanding reading understands the formula as distinguishing God's heavenly dwelling from the presence of his name at the temple. Solomon's prayer supplies important evidence for that interpretation. Other scholars, including Sandra Richter, stress that the ancient legal and political uses of placing a name convey authority and possession. The use of the name can express that God has claimed the sanctuary and determines what people may do there.

Jer 7:12 identifies [[shiloh|Shiloh]] as a place where God formerly made the name dwell. It brings the same language to a sanctuary that had lost its earlier standing. Scholars debate whether Deuteronomy's unnamed chosen place was intended to mean Jerusalem alone from the start and how the passages relate to [[cult-reforms-archaeology|centralization of worship]]. The formulas express divine choice across these texts, while the location and its implications vary with the setting.
