## Overview

Several Jewish groups of the late Second Temple period developed distinctive ways of interpreting Torah, organizing community life and responding to foreign rule. Josephus names the Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes as leading groups and associates a fourth movement with resistance to Roman rule. The New Testament portrays Pharisees and Sadducees in debate and in public life. Some Dead Sea Scrolls preserve a community’s own rules and disagreements. These sources offer different perspectives, and the groups they describe represent only part of Jewish life in the period.

## Jewish and Greco-Roman Evidence

Writing in Greek, [[josephus|Josephus]] presents Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes in terms familiar to readers of Greek philosophy. He attributes strong influence among the people to the Pharisees and describes the Sadducees as associated with a smaller and socially prominent group. He characterizes the Essenes through community life, initiation and discipline. His account of a Fourth Philosophy in Antiquities associates it with Judas the Galilean and opposition at the time of the Roman census of 6 CE. It shares some views with the Pharisees in his account, while treating God alone as master in a way that led to resistance to Roman rule. [@abd, 3:1041–1045]

Philo and Josephus describe Essenes in inhabited places as well as in settings removed from the major centers. The [[community-rule|Community Rule]] and related [[dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]] set out admission, common discipline and standards of purity for a group called the *yaḥad*, or community. Many scholars connect it with the Essenes or with a branch of that movement. The scrolls do not use the name Essene, and the relationship between their communities and the settlement at [[qumran|Qumran]] remains debated. [@abd, 2:619–626] [@neaehl, 4:1240–1242]

## In the New Testament

The groups are attested in sources from the Second Temple period. Matt 3:7 depicts Pharisees and Sadducees coming to John’s baptism. In Mark 7:1–13, some Pharisees and scribes dispute with Jesus about handwashing customs and interpretive traditions. Matt 23:1–36 gives a polemical portrayal of scribes and Pharisees. Other texts portray Pharisees as teachers or as members of the Jesus movement, as in Acts 5:34 and 15:5. Paul identifies himself as a Pharisee in Phil 3:5. These settings give evidence for a range of roles and relationships. [@abd, 3:1041–1045]

Mark 12:18–27 gives a Sadducean objection to resurrection. Acts 23:6–8 likewise distinguishes Pharisees and Sadducees over resurrection and associates the difference with questions about angels and spirits. The details are presented in the course of a dispute. Scribes practiced a profession that cut across such groups, and the Herodians in Mark 3:6 appear in a political alignment. Neither label defines a religious school equivalent to the three Josephus describes. [@abd, 3:1041–1045]

## Interpretive Significance

Josephus’s categories and the New Testament’s debates reflect the authors’ aims and settings. Little Sadducean writing survives, so many accounts of Sadducean belief come from outsiders or opponents. Later rabbinic texts preserve some traditions associated with Pharisees, but the institutions and circumstances changed after the temple’s destruction. Essene comparisons with Qumran are strongest where several practices agree, such as formal admission and communal discipline. Differences in rules, historical setting and self-description matter when deciding how far to extend the identification. [@abd, 3:1041–1045] [@neaehl, 4:1240–1242]
