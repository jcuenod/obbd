## Overview

Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 BCE until the emperor Caligula removed him in 39 CE. A son of Herod the Great and Malthace, he governed as tetrarch under Roman authority and founded Tiberias on the Sea of Galilee. Josephus and the Gospels attest his execution of John the Baptist, with different explanations of his motives. His cities, coins, and household illuminate the political setting of Jesus’ Galilean ministry, and Luke assigns him a role in Jesus’ trial.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Josephus describes the division of [[herod-the-great|Herod the Great]]’s kingdom after his death and Antipas’s tenure in Galilee and Perea. In his account Antipas fortified and rebuilt Sepphoris and founded Tiberias, named in honor of Tiberius, around 19–20 CE. Josephus says the new city occupied ground containing tombs, complicating its settlement for people concerned with ritual impurity (*Ant.* 18.36–38). Archaeology documents urban growth in his territories. Its economic consequences for nearby villages are harder to recover.

Bronze coins minted at Tiberias identify Herod as tetrarch. Their types include plant motifs such as a reed and palm branch, alongside wreaths. The coins provide contemporary evidence for his title and for the official identity of the new capital. Josephus says that Antipas later sought royal rank and was exiled after accusations brought by Agrippa reached Caligula (*Ant.* 18.240–256).

Josephus associates John’s death with Antipas’s fear that John’s influence could lead to sedition. He places the execution at Machaerus in Perea and reports that some people interpreted a later defeat at the hands of Aretas IV as punishment for it (*Ant.* 18.116–119). The defeat followed a breakdown in relations after Antipas planned to divorce Aretas’s daughter and marry Herodias. In Josephus the marriage and John’s death belong to the same period, but the explanation he gives for the execution is political.

## Biblical Portrayal

Mark 6:14–29 calls Antipas king and links John’s imprisonment to criticism of Antipas’s marriage. It depicts his execution as the outcome of a banquet, Herodias’s hostility, and the ruler’s public oath. Mark calls Herodias’s former husband Philip. Josephus calls him Herod and identifies Salome as Herodias’s daughter, whom Mark leaves unnamed. Luke 3:1 gives the official title tetrarch, and 3:19–20 associates John’s imprisonment with his criticism of Antipas. The broad agreement about the ruler’s responsibility is stronger than the agreement about circumstances.

Luke connects Jesus’ ministry with the court through Joanna, wife of Chuza, Antipas’s steward (Luke 8:3). In Luke 13:31–32 Jesus is warned that Antipas wants to kill him and answers with the image of a fox. Luke 23:6–12 presents Pilate sending Jesus to Antipas, whose interest turns to mockery. Acts 4:27 then names both rulers in the community’s prayer, and Acts 13:1 names Manaen, brought up with Antipas, among the leaders at Antioch. John 6:23 locates boats coming from Tiberias and places the new city within the narrative’s Galilean geography.

## Historical Assessment

The account in Luke 23:6–12 is unique to Luke and its historical basis is debated. Mark’s informal royal designation is intelligible for a ruler from Herod’s family, even though the coins document a different formal title. Some readers see an allusion to Antipas in the reed and royal clothing of Matt 11:7–8, especially given the reed on his coins. The passage never identifies a coin, and the image can also work as an ordinary comparison in a setting with reeds and royal households.
