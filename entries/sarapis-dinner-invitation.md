## Overview

P.Oxy. I 110 is a brief Greek invitation to dine at the couch of the lord Sarapis in his sanctuary. The papyrus comes from Oxyrhynchus in Egypt and is dated to the second century CE. Its few lines show that a sanctuary dinner could be arranged through an ordinary social invitation. The language and setting provide a comparison for Paul's discussion of cult meals in 1 Corinthians. The invitation belongs to a later Egyptian setting with its own local practices.

## Description and Provenance

The invitation is written on papyrus in Greek. It was published in the first volume of the Oxyrhynchus papyri and is kept at Eton College, where the collection number is ECM 2314. The Heidelberg catalogue dates the hand to the second century [@poxy-110].

## Contents

In lines 1–4 Chaeremon invites an unnamed recipient to dinner at the couch of the lord Sarapis in the Sarapis sanctuary. The dinner is to take place the following day, the fifteenth, beginning at the ninth hour. The Greek *klinē* means a couch, the furniture on which diners could recline. Some older translations render it “table.” The papyrus supplies no menu, list of guests, or account of how the meal was conducted.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 1 Cor 8:10 Paul imagines someone seeing a fellow believer reclining in an idol's temple. The papyrus helps explain how such a meal might be encountered as part of social life. In 1 Cor 10:20–21 Paul uses *trapeza*, “table,” to speak of participation and allegiance. The two texts share a setting of cult dining with different Greek nouns for the meal furniture.

Paul also considers accepting a private dinner invitation and responding if food is identified as sacrificial (1 Cor 10:27–28). Chaeremon's dinner is explicitly located in a sanctuary. The contrast among settings is part of Paul's argument. For local evidence see [[corinth]], and for a wider account of dining see [[meals-and-banqueting]].

## Scholarly Issues

The invitation names the host and deity, leaving the guest's identity and the composition of the gathering unknown. The text provides evidence for cult dining. The role of an occupational group, any obligation to attend, and the relation of the dinner to a particular sacrifice would require further evidence.
