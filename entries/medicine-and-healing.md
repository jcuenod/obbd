## Overview

Ancient healers treated illness with remedies, bodily care, ritual, and prayer. Mesopotamian tablets describe specialists with overlapping roles, Egyptian papyri preserve instructions for wounds and diseases, and Greek sanctuaries offered healing under the patronage of Asclepius. Biblical texts describe physicians, household remedies, and divine healing from Genesis through the New Testament. They value these forms of care differently according to the setting. The ancient evidence helps readers see why medicine and religion often appear together and why biblical illness terms should be interpreted within their own language and period.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In Mesopotamia, an *asû* was a healer associated with remedies and treatment, while an *āšipu* was a specialist often concerned with diagnosis, ritual, and incantation. Their work overlapped. The diagnostic series *Sakikkû* assigns significance and probable outcomes to symptoms and other signs. The Laws of Hammurabi set payments and liabilities for some treatments, and Babylonian and Hittite laws can require a person who caused an injury to provide for treatment [@roth-law] [@cos1].

The Egyptian Ebers and Edwin Smith papyri show the range of care, from collections of remedies to the examination and treatment of injuries. Herodotus 2.84 later describes physicians in Egypt as specialists in particular illnesses. Greek and Roman audiences knew learned medical traditions associated with Hippocrates and sanctuaries of Asclepius at places including Epidaurus, Pergamum, and Corinth. At such shrines people sought healing through the god, sometimes through a dream.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 15:26 identifies YHWH as Israel's healer. Exod 21:18–19 likewise assumes a need for ordinary care and makes an assailant pay for lost time and recovery. In Gen 50:2, Joseph's physicians embalm Jacob, a detail that reflects the specialists' role in a funerary context. Isa 1:6 imagines untreated wounds in order to portray the condition of the people, and Jer 8:22 asks about balm and a physician in Gilead. Jer 46:11 invokes Egyptian remedies to underscore that a nation's wound cannot be healed in the situation envisaged.

Prophets are agents of healing in 1 Kgs 17:17–24 and 2 Kgs 4:32–37 and 5:9–14. In 2 Kgs 20:7 a preparation of figs is applied to [[hezekiah|Hezekiah's]] boil. 2 Kgs 1:2 has Ahaziah inquire of [[baal-zebub|Baal-zebub]] about his prospects for recovery. In 2 Chr 16:12, Asa seeks physicians and is criticized for failing to seek YHWH. The text gives no detailed account of what the physicians did. Sir 38:1–15 enjoins honor for physicians and attributes remedies to the creator while also commending prayer.

## Interpretive Significance

Tob 2:10 and 11:7–13 contrasts the failure of one treatment with healing in a story involving divine guidance. Mark 5:25–26 shows how costly and unsuccessful treatment could be, while Luke 10:34 depicts oil, wine, and bandaging as familiar forms of immediate care. Col 4:14 calls Luke a physician. These passages offer different perspectives on the practical value of healing and its relation to faith.

The evidence for a distinct profession in early Israel is thinner than the Egyptian and Mesopotamian evidence. Biblical terminology also has limits for modern diagnosis. The condition called *ṣāraʿat* in Hebrew covers a category of impurity and cannot simply be identified with Hansen's disease. Ancient accounts of remedies and recovery are most informative about the practices, expectations, and social relationships the writers describe.
