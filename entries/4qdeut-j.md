## Overview

4QDeuteronomy j, also called 4Q37, is a fragmentary Hebrew manuscript from Qumran Cave 4 among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Its handwriting belongs to the Herodian period, named for Herod and his successors around the beginning of the Common Era. At Deut 32:8 it preserves a reading referring to divine beings rather than Israelites when the Most High establishes the boundaries of nations. This evidence matters for reconstructing the Hebrew text and interpreting Israel's place among the nations. A different Qumran manuscript preserves a related variation at the end of the same song.

## Description and Provenance

The Israel Antiquities Authority identifies 4Q37 as parchment, prepared animal skin, written in Hebrew square script, the letter forms familiar from later Hebrew texts. The designation combines the cave number, the abbreviation for Qumran, the biblical book, and a letter distinguishing this copy from others. Its classification as Herodian rests on the style of handwriting, not on a date written by the scribe. ## Contents

The surviving manuscript has been classified as an excerpted collection of passages from Deuteronomy and Exodus rather than a continuous copy of Deuteronomy. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the exact contents, arrangement, and excerpted classification of 4Q37 in the published edition. --> Such a selection could have served reading, prayer, or instruction, but an excerpted format alone would not establish a particular use. Its best-known surviving wording is *bene elohim*, meaning "sons of God," at Deut 32:8. The expression can designate members of the heavenly realm rather than biological offspring.

The Masoretic Text, the traditional Hebrew text transmitted by Jewish scribes, instead has "sons of Israel" at this point. The [[septuagint]], the ancient Jewish Greek translation, supplies another witness to a reading about heavenly beings. Emanuel Tov's published comparison reports that most Greek manuscripts have "angels of God," while some have "sons of God." These forms should not be collapsed into a single uniform Greek reading, and the Greek wording alone cannot establish every letter of its Hebrew source.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Textual criticism compares manuscript readings to reconstruct earlier forms of a text. Many textual critics judge the reading about divine beings earlier than the Masoretic reading in Deut 32:8–9. It fits the movement from the distribution of nations to Israel as the particular portion of YHWH, the God of Israel. The manuscript supplies direct evidence that this Hebrew reading circulated before the medieval Masoretic manuscripts; it does not preserve the poet's original copy.

Related biblical language helps explain this reading without making every passage part of one system. In Job 1:6; 2:1; 38:7, the [[sons-of-god]] appear before YHWH or rejoice at creation. Ps 82:1, 6 depicts an assembly of gods and calls them sons of the Most High, language associated with the [[divine-council]], a heavenly assembly. Gen 10:1–32 supplies the [[table-of-nations]], traditionally counted as seventy peoples, but Deut 32:8 does not itself state that number. Dan 10:13, 20–21 portrays heavenly princes associated with Persia, Greece, and Israel; this is comparable imagery, not proof of direct literary dependence. [@ddd]

A separate manuscript, 4QDeuteronomy q, or 4Q44, preserves a longer form of Deut 32:43 that calls heavenly beings to worship God. The Greek form also contains heavenly summons, though its wording and extent differ from the Qumran text. Heb 1:6 says, "Let all God’s angels worship him" (ESV), wording associated with the Greek tradition of this verse. This connection concerns the reception of a textual form, not evidence that Hebrews used 4Q37 or 4Q44 directly.

## Scholarly Issues

The preference for a divine-being reading does not settle the poem's religious history. Scholars often explain "sons of Israel" as a theological revision that avoids assigning nations to divine beings, but the surviving copies do not identify the reviser or the motive. The interpretation of [[elyon]], meaning "Most High," is also debated. Some understand the title and YHWH as referring to the same deity, who assigns nations while retaining Israel; others see an older picture in which the Most High allots Israel to YHWH. Textual evidence establishes the variant more securely than it establishes either historical reconstruction. [@ddd]
