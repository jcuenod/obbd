## Overview

The Seal of Shema is an Israelite stamp seal found at Megiddo in 1904. Its jasper face bore a striding lion and an inscription identifying Shema as the servant of Jeroboam. The original is lost, and study relies on early records and casts. Most scholars identify the king as Jeroboam II, although the archaeological context leaves room for debate. The object sheds light on the use of seals by Israel's elite and on the title of a servant attached to a ruler.

## Description and Provenance

Gottlieb Schumacher's expedition recovered the seal near the southern gatehouse at [[megiddo|Megiddo]]. It was an oval, scaraboid-shaped stamp seal in jasper. The original was sent to Istanbul and its present whereabouts are unknown. Early photographs, drawings and casts preserve details of the face. These records allow comparisons of the lettering and design. They impose limits on techniques that require direct study of the stone or carved surface. Modern impressions reproduce the recorded carving rather than supplying a new archaeological context. [@avigad-sass-wsss, 49, no. 2] [@neaehl]

## Contents

A striding lion with its mouth open occupies the center. The inscription above and below it identifies the owner as Shema and names his relationship to Jeroboam. The opening *l-* is the usual ownership marker on many West Semitic [[seals-and-bullae|seals]]. The [[lion-imagery|lion]] belongs to a wider visual vocabulary of strength and authority. On an official's seal such an image could signal elevated status. It supplies no independent identification of a specific office or emblem of state. [@avigad-sass-wsss, 49, no. 2] [@anep, fig. 276] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 190–191]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 14:23–29 tells of Jeroboam II's long reign in Samaria and the recovery of territory. If he is the king in the inscription, the seal attests an official associated with the royal administration known from that period. 1 Kgs 12:20 names the earlier Jeroboam as king of Israel and supplies the alternative historical setting. The expression applied to Shema resembles the designation of Asaiah as the king's servant in 2 Kgs 22:12. In this setting the language of service can mark a court relationship and need not indicate servile status. Shema's specific responsibilities are unknown. [@abd, 4:676–677] [@avigad-sass-wsss, 49, no. 2]

## Scholarly Issues

The original find was near Gate 1567, but the records do not date its deposition with precision. Reconsideration of the gate's association with strata at Megiddo illustrates the difficulty of dating an object by proximity alone. The style of the lion and the form of the letters generally favor the eighth century, and thus Jeroboam II. A date under Jeroboam I would make the seal an earlier example of elite administration and Hebrew writing in the monarchy. The inscription itself contains neither a patronymic nor a regnal date that could distinguish the two kings. [@neaehl] [@avigad-sass-wsss, 49, no. 2]
