## Overview

Hammurabi was the sixth king of Babylon’s First Dynasty. During a reign dated 1792–1750 BCE in the middle chronology, he made Babylon the dominant power in southern Mesopotamia. His letters and royal inscriptions document government and warfare. A monumental collection of laws sets out his claim to have established justice and protected vulnerable people. The collection supplies a major comparison for the Covenant Code in Exodus, while its image of a just ruler helps explain the responsibilities assigned to kings in Psalms and Kings.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The correspondence from [[mari-letters|Mari]] follows shifting relationships among Hammurabi, Zimri-Lim of Mari, Rim-Sin of Larsa, and other rulers. These letters were produced while alliances were being made and broken. They show messengers, requests for troops and intelligence, and rulers making decisions with incomplete knowledge. Hammurabi eventually conquered Larsa and turned against Mari. The archive that documents his former ally’s dealings with him survived in the palace at Mari.

Hammurabi’s own records come from royal and administrative settings. Year names identify events that court scribes considered worth commemorating, including campaigns and building projects. Letters to officials in southern Mesopotamia give a different view of his rule through matters such as irrigation, land, and official obligations. The conventional dates place these activities in the eighteenth century BCE. Lower chronologies shift the absolute dates while preserving the basic sequence of his reign.

The best-known witness to the [[code-of-hammurabi|law collection]] is a stone stele found at Susa, where it had been taken from Babylonia. Its relief shows Hammurabi standing before the enthroned sun god [[shamash|Shamash]], who extends the rod and ring. The scene places the king’s justice under divine authority. The prologue and epilogue speak in Hammurabi’s royal voice. They present him as the ruler charged with securing justice and preventing the strong from oppressing the weak. The provisions between them address property, bodily injury, family disputes, labor, and other matters.

## Biblical Portrayal

Psalm 72:1–4, 12–14 asks God to give the king justice and describes him defending the poor, rescuing the needy, and treating their lives as precious. Solomon asks for discernment to judge in 1 Kgs 3:9, and Israel recognizes divine wisdom in his judgment in 3:28. Both texts assume that royal justice depends on a gift or commission from the divine, a familiar claim in accounts of ancient Near Eastern [[shepherd-kingship|kingship]].

The sequence in Exod 21:1–23:19 contains case laws framed by particular circumstances and consequences. The injury and livestock cases offer close comparisons with Hammurabi’s collection and with other [[ane-law-collections|Near Eastern law collections]]. Exodus also places these laws within a larger account of covenant and divine instruction. In Exod 24:12 God summons Moses to receive stone tablets with instruction God has written. Deut 4:5–8 praises Israel’s statutes as righteous before the nations. Hammurabi’s royal epilogue provides a comparison for public claims to justice, though direct polemic in Deuteronomy remains unestablished.

## Historical Assessment

The repeated copying of Hammurabi’s laws shows their importance to Mesopotamian scribes. How judges used the collection is harder to determine. Administrative letters, contracts, and records of disputes offer separate evidence for legal practice. The lasting circulation of the text makes transmission worth investigating, but resemblance between individual cases does not establish that a biblical writer consulted Hammurabi’s stele or any particular copy. An older proposal identified Hammurabi with Amraphel of Gen 14:1. Neither the name nor an independent chronology of the story sustains the identification.
