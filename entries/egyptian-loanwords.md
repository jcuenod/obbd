## Overview

Biblical Hebrew contains words borrowed from Egyptian, especially in accounts of Joseph, Moses, and life along the Nile. Some describe plants, textiles, and measures. Others come from Egyptian titles, and several personal names also have recognizable Egyptian forms. Egypt and the Levant were in contact for many centuries, and words could circulate long after their first use. The vocabulary can help identify the cultural setting of a scene or the kind of specialist involved. Its value for dating a passage depends on the history of each word or name.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian loanwords are Egyptian words taken into another language's ordinary vocabulary. A personal name such as Potiphera, by contrast, can be passed on as someone's name without becoming a Hebrew word for anything. Scholars compare a proposed source and the Hebrew form for meaning and for the ways in which one language represents the sounds of the other. The period when a form first appears in the surviving Egyptian documentation can matter. Egyptian spellings, changing pronunciation, and uneven survival of documents limit the conclusions that can be drawn.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Several terms cluster in the story of Moses' exposure. In Exod 2:3, *tēbâ* means a chest or basket, *gōmeʾ* is papyrus, and *sûp* refers to reeds or rushes. These have Egyptian comparisons, as does *yĕʾōr*, the word used for the [[nile|Nile]]. In Gen 41:2 the marsh grass is *ʾāḥû*, another probable Egyptian loan in a river setting. The term *sûp* appears in [[yam-suph|Yam Suph]], although the place referred to there is a separate question.

In Gen 41:8 Pharaoh summons *ḥarṭummîm*, commonly translated as magicians. The word is usually traced to an Egyptian title associated with chief lector priests, specialists in the recitation and performance of ritual texts. Exod 7:11 applies the word to Pharaoh's specialists too. In Gen 41:42 Joseph is clothed in fine linen, *šēš*, a word also traced to Egyptian. The shout *ʾabrēk* in Gen 41:43 is much less certain. Proposed interpretations include an Egyptian derivation and comparison with Akkadian *abarakku*, an administrative title. The context shows that the call attends Joseph's installation, while the form and original meaning remain disputed.

Egyptian-origin vocabulary occurs beyond descriptions of Egypt. In Exod 29:40, *hîn* is a liquid measure and *ʾêpâ* is a dry measure. Both are commonly traced to Egyptian. The terms appear as part of Hebrew instructions for offerings, illustrating how words for measurement could circulate across cultural settings.

Exod 2:10 explains Moses' name through a Hebrew verb for drawing out, referring to his rescue from the water. Many scholars also compare his name with an Egyptian element meaning “born” or “child,” familiar from names like Thutmose. These are distinct explanations of what the name does in the story and where it may have come from. Phinehas in Exod 6:25 and 1 Sam 1:3 corresponds well to an Egyptian name meaning “the Nubian.” An Egyptian derivation has also been proposed for Hophni in 1 Sam 1:3, with less certainty.

## Interpretive Significance

In Gen 41:45, Pharaoh gives Joseph the name Zaphenath-paneah, and the narrative names Asenath and her father Potiphera. Donald Redford and other scholars have emphasized forms that fit first-millennium Egyptian naming conventions, including the Saite period. Kenneth Kitchen and James Hoffmeier have emphasized older attestations of some elements and name types. An older element can remain in use, and some proposed combinations are better documented in later periods. The terms make the text's Egyptian setting more intelligible, while conclusions about composition or the [[exodus-historical-questions|history of the exodus]] require other evidence.
