## Overview

Necromancy is the practice of consulting the dead for knowledge or guidance. Biblical law forbids such consultations, while narratives and prophetic texts show that people knew of them and imagined the dead as able to speak. The best-known example is Saul's visit to the medium at Endor. Ancient texts from Mesopotamia, Anatolia, and Greece provide points of comparison for encounters with the dead. Consulting the dead was one possibility within a much wider range of customs involving the burial, remembrance, and care of deceased people.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian incantations and ritual texts occasionally envisage calling up a ghost for information. Far more texts concern ghosts as beings to be cared for or driven away when they trouble the living. A ghost, *eṭemmu* in Akkadian, could have an ongoing relation to household and burial rites. Consultation was therefore one possible use of a relationship with the dead. See [[care-for-the-dead|Care for the Dead]].

Ritual texts from Anatolia refer to pits that could give access to powers of the underworld. Hittite *āpi* and Akkadian *apu* have been compared with Hebrew *ʾôb*, a term associated with mediums or spirits. Other explanations relate the Hebrew word to an ancestor or understand it directly as a ghost. The Greek Odyssey offers a literary parallel in which Odysseus seeks knowledge of the future from the dead seer Tiresias (Odyssey 11).

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 1 Sam 28:3–25 Saul consults a woman identified by her association with an *ʾôb*. He does so after failing to obtain a response from God through other means. The woman sees a figure rising, and Saul recognizes Samuel from her description. The message confirms the king's defeat and impending death. The account tells the story of a desperate consultation and reports too little about its procedure to reconstruct the practice in detail.

Lev 19:31 forbids turning to *ʾōbôt* and *yiddĕʿōnîm*, terms often translated as mediums and familiar spirits or spirit specialists. The second term is associated with knowing. Lev 20:6, 27 gives penalties for consulting or practicing such arts. Deut 18:11 includes the person who seeks the dead among prohibited practitioners. 2 Kgs 21:6 associates Manasseh with mediums and other proscribed arts, and 2 Kgs 23:24 says Josiah removed practitioners and objects that included mediums and [[teraphim|teraphim]]. Their presence in the same list places them within a broader critique of unauthorized religious practices.

## Interpretive Significance

Isa 8:19–20 asks why people should consult the dead for the living and mocks the low voices associated with such consultations. Isa 19:3 pictures Egypt seeking help from idols, the dead, and specialists. Isa 29:4 gives Jerusalem a ghostlike voice rising from the ground. Isa 65:4 associates sitting among tombs with other censured behavior, although it does not explicitly describe consultation. These allusions show how a setting or sound could evoke ideas about the dead.

The extent of ongoing ritual for ancestors in Judah remains debated. Practices such as burial, commemoration, and provision for the dead supply a wider context for the biblical texts. They give no single explanation for the knowledge Saul seeks or for the prohibitions directed at those who consult the dead.
