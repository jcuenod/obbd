## Overview

The *Report of Wenamun* is a Late Egyptian narrative about a man sent from Thebes to Byblos for cedar to build the sacred barque of Amun. Its setting is conventionally placed around 1075 BCE, near the end of Egypt's Twentieth Dynasty, and its surviving copy is later. Wenamun meets suspicion and resistance as he travels along the Levantine coast. His negotiations illuminate the purchase and transport of timber in the early Iron Age and provide a comparison for biblical accounts of cedar acquired from Phoenician ports.

## Manuscripts and Date

Papyrus Pushkin 120 in Moscow is the sole surviving manuscript. It is written in hieratic, the cursive Egyptian script, and breaks off before the story is resolved. Its opening dates the voyage to year five without naming a king. The conventional interpretation places it in the Renaissance era under Ramesses XI and fits the appearance of Herihor at Thebes and Smendes at [[tanis|Tanis]]. The date of the voyage within the story, the date of composition and the date of the surviving copy are separate questions. The composition and copy are often placed in the Twenty-first or early Twenty-second Dynasty. [@ael, 2:224–230] [@cos1, 1.41]

## Contents

Wenamun travels to [[byblos|Byblos]] to acquire timber for Amun's barque, a boat used in cult. At Dor, a member of his crew steals valuables, and the local ruler declines to compensate him. At Byblos, Zakar-Baal initially refuses an audience. During an offering a young man is seized by the god and calls for Amun and the Egyptian envoy who bears him to be brought in. When Zakar-Baal hears the request, he cites the precedent of earlier Egyptian payments. Wenamun appeals to the authority of Amun and eventually secures timber after goods are sent from Egypt. The damaged ending leaves him in Alashiya, usually identified with Cyprus. [@ael, 2:224–230] [@cos1, 1.41]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The terms over which Wenamun and Zakar-Baal bargain give context to the exchange of timber for provisions between Solomon and [[hiram-of-tyre|Hiram]] in 1 Kgs 5:6–11 (MT 5:20–25). The Gebalites who help prepare construction materials in 1 Kgs 5:18 (MT 5:32) and repair ship seams in Ezek 27:9 illustrate the association of Byblos, or Gebal, with crafts related to [[phoenician-seafaring|maritime commerce]]. Josh 17:11 includes Dor in Manasseh's territorial allotment. Wenamun's narrative calls it a town of the Tjeker, one of the groups conventionally called the [[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]]. The two texts present the town within different political settings. [@abd, 2:922–923] [@cos1, 1.41]

The scene of possession at Byblos is early evidence for a Levantine tradition of inspired speech in a ritual setting. The prophetic company in 1 Sam 10:5–13 offers a comparison, as do accounts of people speaking under divine impulse at Mari. The settings and purposes differ. At Byblos the words prompt an audience, while in Samuel Saul's inspired activity accompanies his designation as ruler. [@cos1, 1.41] [@nissinen-prophets, 219–221]

## Scholarly Issues

Some scholars have read the work as an actual report or a literary adaptation of one. Many now see a literary composition using official report conventions. The first-person voice and precise details make Wenamun's plight vivid. His lack of control abroad is compatible with Egypt's reduced political leverage at the end of the New Kingdom, even if the itinerary and exchanges cannot be treated as documentary records. [@ael, 2:224–230] [@cos1, 1.41]
