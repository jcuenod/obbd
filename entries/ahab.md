## Overview

Ahab, son of Omri, ruled the northern kingdom of Israel from Samaria in the ninth century BCE. The Assyrian king Shalmaneser III names him among the rulers who fought against Assyria at Qarqar in 853 BCE, alongside Damascus and Hamath. This inscription gives Ahab a place in the military history of Syria and the Levant. Kings omits the battle and focuses on his marriage to Jezebel, his patronage of Baal, and his encounters with prophets. The Assyrian and biblical accounts illuminate different aspects of the same reign.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Shalmaneser records the [[battle-of-qarqar|battle of Qarqar]] on one of the [[kurkh-monoliths|Kurkh Monoliths]]. His account names Ahab of Israel alongside Hadadezer of Damascus and Irhuleni of Hamath. It credits Ahab with 2,000 chariots and 10,000 troops. The chariot figure is unusually high and may reflect exaggeration or a copying error. Even with that uncertainty, the prominent place of Israel's force in the account points to the military weight the Assyrian court attached to it. [@rima3]

The [[mesha-stele|Mesha Stele]], a victory inscription from Moab, reports that [[omri|Omri]] had oppressed Moab and that his son continued the policy. The inscription names Omri but leaves the son unnamed. The expression can be read as a reference to Ahab or more broadly to a successor in Omri's dynasty. Kings dates the Moabite revolt after Ahab's death (2 Kgs 1:1 and 3:5), and the two sources organize the history differently. Their points of agreement establish Israelite domination and subsequent Moabite resistance more securely than they establish the sequence of individual campaigns. [@kai] [@ahituv-echoes]

## Biblical Portrayal

The account of Ahab's accession emphasizes his marriage to [[jezebel|Jezebel]], daughter of the Sidonian king, and his construction of a temple for Baal at Samaria (1 Kgs 16:29–33). This judgment frames his part in the stories of Elijah and other prophets. In the story of Naboth's vineyard, the confrontation takes place at [[jezreel|Jezreel]], where Naboth holds land next to the royal residence (1 Kgs 21:1–16). The account closes the reign by referring readers to a royal record for Ahab's buildings and his ivory house (1 Kgs 22:39).

## Historical Assessment

Excavations at Samaria uncovered substantial stone construction on its royal hill, and excavators at Jezreel documented a large fortified enclosure that they attributed to the Omride period. These remains illustrate the resources available to the dynasty. The carved [[samaria-ivories|Samaria ivories]] also show how luxuriously a royal center could be furnished. It is difficult to associate individual pieces with Ahab's building work or to locate Naboth's vineyard from the excavated remains. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

The alliance at Qarqar places Ahab alongside Damascus, while 1 Kgs 20:1–34 depicts him resisting the Aramean ruler [[ben-hadad|Ben-hadad]] and 1 Kgs 22:1–38 ends with his death in battle against Aram at Ramoth-gilead. Regional alliances could change, and Kings itself mentions a period without war between the two kingdoms. Some scholars also propose that one or both war accounts preserve traditions about later Israelite rulers, with Jehoahaz or Jehoash often suggested for the conflict in 1 Kings 20. The identity of Ben-hadad in relation to the Hadadezer of the inscriptions is debated. Qarqar gives a secure point in Ahab's career, while the historical setting of the war stories and the circumstances of his death remain open questions.
