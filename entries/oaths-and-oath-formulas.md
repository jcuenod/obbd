## Overview

An oath in ancient Israel or the ancient Near East invoked a binding witness or consequence to guarantee a promise or the truth of a statement. Oaths could settle a dispute, confirm loyalty, or place a commitment under divine sanction. Their wording sometimes names the divine witness and states the penalty. In other cases the words leave the penalty unstated, while a gesture or familiar formula conveys the force of the undertaking. These conventions help explain why several biblical oaths sound incomplete or have a force opposite to a literal English rendering.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Akkadian records use formulas invoking the life of a god or king. The authority invoked depended on the institutions and parties involved. Treaties could call on numerous gods to witness the agreement and punish violations, while private cases could call for an oath to establish a disputed fact. The wording and function vary among royal letters, contracts, court records, and [[covenant-and-treaty-form|treaties]].

Mesopotamian law collections illustrate the exculpatory oath, an oath that clears a party of liability under specified conditions. Hammurabi’s Laws §266 provides an example concerning a loss from a herd that a herdsman attributes to divine action or a lion. The herdsman clears himself before the god, and the owner bears the loss. Such provisions make the oath part of assigning responsibility in cases that may lack eyewitnesses. Its force depends on the sanction attached to a false statement.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 14:22–23 Abram raises his hand to God and renounces a claim to spoil. The gesture accompanies a verbal undertaking. In Gen 24:2–9 Abraham’s servant places his hand under Abraham’s thigh to swear about a wife for Isaac. The gesture may evoke kinship or descendants, especially given the subject of the oath. Genesis does not explain it. Deut 32:40 depicts God lifting a hand to heaven and swearing by his own life. Amos 8:14 attaches similar formulas to objects of illicit devotion, showing how invoking an authority could itself become a point of dispute.

A conditional self-curse calls for harm to fall on the speaker if the declaration proves false. Ruth 1:17 makes the appeal to divine sanction explicit while leaving the harm itself unspecified. Saul invokes divine punishment in 1 Sam 14:39, 44–45, and the people oppose him with an oath of their own. The consequences may be entirely implicit. In these elliptical formulas Hebrew *ʾim*, “if,” can introduce what the speaker swears will never happen. *ʾIm lōʾ*, “if not,” can introduce what the speaker swears will happen. In 2 Sam 3:35 David’s self-curse shows why a conditional expression about tasting food functions as a refusal.

Exod 22:10–11 (MT 22:9–10) provides for an oath before YHWH when livestock entrusted to another person is lost without a witness. Num 5:19–22 describes a ritual oath invoking a specific curse in a different kind of dispute. In Gen 31:53 different ancestral names for God appear in the exchange between Jacob and Laban. Ezek 17:13–19 treats the violation of a political oath as an offense with divine consequences.

## Interpretive Significance

Oaths supplied a framework for trust in speech where circumstances left facts uncertain or commitments vulnerable to reversal. They could also place unequal burdens on parties, as the procedure in Numbers illustrates. In Matt 5:33–37 Jesus addresses familiar formulas involving heaven, earth, Jerusalem, and one’s head, and commends plain affirmations and denials. The background explains why the alternatives were intelligible as ways of making words binding.
