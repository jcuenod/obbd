## Overview

Biblical prayer takes bodily form through standing, kneeling, lifting or spreading the hands, and turning toward a sanctuary. Such gestures also appear in ancient Near Eastern texts and images. Their use can express attention, dependence, praise, or appeal, depending on the setting. They do not always carry a single meaning or form a prescribed sequence. Comparisons with Egyptian and Mesopotamian evidence clarify the familiarity of particular gestures, while biblical accounts show how worshipers used them in public worship, private prayer, and moments of distress.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In Akkadian, *nīš qāti* means “lifting of the hand.” Mesopotamian scribes also used the Sumerian designation *šuʾila*, “hand-lifting,” for a type of prayer. These labels connect bodily action and speech within ritual performance. A designation alone does not show exactly how long the hand remained raised or whether the posture was identical across ceremonies.

Images supply different evidence. Mesopotamian worshiper statues can show hands brought together before the body. Egyptian artists depicted forms of adoration with hands raised. At [[hazor|Hazor]] an engraved stele depicts hands beneath a celestial emblem, and painted storage jar fragments from [[kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet ʿAjrud]] show figures with raised arms. These gestures are commonly interpreted in relation to worship. Images do not preserve the spoken words, and identification of the figures and particular occasions can remain uncertain.

## In the Hebrew Bible

First Samuel 1:26 remembers Hannah standing for prayer, a posture also intelligible for public address. In 1 Kgs 8:22 Solomon stands before the altar and spreads his hands. Verse 8:54 depicts him rising from kneeling after his prayer. The sequence gives more than one posture in the same scene. In 8:38 the text envisages an individual spreading hands toward the temple, and Ps 28:2 connects raised hands with the sanctuary.

Exod 9:29, 33 depicts Moses spreading his hands in appeal during the plagues. Exod 17:11–12 connects Moses’ raised hands with the fighting against Amalek, though the passage does not explicitly narrate a prayer. Ps 63:4 (MT 63:5) and Ps 134:2 use hand lifting in praise or blessing, and Ps 141:2 associates it with the evening offering. Lam 2:19 directs the same kind of gesture toward urgent petition on behalf of children. In 1 Tim 2:8 raising the hands belongs to prayer and is linked to an appeal for conduct free from anger and contention.

Ezra 9:5 depicts the speaker falling to his knees and stretching out his hands. Dan 6:10 (MT 6:11) presents regular kneeling toward Jerusalem under a royal prohibition. First Kings 18:42 depicts Elijah bent low with his face between his knees, another position outside a simple list of fixed forms. Orientation can make a place of worship present to a person at a distance. Bowing and [[prostration-and-obeisance|full prostration]] also belong to the larger vocabulary of deference and worship.

## Interpretive Significance

Standing appears frequently enough to be regarded as an ordinary posture, with kneeling and raised hands also well attested. A gesture’s meaning follows from the setting and from words spoken with it. The same raised hands can accompany praise, a request for relief, or public acknowledgment of divine authority. Ancient parallels help make those actions visible to modern readers, while the narrative and ritual context determines how each passage uses them.
