## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern law collections are written compilations of legal provisions from Mesopotamia and Anatolia. The surviving examples span the late third to the first millennium BCE and include the famous inscription of Hammurabi of Babylon. They show how scribes framed questions about injury, theft, marriage, labor and property, and how kings presented themselves as providers of justice. They provide a setting for the legal collections in Exodus, Leviticus and Deuteronomy, which use some of the same forms and deal with many of the same problems.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The earliest large collections in Sumerian are the [[laws-of-ur-nammu|Laws of Ur-Nammu]] (c. 2100 BCE) and the [[laws-of-lipit-ishtar|Laws of Lipit-Ishtar]] (c. 1930 BCE). The Akkadian [[laws-of-eshnunna|Laws of Eshnunna]] and the [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] followed around the eighteenth century BCE. In Anatolia, the [[hittite-laws|Hittite Laws]] preserve early provisions and later revisions. The principal surviving tablets of the [[middle-assyrian-laws|Middle Assyrian Laws]] come from around the eleventh century BCE and transmit older material. A fragmentary Neo-Babylonian collection is usually assigned to around the seventh century BCE. [@roth-law]

The collections generally put a case and its consequence together. A provision might explain what happens if an animal damages property or kills someone. Such an if–then provision is called *casuistic* law. A direct command or prohibition without an explicit conditional clause is called *apodictic* law. The distinction, associated with Albrecht Alt, is useful for describing form, but scholars no longer regard direct commands as peculiar to Israel.

Most scholars call these writings collections because they differ from comprehensive statutory codes. Court records rarely cite them, and individual collections leave many ordinary disputes unaddressed. Jean Bottéro and Fritz Rudolf Kraus, in different ways, emphasized their scholarly or royal literary purposes. Raymond Westbrook argued that they could also be understood as reference works within a broadly shared legal tradition. The surviving evidence allows different settings for different texts, from scribal exercises to Hammurabi's monumental account of royal justice. [@roth-law]

## In the Hebrew Bible

The collection commonly called the Covenant Code lies within Exod 20:22–23:33. Its provisions move between worship, treatment of dependent people and disputes over injuries or property. Exodus presents them as divine speech at Sinai. In Exod 21:28–32, for instance, a series of cases distinguishes an unexpected fatal goring from a death caused by an animal whose owner was warned of its dangerous habits. Other Near Eastern collections make a comparable distinction, though the remedies and penalties vary. [@roth-law]

The Holiness Code in Lev 17–26 and the Deuteronomic collection in Deut 12–26 also combine matters of worship with obligations to other people. Lev 19:9–10 grounds an instruction to leave produce for the poor and the resident foreigner in the authority of Israel's God. Deut 15:12–18 addresses the people directly and explains the obligation to furnish released Hebrew dependents with provisions by recalling Israel's slavery in Egypt. Such explanations of why a command should be obeyed are often called motive clauses.

## Interpretive Significance

David P. Wright argues that the Covenant Code directly reworks the Laws of Hammurabi, pointing to correspondences in the sequence and selection of cases as well as their subject matter. Others explain the similarities by the circulation of legal cases and traditions among scribes, without requiring the use of that particular work. Similarity in a single ruling gives less evidence of direct use than a sustained sequence of corresponding rulings or phrasing.

A related debate concerns revision within the Bible. Bernard M. Levinson argues that Deuteronomy revises earlier laws, including parts of the Covenant Code. The requirement in Deut 15:12–18 to equip newly freed dependents with produce can be compared with Exod 21:2–6, which specifies release after six years and discusses the household circumstances of the freed person. Reading these as revisions raises a further question about the way the Bible presents different measures as expressions of divine authority.
