## Overview

Royal ideology comprises claims about a ruler's authority, obligations and relationship to the gods. Egyptian, Mesopotamian and Levantine sources represent kings as chosen or empowered by the gods to uphold justice, secure prosperity and defeat enemies. Biblical texts apply comparable language to the kings of Israel and Judah, especially the Davidic dynasty. They also hold kings responsible for oppression and place limits on royal power. The resulting tension between divine commission and accountability shapes royal hymns, prophetic hopes and narratives of failed rule.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian titulary presents the pharaoh in relation to Horus and Re and assigns him responsibility for maintaining *maat*, the order of society and the cosmos. Temple imagery shows rulers subduing enemies and bringing offerings. In Mesopotamia the prologue and epilogue to the [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] portray the king as appointed to establish justice and protect people from oppression. An Assyrian coronation hymn for Ashurbanipal petitions the gods for the king's truth and justice, long reign, affordable goods and agricultural plenty. These claims make good rule visible in both institutions and material well-being. [@anep] [@roth-law] [@cos1, 1.142]

Biblical language for [[justice-and-righteousness|justice and righteousness]] in 2 Sam 8:15 can be compared with Akkadian expressions such as *kittum u mīšarum*, truth and justice. Royal release edicts give substance to the comparison by showing what declarations of just rule could mean for debtors and others at risk. The corresponding terms and measures differed between societies. [@roth-law] [@abd]

## In the Hebrew Bible

The promise in 2 Sam 7:8–16 gives [[david|David]] a dynasty and describes YHWH's relation to his successor as that of father and son. Ps 89:19–37 (MT 89:20–38) reworks this promise as a lasting covenant. Ps 2 connects royal installation on Zion and filial language with rule over the nations. Ps 110 depicts a ruler seated at YHWH's right hand and declares a priesthood associated with Melchizedek. Ps 20 and 21 pray for and celebrate victory. [@abd] [@keel-symbolism]

In Ps 72, judgment for the poor, abundance and the reach of the king's rule belong together, inviting comparison with Ashurbanipal's coronation hymn. Isa 9:6–7 (MT 9:5–6) gives a royal child throne names and anticipates a just Davidic reign. Isa 11:1–9 expects a ruler who receives YHWH's spirit and judges the poor fairly. Deut 17:14–20 subjects the king to the law and restricts horses, wives and wealth. 1 Sam 8:11–18 warns that a king can appropriate land, labor and produce. [@cos1, 1.142] [@abd]

## Interpretive Significance

Twentieth-century Myth and Ritual scholars including Sigmund Mowinckel and Ivan Engnell used royal texts across cultures to reconstruct ritual dramas or propose forms of divine kingship in Judah. Later studies have questioned such broad reconstructions and emphasized the differences between local texts and institutions. Davidic [[king-as-son-of-god|sonship]] is generally read as the sacral status of a ruler chosen by YHWH. In Ps 45:6–7 (MT 45:7–8), Hebrew *ʾĕlōhîm* can be read as addressing the king, though the grammar and implications are debated. Many scholars assign parts of the royal psalms to the monarchy, with later reuse for [[messianic-expectations|messianic hopes]]. The original occasions and dates of individual poems remain disputed.
