## Overview

The Babylonian exile began with the forced removal of Judeans after Babylon's capture of Jerusalem in 597 BCE and continued with further deportations after the city and its temple were destroyed in 587 or 586 BCE. The experience differed among the deportees, who included royalty, skilled workers, and families settled in the Babylonian countryside. Many people remained in Judah, and many of the deportees' descendants remained in Babylonia after Persia took power. The period is central to the historical setting of Jeremiah and Ezekiel and to the Bible's accounts of loss, survival, and return.

## Sources

A Babylonian chronicle dates the first capture of Jerusalem to March 597 BCE [@abc, 102]. The [[jehoiachin-ration-tablets|Jehoiachin ration tablets]] name the deported king of Judah among the people receiving oil from the Babylonian palace, along with members of his family. These records attest his support by the palace during Nebuchadnezzar's reign. Kings describes a later change in his status under a new king (2 Kgs 25:27–30).

Business and administrative records from rural Babylonia document a different part of the population. The [[al-yahudu-tablets|Āl-Yāhūdu tablets]], which date from 572 BCE into the fifth century BCE, attest Judean families in settlements that included Āl-Yāhūdu, “Town of Judah.” Some families farmed land subject to obligations to the state, and documents also show Judeans taking part in credit and other local transactions. These arrangements gave some households room to organize their lives while retaining duties imposed by the authorities. The later [[murashu-archive|Murašû archive]] from Nippur attests people with Judean names in the Persian-period economy.

## Reconstruction

The first deportation followed Jerusalem's surrender to [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]]. The [[fall-of-jerusalem-587|city's second capture]], after the revolt of Zedekiah, brought the destruction of the temple and palace, with another removal of inhabitants. Jeremiah records a further deportation in Nebuchadnezzar's twenty-third year, usually placed around 582 BCE (Jer 52:28–30). The event that prompted it remains uncertain.

The destruction of Jerusalem and settlements in Judah's western foothills was severe. Hans Barstad challenged the idea that the land was left empty. Archaeological work summarized by Oded Lipschits points to continuing occupation in Benjamin, immediately north of Jerusalem, and a decline in many other areas. The scale of population loss remains debated. Local continuity and severe destruction are both visible in the evidence.

## Biblical Accounts

Kings describes the first deportees as including the king, other members of the court, and skilled workers (2 Kgs 24:10–16). It says that people remained to work the land after the second capture, and later reports that some went to Egypt after the killing of the Babylonian-appointed governor Gedaliah (2 Kgs 25:8–26). The numbers given for deportation in Kings and Jer 52:28–30 differ. They may reflect different categories of people or different records. Their relationship and the total number removed are unknown.

Jer 29:4–7 tells the deportees to build houses, plant gardens, and seek the welfare of their new city. Ezekiel places himself among the exiles by the Chebar and names the settlement of Tel-abib (Ezek 1:1–3; 3:15). These texts are consistent with the existence of settled Judean communities. Ps 137 instead expresses the pain of displacement and attachment to Jerusalem.

Jer 25:11–12 describes seventy years of Babylonian domination, an interval often read as a rounded or schematic period. Ezra places Cyrus's authorization to rebuild the Jerusalem temple and go to Judah in his first year, conventionally reckoned as 538 BCE following the Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE (Ezra 1:1–4). The [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]] provides evidence of restoration measures elsewhere, while the Judean policy is reported in Ezra. Return was partial and unfolded over time.
