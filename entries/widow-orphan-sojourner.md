## Overview

Biblical laws and prophets frequently name widows, orphans, and resident outsiders as people liable to lose access to land, resources, or justice. Ancient Near Eastern rulers also presented defense of widows and orphans as a duty of government. The Bible extends such concern through court procedures, provisions for harvest and tithes, and appeals to Israel’s experience in Egypt. Psalms portray God as the protector of these people. The comparison helps explain how a familiar royal ideal became a standard for judging rulers, communities, and worshipers.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The prologue to the laws associated with Ur-Nammu says that the weak were protected from people with greater wealth or power. The epilogue to the [[code-of-hammurabi|Laws of Hammurabi]] similarly presents royal justice as protection for widows and orphans. These claims announce an ideal of government. They leave open how consistently officials protected vulnerable people in practice.

In the Ugaritic [[aqhat-epic|Aqhat story]], Danel judges the cases of the widow and orphan. In the Kirta narrative similar responsibilities appear as a standard against which royal conduct can be criticized. The Egyptian [[eloquent-peasant|Eloquent Peasant]] likewise appeals to the duty of an official toward people lacking powerful support. These accounts show that the widow and orphan could serve as a recognizable pair across societies with different legal institutions.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Exod 22:21–24 (MT 22:20–23) prohibits mistreating the resident outsider and oppressing widows and orphans. Deut 10:18 portrays God executing justice for the widow and orphan and providing for the outsider. Deut 14:28–29 includes these groups among recipients of the local tithe, alongside Levites. Deut 24:17–22 forbids unfair judgment and reserves part of the harvest for people in need, and Deut 27:19 condemns perversion of their justice. The remembrance of Israel’s own experience in Egypt gives the rules a historical and moral rationale.

Ps 68:5 (MT 68:6) pictures God as a father to orphans and defender of widows. Ps 82:3–4 calls for justice and rescue for the weak. Ps 146:9 brings the resident outsider, orphan, and widow together under God’s care. Isa 1:17, 23 and Jer 22:3 apply the ideal to public conduct and leadership. Mal 3:5 includes oppression in a list of wrongs subject to divine judgment, while Jas 1:27 makes attention to orphans and widows part of its account of faithful practice.

## Interpretive Significance

The Hebrew term *gēr* often designates a resident who comes from outside the ordinary kinship structure and may depend on protection within a community. Its implications vary by law and period. Greek translations use *prosēlytos* in some passages, a term that later came to have a more specific association with conversion. That later usage should not govern every earlier reference. Scholars also debate whether the biblical term for widow sometimes indicates a woman lacking adult male support. The social risk matters for interpretation even when a text does not define the beneficiary’s family circumstances.

The familiar pairing of widow and orphan and the recurring inclusion of an outsider make vulnerability visible in concrete terms. Biblical law locates the response in practices people could carry out, from fair hearings to gathering crops left behind. These instructions, appeals, and accusations give the ancient royal ideal a wider range of applications.
