## Overview

Mesopotamian rituals for installing a divine statue included washing and opening its mouth. In ritual texts the acts prepare an image to receive offerings and act as a deity's cult presence. Ancient Egypt also had an opening-of-the-mouth ceremony for statues and the dead. The two traditions have distinct procedures and settings. They provide background for biblical texts that emphasize the inability of images to speak, breathe, or respond, including the polemic against manufactured gods in Isaiah and the Psalms.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Mesopotamian names *mīs pî* and *pīt pî* refer to washing and opening the mouth. Most of the detailed evidence comes from first-millennium ritual tablets from places such as Nineveh and Babylonia. During induction, the statue was ritually purified and moved through spaces associated with its preparation and entry into the temple. The ceremony marked its transformation from a workshop product to a [[cult-images|cult image]], capable in ritual terms of receiving food and other offerings.

The published ritual instructions include a symbolic removal of the craftsmen's hands with a tamarisk implement and a denial of human manufacture. The denial belongs to the ceremony that gives the image its cultic standing, even though people carved and assembled it. References elsewhere may attest earlier forms of the ritual. The surviving handbooks provide the firmer basis for describing its specific prayers and actions.

In Egypt, a ceremony conventionally called the Opening of the Mouth could be performed on a statue or mummy. Ritual scenes depict implements, including ones shaped like an adze, brought to the face. The ceremony prepared the recipient to exercise faculties such as breathing or receiving food. The Egyptian funerary uses and equipment are particular to their own tradition.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Isa 44:9–20 the prophet describes people cutting wood and using part of it to make an image. He emphasizes the bodily labor and ordinary tools of the makers. That emphasis is particularly pointed when compared with the Mesopotamian ritual's denial that craftsmen made the god. Jer 10:3–5, 9 also foregrounds human fabrication and describes an image that cannot speak. Human effort and precious materials give the object form and finish.

Ps 115:4–8 gives the images mouths that cannot speak and other bodily features that cannot function. Ps 135:15–18 adds that the images have no breath in their mouths. Hab 2:18–19 describes an image plated with gold and silver and asks whether it can teach. The ability to speak or breathe is precisely what separates a responsive god from the object portrayed in these passages.

## Interpretive Significance

Read against worship practices in surrounding cultures, the biblical descriptions focus on capacities that the induction rituals claimed to furnish. They can be understood as challenges to the claims made about an image in worship. The parallels provide context for the polemic, while the wording does not establish a particular ritual text as the biblical authors' source.

In Isa 6:5–7 a seraph touches Isaiah's unclean lips with a coal from the altar, and the prophet is told that his guilt is removed. Victor Hurowitz compared this purification of a human mouth with Akkadian evidence. The shared concern with preparing a mouth for its role makes the comparison illuminating even though Isaiah's scene has a different object and purpose.
