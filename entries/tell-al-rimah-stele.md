## Overview

A stone stele of the Assyrian king Adad-nirari III was recovered at Tell al-Rimah in northern Iraq in 1967. The inscription recounts a western campaign and names tribute from Joash of Samaria alongside payments from other rulers and peoples. The name and designation match Joash, king of Israel, in 2 Kings 13. The stele also credits the Assyrian governor Nergal-ereš with settlement activity. It brings the international pressures on Israel into view and preserves the Assyrian court's account of tribute during the same period as Israel's conflict with Damascus.

## Description and Provenance

The excavators found the stele in an Assyrian temple context. The stone is a grey material described by the first editor as Mosul marble. It bears a carved figure of the king and an Akkadian cuneiform inscription of twenty-one lines. Part of the text was deliberately erased in antiquity. The portions that remain legible record the foreign campaign and reflect the value of the monument as a local statement of royal authority [@rima3, 210–212; @cos2, 2.114F].

## Contents

The inscription begins with a dedication to Adad and a statement of royal ancestry. The campaign section describes the king receiving tribute after advancing west. Line 8 lists tribute from Iaʾasu of Samaria, together with tribute from Tyre and Sidon. The king also recounts receiving a payment from Mariʾ of Damascus. The text attributes success to the authority of the Assyrian king and his god. Lines 13–21 concern the governor Nergal-ereš and the establishment or restoration of settlements, followed by a curse. The governor's section was erased in antiquity, but enough remains for substantial reconstruction [@rima3, 210–212; @cos2, 2.114F].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Joash rules Israel from Samaria in 2 Kgs 13:10–13, and the chapter describes his recovery of towns from Ben-hadad after the death of Hazael (2 Kgs 13:22–25). The stele gives independent evidence that a ruler with that name at Samaria paid tribute to Assyria. Its mention of the city also fits the account in 1 Kgs 16:24 that Omri established Samaria as a royal center. The Assyrian inscription's use of Samaria and the better-known Assyrian designation based on Omri illustrate how the same polity could be named in different ways.

Some identify the “savior” raised up for Israel in 2 Kgs 13:5 with Adad-nirari III or understand the notice against the background of Assyrian pressure on Damascus. Such pressure gives a plausible setting for diminished Aramaean control. The biblical verse leaves the savior unnamed, and the stele describes tribute rather than intervention for Israel's benefit.

## Scholarly Issues

The text presents the western victories as achievements of a single campaign, in keeping with the compression customary in royal inscriptions. Its relation to dated Assyrian campaign records is used to narrow the likely historical setting, but the individual notices need not share a precise year. The erasure also leaves open when and why Nergal-ereš's contribution was removed.
