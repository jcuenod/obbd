## Overview

The Meṣad Ḥashavyahu ostracon is a late seventh-century BCE Hebrew letter written on a piece of pottery. It was found in 1960 at a coastal fortress near Yavneh-Yam. A worker asks an official to return a garment taken by a man named Hoshayahu. The petition is a rare account of a complaint from someone engaged in harvesting. It gives a social setting for biblical laws about retaining a debtor's cloak and for the role of witnesses, while the reason for the seizure and an expression about stopping work remain debated.

## Description and Provenance

Joseph Naveh's excavation recovered the letter at Meṣad Ḥashavyahu, a small fort near the Mediterranean shore. The pottery and script place the occupation and the letter in the late Iron Age. Meṣad Ḥashavyahu is a modern name for the site. The surviving Hebrew [[ostraca|ostraca]] and East Greek pottery give evidence for people or connections of more than one kind in the community [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 357–370] [@neaehl, 2:585–586].

## Contents

The writer addresses an official as his lord. He describes his work as a reaper and says that Hoshayahu son of Shobai took his garment. He appeals to other workers as witnesses that he had fulfilled his obligations and asks to have the garment returned. The wording belongs to a petition submitted to someone with power to intervene. It does not preserve the overseer's answer or a judicial ruling.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Exod 22:26–27 (MT 22:25–26) and Deut 24:12–15 protect an impoverished person whose cloak has been taken in pledge and concern treatment of a worker. Amos 2:8 and Job 22:6 likewise associate seized clothing with abuse. The ostracon shows that loss of a garment could prompt an appeal. The writer does not specifically call it a loan pledge, and the seizure may have been a penalty or action connected with the harvest.

The companions invoked in the letter offer a comparison for witnesses in Deut 19:15, though the appeal does not take the form of a court record. The official's position also fits the wider role of petitioning a superior in ancient legal practice. The evidence gives a view from the person seeking redress, with the outcome unknown.

## Scholarly Issues

A short expression involving *šbt* has been understood as “before the [[sabbath|Sabbath]]” or in relation to stopping work. Amos 8:5 shows that a Sabbath could affect economic activity, but the ostracon's choice of meaning remains disputed. It cannot be used as unqualified proof of a harvest prohibition on the Sabbath.

The political authority behind the fort is also debated. Hebrew letters and names have supported a connection with [[josiah|Judah under Josiah]]. East Greek pottery and the wider coastal setting have prompted reconstructions involving Egyptian authority and Greek soldiers. Cultural materials can circulate across political boundaries. Neither class of evidence identifies the fort's governing authority on its own.
