## Overview

The Pool of Siloam in John is usually identified with a large stepped pool at the southern end of Jerusalem’s ancient southeastern ridge. Excavations begun in 2004 exposed steps and paving dated to the late Second Temple period. Earlier waterworks and a later Byzantine pool also belong to the area, so the name Siloam can refer to different structures. The discovery gives a setting for the washing described in John 9, while the stepped design alone does not establish whether the pool served ritual bathing, water collection, or several purposes.

## Location and Identification

The stepped pool lies near the south end of the ridge often called the [[city-of-david|City of David]], within the area of modern Silwan. Water from the Gihon Spring was conveyed through channels and the [[hezekiahs-tunnel|Siloam Tunnel]] into this lower part of Jerusalem. A smaller pool at the tunnel outlet preserves the name in later tradition. The monument excavated from 2004 lies nearby but occupies a broader basin. The topography, date, and continuity of the name support identifying the larger basin with the setting of John.

## Archaeological History

Excavations directed by Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron exposed stone steps leading down into the basin. Coins and pottery associate its use with the late first century BCE and the first century CE. Earlier phases of Jerusalem’s water system are attested in the area, but a later flight of stairs cannot supply the plan or date of the Iron Age pool. The smaller pool in a later Christian setting likewise reflects subsequent construction and commemoration.

The steps offered access as the water level changed. Some scholars interpret the large basin as a place where pilgrims could undergo immersion. Its scale and relation to the approach toward the temple make that proposal understandable. Other uses include urban water supply or bathing, and the evidence does not require an exclusive function. Excavations have also documented a monumental stepped street leading north toward the temple precinct. Work in Silwan takes place within a populated modern neighborhood and concerns a landscape shared by present residents and archaeological institutions.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

John 9:7 sends the man born blind to wash in the Pool of Siloam and interprets its name within the story. The excavation helps readers place that action within first-century Jerusalem. John 7:37–38 uses water imagery at a feast, sometimes read in light of traditions about drawing water from Siloam. It does not name the pool. Luke 13:4 mentions a tower at Siloam in another part of the remembered urban landscape. Neither the pool nor the street establishes which tower the verse describes.

Isa 8:6 invokes gently flowing waters of Shiloah. Neh 3:15 names a pool near features of Jerusalem’s southern defenses. Second Kings 20:20 credits Hezekiah with a pool and conduit, and Isa 22:9–11 describes preparations that included collecting water. These passages reflect the longstanding importance of water at the lower end of the ridge. They span different periods and do not all refer to the same visible structure.
