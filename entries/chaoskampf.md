## Overview

*Chaoskampf* is a German term meaning “struggle against chaos.” Scholars use it for ancient stories in which a god defeats the Sea or a sea monster and establishes authority. Examples appear in Bronze Age Syria and Anatolia and in Babylonian literature. Biblical poets also present Yahweh as ruling the sea or defeating creatures such as Leviathan and Rahab. The setting and result of the victory vary. In different texts it can explain kingship, describe creation, recall deliverance or express hope for a final defeat of hostile powers.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the Late Bronze Age [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]] from Ugarit, Baal defeats a rival called [[yam|Yam]] (“Sea”). The victory helps secure Baal's royal standing and belongs to the story that leads to the building of his palace. Other Ugaritic passages refer to a twisting serpent with seven heads, associated with traditions about Baal and Anat defeating monstrous opponents. This is the closest linguistic and literary setting for several biblical descriptions of a sea serpent. [@ktu3] [@parker-unp] [@day-dragon]

The motif was known earlier in Syria. A letter from the Mari archives reports a message to Zimri-Lim in which the storm god Addu says he gave the king the weapons he used against Sea. Hittite traditions of the storm god's battle with the serpent Illuyanka offer another comparison. In the Babylonian [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]], Marduk defeats Tiamat and makes parts of the ordered universe from her body. The Babylonian text joins combat with the making of the cosmos, while the Baal Cycle focuses on rule among the gods. [@nissinen-prophets] [@cos1] [@lambert-bcm]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Ps 74:12–17 brings together the defeat of the sea, the crushing of [[leviathan|Leviathan's]] heads and the establishment of features of the world such as day, night and seasons. Ps 89:9–12 places the defeat of [[rahab-sea-monster|Rahab]] and rule over the sea beside the founding of heaven and earth. In Isa 51:9–10 the images of cutting Rahab and drying the sea are brought together with the passage of the redeemed. The poet uses them to recall deliverance and appeal for divine action in the present. [@day-dragon]

Isa 27:1 uses the descriptions “the gliding serpent” and “the coiling serpent” (NIV) for Leviathan. The paired expressions closely match the language of a Ugaritic reference to the serpent. Here the action is placed in the future, when Yahweh will kill the dragon in the sea. Rev 12:3–9 uses the image of a many-headed dragon in a heavenly battle and identifies it with the devil and Satan. [@parker-unp] [@day-dragon]

## Interpretive Significance

Whether Gen 1:2, 21 deliberately recasts a story of divine combat is debated. Hermann Gunkel's influential approach placed the chapter in the wider tradition of creation through victory over threatening waters. Other scholars, including David Tsumura, emphasize that Genesis portrays the deep and the great sea creatures without a struggle and question whether a specific combat myth is in view. The vocabulary and setting invite comparison, while the narrative gives God direct command over the waters and their inhabitants.

Scholars also question whether “chaos” is the best description of every opponent. Yam is a rival claimant to royal power, and a biblical dragon can stand for a threat within history or one expected in the future. Reading each scene within its own story makes it possible to trace the recurrent imagery without assigning every version the same meaning. [@day-dragon]
