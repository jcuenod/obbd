## Overview

Writing in ancient Israel and Judah served administration, correspondence, ritual, and the transmission of literature. Inscriptions show that writing was used in villages, urban centers, and military posts, although the evidence varies greatly by period and place. A trained scribe could compose and copy extensive texts, while other people might read letters, enter quantities, or recognize familiar labels. The surviving texts make that range visible. They give little basis for estimating the proportion of the population that could read or write, and they cannot by themselves date the composition of biblical books.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Egyptian and Mesopotamian archives preserve examples of specialized scribal training and the varied work it supported. The alphabet used in the Levant required mastery of fewer signs than the Mesopotamian cuneiform system. A small sign inventory still left writers to learn conventions of spelling, letter shapes, and document forms. Levantine abecedaries preserve practice in the order or execution of the signs. One example is the [ʿIzbet Ṣarṭah ostracon](izbet-sartah-abecedary.md), with lines of poorly understood writing and a sequence of letters. The script and language of the early Iron Age [Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon](khirbet-qeiyafa-ostracon.md) are disputed, limiting its use as direct evidence for Hebrew composition at that date [@cos1; @ahituv-echoes].

The late Iron Age record includes letters, labels, seals, monumental inscriptions, and accounts. In [Lachish Letter 3](lachish-letters.md), Hoshayahu disputes a charge concerning his ability to read a letter. The exchange implies that competence in reading could matter to an official's standing. Handwriting analysis of a small group of [Arad ostraca](arad-ostraca.md) identified several writers engaged in military administration around 600 BCE. The results support multiple writers in a particular institutional network, and identifying the exact number depends on the method and the attribution of short samples [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions; @ahituv-echoes].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Judg 8:14 a young man supplies written names of the leaders of Succoth. The narrative depicts writing outside a formal scribal scene, but one character gives no measure of the ordinary distribution of the skill. Deut 6:9 and 11:20 command writing on doorposts and gates, while Deut 17:18–19 requires a written copy of the law for the king to read regularly. Deut 27:2–8 puts writing on plastered stones. The [Deir ʿAlla inscription](deir-alla-inscription.md) demonstrates that plaster could bear an extended text in the region.

Other passages show writing as part of specific practices. Num 5:23 has the priest write a curse and wash it into water. Isa 8:1 and Hab 2:2 envisage writing that can be read or recognized by others. Isa 29:11–12 uses contrasting inability to read a sealed text and inability to read at all. Job demands a written charge and offers his mark or signature (Job 31:35). Reading aloud, dictation, and public inscription allowed people with different abilities to take part in a literate culture [@abd].

## Interpretive Significance

The visible growth in surviving inscriptions toward the end of the monarchy supports increased use of writing, especially in administration. Differences in excavation, population, and the decay of papyrus also affect the number of surviving samples. Evidence for regular correspondence shows a capacity that could support literary work, and long compositions required further training, time, and institutions. These distinctions explain why historians disagree over the extent of literacy and the dates when major biblical texts were composed.
