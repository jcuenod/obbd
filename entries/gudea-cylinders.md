## Overview

The Gudea Cylinders are two large clay cylinders inscribed in Sumerian at the end of the third millennium BCE. They come from Girsu in southern Mesopotamia and commemorate the rebuilding and dedication of the Eninnu, the temple of the god Ningirsu. One cylinder describes dreams and preparations for construction. The other describes the god taking up residence and the celebrations that followed. The story is an extended example of a temple-building account and provides a useful comparison for the sequence of preparations, construction, and dedication in the biblical accounts of Solomon's temple.

## Description and Provenance

The two cylinders, conventionally called A and B, were found at Telloh, ancient Girsu, in southern Iraq. Both are held by the Louvre. Scribes wrote the text in columns on the curved surfaces, using cuneiform, a script made with wedge-shaped impressions. The composition is among the longest surviving works in Sumerian. It records a project undertaken by [[gudea|Gudea]], ruler of Lagash, a state in which Girsu was an important center. [@cos2]

## Contents

Cylinder A begins in the world of the gods and presents Ningirsu's decision to have his temple built. Gudea receives a dream whose images require interpretation. He consults the goddess Nanshe, who explains that Ningirsu wants him to build the temple. The account moves from the divine instructions to the assembling of labor, the arrival of building materials, and the construction itself. The narrative presents preparations as part of carrying out a divine commission. [@cos2]

Cylinder B describes the entry of Ningirsu and his consort Baba and the arrangements that support their residence. The dedication includes offerings and celebration. The account portrays a temporary easing of social distinctions and restrictions during the festivities. These claims express the order and abundance expected to accompany the god's presence. The composition ends with praise of Ningirsu. [@cos2]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Victor Hurowitz identified a recurring sequence in ancient [[temple-building-accounts|temple-building accounts]] that includes a decision, divine authorization, preparation, construction, dedication, and blessing. The cylinders give an early, elaborate example. In 1 Kgs 5:1–10, Solomon secures materials and labor for the temple. The account later culminates in sacrifice and celebration at the dedication (1 Kgs 8:62–66). These points of comparison concern how a ruler's actions and the installation of worship are narrated. [@hurowitz-exalted-house]

Exod 25:8–9 associates the sanctuary with a pattern shown to Moses. In 1 Chr 28:11–19, David gives Solomon a plan associated with divine instruction. Gudea's dream performs a related function in authorizing the work, although the story uses different means to communicate and explain the design. These texts show how instructions from a deity can give a sanctuary's construction special authority. [@hurowitz-exalted-house]

## Scholarly Issues

Some scholars have proposed a lost earlier cylinder. Others regard the beginning of A and the sequence continuing through B as coherent enough to stand on their own. The surviving account is extensive whichever reconstruction is preferred. Its much earlier date and its language make it especially valuable as evidence for the long history of the genre. The comparison with Kings does not identify a direct line of borrowing from this specific Sumerian work. [@cos2] [@hurowitz-exalted-house]
