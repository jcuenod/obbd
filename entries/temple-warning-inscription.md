## Overview

Two Greek inscriptions from Jerusalem preserve a warning associated with the inner courts of Herod’s temple. They forbid foreigners to pass the surrounding barrier and make an intruder responsible for the death that would follow. Josephus describes such notices, and Acts portrays a disturbance over an accusation that Paul brought a gentile into a restricted area. The inscriptions give direct evidence for the boundary. They leave open how its penalty was enforced and whether later Christian language about a dividing wall refers to this particular structure.

## Description and Provenance

A nearly complete stone was found in 1871 reused near the temple precinct and is now held in Istanbul. A fragment of another copy was recovered in Jerusalem in 1935 and is held in the Israel Museum. Neither was found in its original position. Their shared wording and the descriptions of Josephus connect them with the balustrade, a low barrier separating the inner precinct from areas open to foreigners. The barrier is often called the *soreg*.

## Contents

The Greek inscription prohibits any foreigner from entering within the barrier and enclosure around the sanctuary. It adds that anyone caught will be responsible for the death that follows. The wording places responsibility on the person who crosses the line. It does not name a court or explain who would act on the warning. Josephus (*War* 5.193–194, 6.124–126, *Ant.* 15.417) mentions the restriction and says notices appeared in Greek and Latin. The two surviving examples are Greek. Philo also refers to restricted access to the sanctuary.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Acts 21:27–30 portrays a crowd accusing Paul of bringing Greeks into the temple and defiling it. The narrative identifies the accusation as an inference drawn from seeing him elsewhere with Trophimus. Acts 24:6 includes an allegation of attempted profanation. The inscription gives a concrete setting for the accusation and for the seriousness of the perceived crossing. It does not attest that Paul or Trophimus actually passed the boundary.

Ezek 44:9 and Lam 1:10 connect admission to sacred space with boundaries of communal identity. They belong to earlier literary contexts. Mark 11:17 invokes prayer for all nations, a broader claim that does not describe the administrative layout of the courts. Eph 2:14 pictures a dividing wall overcome. Some readers see an allusion to the temple barrier. The surrounding argument also supports reading the wall as a general metaphor for hostility. An architectural reference remains possible rather than necessary.

## Scholarly Issues

Josephus portrays the restriction as one the Roman authorities recognized. The surviving warning does not distinguish formal execution, immediate violence, or other ways of understanding the predicted death. John 18:31 concerns a different legal situation. It cannot settle the scope of an exception involving the temple. The epigraphic evidence is strongest for the formal distinction between areas and for the consequences the notice threatened.
