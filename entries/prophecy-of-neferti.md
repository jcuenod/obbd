## Overview

The Prophecy of Neferti is an Egyptian literary work that sets a speech about the future in the court of King Sneferu. The sage Neferti describes social upheaval and a failing Nile, then predicts that a ruler called Ameny will restore order. The name and themes are commonly associated with Amenemhat I and the beginning of the Twelfth Dynasty. The combination of an older narrative setting and a later ruler makes the work useful in studying retrospective predictions, royal legitimacy, and biblical images of disorder and restoration.

## Manuscripts and Date

The principal manuscript is Papyrus Hermitage 1116B, an Eighteenth Dynasty copy. Other witnesses include writing boards and ostraca. The work is commonly assigned to the early Middle Kingdom on literary and historical grounds. That date is a proposal about composition, since the surviving manuscripts are later. The court of Sneferu, a Fourth Dynasty ruler, provides the setting in which the narrative places the speech.

## Contents

Sneferu asks for entertainment or instruction at court, and Neferti speaks about what lies ahead. His account presents a river that no longer flows as expected, outsiders in the Delta, and reversals in family and social relations. The catalogue of disasters describes an Egypt in which the ordinary order has failed. The prediction that Ameny will restore order gives a ruler the role of remedying those failures. The text also speaks of frontier defenses known as the Walls of the Ruler, a feature that belongs to its vision of protecting Egypt’s eastern approach.

The ruler’s identity with Amenemhat I is widely accepted. The narrative gives that identification force indirectly by placing the prediction long before Amenemhat’s reign. Literary images of upheaval have parallels in the [[admonitions-of-ipuwer|Admonitions of Ipuwer]]. They show a repertoire for imagining disorder, without supplying a straightforward report of living conditions in the First Intermediate Period.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The historical setting and prediction invite comparison with *vaticinium ex eventu*, a prediction put into the past after some of its events have taken place. Many scholars use this category for parts of Dan 11:2–45, where the historical sequence can be followed in detail until the account reaches its writer’s horizon. First Kings 13:2 names Josiah well before his reign within the story, another example of a prediction given significance by a later event. The Egyptian setting and aims of royal legitimation differ from the biblical works, so comparison is strongest at the level of literary form.

Isa 11:1–9 pictures a ruler who brings justice and transformed conditions, and Isa 19:5–8 portrays the failing Nile as a source of devastation. Mic 7:6 uses conflict within families to convey disintegration. These motifs allow comparison with the Egyptian work without establishing a shared source. Gen 16:7 and Exod 15:22 mention Shur in the Egyptian border region. Its possible association with a word for “wall” and with frontier defenses such as those in Neferti remains hypothetical.

## Scholarly Issues

The designation prophecy names the work’s use of a prediction within the story. It need not assign Neferti to the same social institution as the prophets known from Israel or Mesopotamia. Ancient readers could encounter the composition as a work of scribal literature long after its initial setting. Distinguishing the date of the fictional court, the likely purpose of the composition, and the date of its copies keeps its different kinds of historical evidence in view.
