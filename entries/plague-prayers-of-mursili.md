## Overview

Ḫatti’s king Muršili II addressed prayers to the gods during an epidemic that the texts say had continued for about twenty years. The Late Bronze Age prayers seek an explanation through earlier records and divine inquiry. They consider neglected offerings and a broken oath with Egypt, and the king accepts responsibility for addressing wrongs that preceded him. Their combination of confession, argument, and intercession provides a close comparison for biblical accounts in which a ruler responds to public calamity and to offenses committed in an earlier reign.

## Manuscripts and Date

The prayers survive on cuneiform tablets and fragments from the Hittite capital at Ḫattuša. The principal group is catalogued as CTH 378. It belongs to the reign of Muršili II, conventionally placed in the late fourteenth century BCE, although copies need not have been written at the moment of performance. The manuscripts preserve several distinct treatments of the crisis. Their titles and their interpretation as parts of a numbered series owe something to modern editors.

## Contents

In the so-called Second Plague Prayer, Muršili says that the epidemic persisted from the reign of his father Šuppiluliuma I. The prayer links its spread with prisoners brought into Ḫatti during conflict with Egypt. That account preserves the court’s explanation and provides no way to identify a specific illness. The king describes recovering earlier records and consulting the gods through [[divination|oracular inquiry]]. One recorded obligation concerns neglected offerings to the Euphrates, called Mala in Hittite. Another concerns an oath involving Egypt connected with the people of Kuruštama.

Muršili addresses the Storm God and the gods of Ḫatti. He acknowledges a breach that occurred before his own reign, argues that earlier wrongdoing has already brought extensive harm, and asks that the plague end. The form is sometimes described with the Hittite term *arkuwar*, a formal plea or argument. The prayer can therefore resemble a presentation of a case to a superior. Other prayers vary in the explanations they pursue or the gods they address.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Second Samuel 21:1–14 describes David seeking an explanation for famine and being told that Saul’s violence against the Gibeonites is responsible. Josh 9:15–20 places the relationship with those people under an oath. Both the Hittite and biblical cases depict a ruler consulting divine authority in a crisis linked to an older breach. The details of the remedies and the narratives’ purposes differ. The parallel concerns how public suffering and a successor’s responsibility are explained. It does not establish literary dependence.

Second Samuel 24:10–17 joins plague and royal intercession to a wrong David acknowledges as his own. Lam 5:7 gives voice to those who suffer for earlier generations’ actions. Ezek 18:2 cites a proverb about inherited consequences and goes on to challenge its use. These texts show that the relationship between past wrongdoing and present suffering was debated within the Bible itself.

## Scholarly Issues

The Hittite account records royal interpretations of disease, political action, and obligation. Historians can use it as evidence for what the court said and did in response to a crisis. They cannot reconstruct the whole history of the illness from the theological explanations the prayers offer. The strong comparison with Samuel lies in the formal sequence of calamity, inquiry, and response to an oath, with each account presenting its own understandings of cause and obligation.
