## Overview

The Flood Tablet in the British Museum is a seventh-century BCE clay manuscript from Nineveh. It preserves part of Tablet XI of the Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh, in which the survivor Utnapishtim recounts a flood sent by the gods. George Smith announced his identification of the story in 1872. Its account of a boat coming to rest on a mountain, birds testing the waters, and a sacrifice after the flood prompted new comparisons with Genesis. The tablet is one witness to a Mesopotamian tradition that developed long before this copy was made.

## Description and Provenance

The tablet comes from Kuyunjik, one of the mounds of ancient [[nineveh|Nineveh]]. The British Museum registers it as K.3375. Its Akkadian text was written in cuneiform, the wedge-shaped writing impressed on the clay. It belongs to the Neo-Assyrian scholarly collections associated with [[library-of-ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal's library]]. The damaged edges and missing portions are supplemented in modern editions where other copies preserve the same text. [@george-gilgamesh, 1:412]

## Contents

The flood story appears as a speech in the [[gilgamesh-epic|Epic of Gilgamesh]]. Utnapishtim recounts how the god Ea warned him and gave instructions for building a boat. He survived with others on board and sent out birds once the vessel came to rest. He then made an offering, and the gods gathered at its scent. At the end of the episode, the gods give Utnapishtim and his wife a form of life unavailable to ordinary humans. The speech explains why Gilgamesh's journey to the survivor offers little prospect of achieving the same outcome. [@george-gilgamesh, 1:700–725] [@anet, 93–97]

The Babylonian poem incorporates material already attested in the older [[atrahasis|Atrahasis tradition]]. Details of the warning, boat, and aftermath can be compared across both works. K.3375 records one late copy of the story, and the age of the object differs from the age of the material it preserves. [@lambert-millard-atrahasis, 88–103] [@george-gilgamesh, 1:412, 508–28]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Gen 8:4, 6–12 and 20–21, Noah's vessel comes to rest, he sends a raven and doves, and he offers a sacrifice afterward. The Babylonian narrative likewise contains a landing, a sequence of bird releases, and an offering. The birds and their order differ, and Genesis uses the sacrifice to introduce YHWH's resolve concerning the future. The combination and order of several shared details support a historical relationship among the telling of these flood traditions. The particular paths by which the traditions reached the biblical writers remain debated.

## Scholarly Issues

Smith presented his paper to the Society of Biblical Archaeology on December 3, 1872. The ensuing public interest led the London Daily Telegraph to fund his 1873 expedition to Nineveh. A fragment he found was initially understood as filling a gap in the Gilgamesh version. It is now assigned to Atrahasis. The history of that identification shows how additional discoveries helped scholars distinguish between Mesopotamian flood accounts.

The discovery also became part of public disputes over the meaning of biblical parallels. Friedrich Delitzsch's lectures on Babylon and the Bible, which began in 1902, brought those debates to a wider audience. The object itself gives evidence for the text known to Assyrian scribes. Its significance for Genesis depends on comparison across a broader set of manuscripts and stories.
