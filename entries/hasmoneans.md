## Overview

The Hasmoneans were the priestly family that led the Maccabean revolt and went on to govern Judea. Jonathan became high priest in 152 BCE, and Simon secured recognition as high priest and civil leader. Later members of the family styled themselves kings, expanded their territory, and issued coins. Pompey curtailed their independence in 63 BCE, and the last reigning Hasmonean fell to Herod in 37 BCE. The dynasty shaped the territory, religious institutions, and disagreements that form the background of the New Testament.

## Sources

First Maccabees traces the rise of Mattathias’s family and ends as John Hyrcanus takes power. It favors the house of Simon, whose claims it presents through speeches, letters, and a public decree. Josephus provides a more extended narrative in *Jewish Antiquities* 12–14. He uses 1 Maccabees in part of his account, so overlap between them does not amount to independent confirmation. The familiar dynastic name comes from Josephus’s genealogy and later Jewish usage.

Coins preserve titles and names of rulers in their own time. Issues of Yehoḥanan, usually attributed to John Hyrcanus I, name the high priest and the council of the Jews, a body whose precise role is debated. Bilingual issues of Alexander Jannaeus name Alexander in Greek and King Yehonatan in Hebrew. The question of which ruler first began Hasmonean [[coinage|coinage]] depends partly on identifying otherwise ambiguous names.

## History

The [[maccabean-revolt|Maccabean revolt]] began under Mattathias and continued under his sons. Rival claims to the Seleucid throne gave Jonathan opportunities to negotiate, including the high priesthood granted by Alexander Balas. Simon succeeded him, and John Hyrcanus I took power after Simon’s murder. According to Josephus, Aristobulus I was the first of the family to take the royal title. Alexander Jannaeus expanded the kingdom further. Salome Alexandra subsequently ruled while Hyrcanus II held the high priesthood. The rivalry between Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II gave Pompey an opening in 63 BCE. Antigonus briefly restored Hasmonean rule in 40 BCE with Parthian help, before [[herod-the-great|Herod]] captured Jerusalem in 37 BCE.

John Hyrcanus brought Idumea under his control. Josephus describes acceptance of circumcision and Judean laws as conditions for remaining, but historians debate how communities experienced and understood incorporation. Later expansion included parts of the north and helped shape the landscape of Jewish Galilee. Josephus credits Hyrcanus with destroying the sanctuary on Mount Gerizim. Excavated coins put the destruction around 110 BCE, later than a straightforward reading of his narrative sequence.

Members of the family combined priestly and political power, drawing opposition from groups with competing accounts of legitimate authority. Qumran texts accuse a figure called the Wicked Priest, often identified with Jonathan or Simon, although no identification is certain. The Lion of Wrath in the Nahum pesher is commonly associated with Jannaeus and the mass crucifixion reported by Josephus (*Ant.* 13.380). [[psalms-of-solomon|Psalms of Solomon]] 17 likewise condemns rulers who usurped the throne associated with David, a complaint commonly read against Hasmonean rule.

## In the Bible

First Maccabees 2:1–5 introduces Mattathias of the priestly division of Joarib and names his five sons. The account of Judas’s embassy in 8:17–32 portrays Rome as a source of diplomatic recognition before the family held lasting power. Alexander Balas’s letter and Jonathan’s assumption of priestly dress in 10:18–21 place his appointment in the setting of Seleucid rivalry. The dates used in documents in 13:41–42 signal a claim to political autonomy under Simon. The decree in 14:27–47 recognizes Simon as ruler and high priest pending the appearance of a trustworthy prophet. Simon’s murder at Dok and the survival of John Hyrcanus in 16:11–22 set up a dynastic succession while revealing how fragile it could be.

Some readers take the limited aid promised in Dan 11:34 as a guarded reference to Maccabean resistance. The verse gives no name, and the interpretation remains disputed. In John 4:20 the Samaritan woman invokes ancestral worship on the mountain as a claim to sacred authority. The destruction of the Gerizim sanctuary gives historical depth to the exchange. It did not end Samaritan identification with the mountain.
