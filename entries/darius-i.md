## Overview

Darius I ruled the Persian Empire from 522 to 486 BCE. He took power after the death of Cambyses and suppressed revolts across several regions. Inscriptions and administrative tablets document his claim to the throne, his building projects, and the workings of imperial government. The books of Haggai, Zechariah, and Ezra date the rebuilding of Jerusalem's temple to his reign. Those texts make Darius important for understanding how a small community within the empire sought to restore its sanctuary and how later biblical writers remembered that process.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[behistun-inscription|Behistun Inscription]] gives Darius's account of his accession in 522 BCE. He claims that he removed an impostor and then overcame several rebels. The account presents Darius as the rightful king and records the geographical extent of the disturbances. Its versions in multiple languages show how the Persian court presented this claim to different audiences. The Aramaic version from Elephantine shows that the account circulated beyond Iran. [@tad]

Darius built at [[susa|Susa]] and began the royal complex at [[persepolis|Persepolis]]. Under his rule the [[persia|empire]] administered extensive territories through provinces governed by appointed officials called satraps. Coinage and roads supported some of its commerce and communications. The [[persepolis-fortification-tablets|Fortification tablets]] preserve records of rations and travel in the Persian heartland. Such administrative documents give a detailed regional view of practices that royal monuments describe more broadly. [@abd]

## Biblical Portrayal

Hag 1:1, 15 and 2:10 date the prophet's activity to Darius's second year. Zech 1:1, 7 and 7:1 also dates oracles by his reign. The earliest of these notices falls in 520 BCE, shortly after the accession crisis. Their year formulas help place the restoration of the temple within the history of Persian rule, though possible allusions to the wider revolts are debated.

Ezra 5:1–6:15 relates that Tattenai, an imperial governor, questioned the authorization for the temple project. A search located a memorandum attributed to Cyrus, and Darius issued instructions that the work receive support. A cuneiform document naming a governor usually identified as Tattenai offers external evidence for the official. The details of the building dispute and decree are known from Ezra. [@abd]

## Historical Assessment

Ezra 4:6–23 recounts complaints under Xerxes and Artaxerxes, then the narrative returns in Ezra 4:24 to Darius's second year. On the usual reading, the intervening material groups later instances of opposition with earlier ones before returning to the temple project. Ezra 6:15 puts completion in the month of Adar of Darius's sixth year, which yields the conventional date of 515 BCE. Diana Edelman and other scholars have questioned aspects of the biblical chronology. Edelman places rebuilding under Artaxerxes I, a minority reconstruction that depends on her assessment of the literary and genealogical evidence.

Darius II, attested in later Elephantine documents, was a different king. The identification of the Darius mentioned in Neh 12:22 is debated. The [[darius-the-mede|Darius the Mede]] of Daniel's court stories also belongs to a different narrative setting. A proposed echo of Darius I's provincial administration in Dan 6:1 (MT 6:2, using the Hebrew numbering) does not identify the ruler in that story with him. [@tad] [@abd]
