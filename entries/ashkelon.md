## Overview

Ashkelon was a fortified city on the Mediterranean coast of the southern Levant. It was a Canaanite port in the Bronze Age and one of the principal Philistine cities in the Iron Age. Excavations have exposed its Middle Bronze Age gate, early Philistine buildings and pottery, a cemetery, and the remains of the city destroyed by Nebuchadnezzar II in 604 BCE. Egyptian, Assyrian, and Babylonian sources give independent accounts of its encounters with neighboring powers. The finds help explain why the Bible remembers Ashkelon as both a Philistine stronghold and a target of judgment.

## Location and Identification

The ancient city lies along the shore at modern Ashkelon. Its coastal position gave it access to maritime trade, while overland routes connected it with communities in the southern Levant and Egypt. The Egyptian [[merneptah-stele|Merneptah Stele]], from the late thirteenth century BCE, names Ashkelon among the places and peoples over which the pharaoh claims victory [@ael]. That mention precedes the evidence for an Iron Age Philistine city.

## Archaeological History

The Leon Levy Expedition, led by Lawrence Stager and later Daniel Master, excavated at the site between 1985 and 2016. The excavators exposed a huge earthen rampart and a mudbrick gateway with an arch dating to the Middle Bronze Age. Early Iron Age houses, painted pottery in Aegean-related styles, and animal remains that include evidence for pig consumption document changes in domestic life. Pottery and food remains can help trace contacts and differences in practice, but they cannot assign an ethnic identity to each household [@neaehl].

An Iron Age cemetery outside the walls yielded evidence for burial in pits, among other practices. In 2019, Michal Feldman and colleagues reported on the ancient DNA of ten Bronze and Iron Age individuals from Ashkelon, including both cemetery burials and earlier remains from other contexts. They detected a European-related ancestry signal in the early Iron Age sample that was weaker or undetectable in the later sample. This supports some movement of people into Ashkelon around the transition, though the sample is too small to identify a precise place of origin or to determine the ancestry of all [[philistines|Philistines]].

Sennacherib's inscriptions report that the Assyrian king removed Sidqa of Ashkelon and installed a different ruler in 701 BCE [@rinap3-1]. In 604 BCE a Babylonian chronicle records a conquest generally identified as Ashkelon in the month of Kislev, when the city was plundered and its king captured. A well-documented destruction layer includes collapsed and burned buildings. The chronology joins the archaeological evidence to the campaign of [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]], though the city's name on the tablet is damaged [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles]. The site was later occupied again through the Persian period and beyond.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ashkelon is one of five towns named in 1 Sam 6:17. In Judg 14:19 it is the city where Samson obtains clothing by killing thirty men, and 2 Sam 1:20 imagines its inhabitants celebrating Israel's defeat. These accounts reflect Ashkelon's place in biblical memory of Israel's rivals on the coast.

Jer 47:5–7 depicts Ashkelon silenced and under the sword, and Zeph 2:4, 7 imagines desolation followed by use of the coast by Judah's remnant. The destruction in 604 BCE shows what a Babylonian attack meant for the city and is often read alongside Jeremiah's oracle. The oracle itself gives no date for the fall of Ashkelon in those verses. The episode illustrates why evidence for a historical event and the interpretation given it by a prophetic text call for separate judgments.
