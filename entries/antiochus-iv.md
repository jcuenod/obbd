## Overview

Antiochus IV Epiphanes ruled the Seleucid kingdom from 175 to 164 BCE. His royal territory extended across much of the eastern Mediterranean and western Asia. Biblical sources associate him with military campaigns in Egypt, the plundering of the Jerusalem temple and the suppression of Jewish observances. The temple's disruption in 167 BCE helped provoke the Maccabean revolt. Many scholars identify Antiochus with the persecuting ruler depicted in Daniel's visions, which express the crisis in the language of beasts, horns and conflict between kingdoms.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Coins issued in Antiochus's name bear royal titles including *Theos Epiphanēs* (“God Manifest”). Babylonian astronomical diaries, which sometimes record political events alongside observations of the sky, help establish the chronology of his reign and movements. Greek historians preserve further accounts of his campaigns and death. These records place the dispute in [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] within a reign that also involved war with Egypt and efforts to consolidate Seleucid power farther east. [@abd]

## Biblical Portrayal

First Maccabees tells how Antiochus plundered the Jerusalem temple and how his government curtailed practices such as circumcision and Sabbath observance (1 Macc 1:10–64). It places a new cult installation on the altar, where sacrifices are subsequently offered (1 Macc 1:54, 59). Second Maccabees associates the Jerusalem sanctuary with Olympian Zeus and recounts the deaths of people who resisted the measures (2 Macc 6:1–11). The sources reflect different interests in how the events are told. Both connect the measures with the setting of the [[maccabean-revolt|Maccabean revolt]].

The visions in Daniel present comparable events in a different form. A small horn in Dan 7:8, 24–25 persecutes God's holy ones and seeks to alter their calendar and law. In Dan 11:31, a ruler's forces stop the customary temple sacrifice and install “the abomination that makes desolate” (ESV). Scholars commonly identify the ruler with Antiochus. A longstanding proposal hears a hostile play on *Baʿal Šamêm* (“Lord of Heaven”) in the Hebrew behind this phrase, though the identification of the name and the exact form of the installation are debated. [@abd] [@ddd]

## Historical Assessment

The motives for the persecution remain disputed. One explanation emphasizes a royal initiative to impose Greek religious or civic practices. Elias Bickerman instead stressed the initiative of Jewish reformers seeking changes in Jerusalem. Victor Tcherikover interpreted the measures as a response to unrest and rebellion. The surviving narratives describe the resulting conflict more fully than they explain how the decisions were made.

Many scholars date the visions of Daniel to the crisis of 167–164 BCE, distinguishing them from the book's older court stories. Dan 11:21–39 tracks much of Antiochus's career and the temple crisis closely. Dan 11:40–45 envisages a final campaign and places the end of the ruler near the holy mountain. Antiochus actually died on his eastern expedition in 164 BCE. The difference helps explain the date often assigned to this passage, when the author could recount the earlier events and still expect a different end for the king. [@abd]
