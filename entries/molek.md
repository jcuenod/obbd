## Overview

Molek is the name conventionally read in biblical passages condemning the giving of children or making them pass through fire. Scholars disagree whether the underlying Hebrew consonants identify a deity or designate a kind of offering. West Semitic texts attest divine names built from the same consonants, and inscriptions from the Punic Mediterranean use them in sacrificial language. The biblical evidence concerns both the meaning of the term and the practices that writers were condemning. Each question has to be considered in light of the passage where the expression occurs.

## Sources and Iconography

Punic inscriptions from the central Mediterranean use the consonants *mlk* in dedications, often in settings associated with urns containing cremated remains. Otto Eissfeldt interpreted *mlk* as a term for a sacrifice and used it to explain the biblical expression as giving a child as a *molk* offering. Differences in date, region, and wording matter when comparing this material with Judah.

George Heider and John Day defended the interpretation of Molek as a deity. Among the evidence they consider are divine figures named Malik or *mlk* in Akkadian and Ugaritic texts, and the associations of such figures with the dead in some traditions. The consonants also belong to a widespread Semitic root for kingship. In the absence of a securely identified image, depictions of a hollow bull-headed furnace belong to the history of interpretation. Medieval commentary, including Rashi's comments on Jer 7:31, and later art helped shape such pictures.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Lev 18:21 forbids giving offspring to Molek. Lev 20:2–5 assigns a severe penalty and describes the offense as going after Molek, wording comparable to expressions for worshiping other gods. In 2 Kgs 23:10 Josiah defiles [[tophet|Topheth]] in the Valley of Hinnom so that nobody can make a child pass through fire to Molek. Jer 32:35 connects the practice with high places of Baal in the valley. Jer 7:31 explicitly condemns the burning of sons and daughters there. In these passages the writers treat the practices as incompatible with worship of Yahweh.

The traditional Hebrew text of 1 Kgs 11:7 applies Molek to Ammon. Many scholars suspect a textual confusion with [[milkom|Milkom]], who is associated with Ammon elsewhere in Kings. Isa 30:33 describes a burning place prepared for the king, while Isa 57:9 pictures a visit to the king with oil and perfumes. Both passages have sometimes been interpreted in relation to Molek. Acts 7:43 follows the Greek form of Amos 5:26 in referring to the tent of Moloch. The Hebrew and Greek forms of Amos differ considerably and have their own textual history.

## Scholarly Issues

The traditional vowels *mōlek* may reflect a disparaging modification of a form related to *melek*, “king.” This explanation is a proposal about how the written consonants were vocalized. The deity interpretation has to account for the Punic use of the consonants and the offering interpretation for biblical expressions like the one in Lev 20:5. “Passing through fire” has been understood as immolation in some settings and as a rite of dedication in others. The passages that explicitly describe burning give direct textual evidence for [[child-sacrifice|child sacrifice]], while the circumstances and prevalence of the practice remain debated.
