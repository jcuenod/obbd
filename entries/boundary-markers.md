## Overview

Boundary markers indicated where one person's land ended and another's began. In Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Levant, texts from the second and first millennia BCE show how seriously people took interference with land limits. Egyptian wisdom teaching warns against shifting markers, Assyrian laws penalize encroachment, and Babylonian royal monuments protect grants with curses. Biblical law and wisdom similarly condemn moving a neighbor's marker. They also explain why the practice mattered to a household's inherited livelihood and to people who might lack the power to defend their fields.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Egyptian [[instruction-of-amenemope|Instruction of Amenemope]] warns against moving markers at the edges of fields and against encroaching on the land of a widow. By placing the two warnings together, it treats the protection of a boundary as a question of conduct toward vulnerable people. The passage is an instruction to a pupil, so it expresses a standard of behavior without laying down a judicial penalty [@ael, 2:157–158].

The Middle Assyrian Laws address encroachment in a different form. An offender who incorporates part of a neighbor's land into his own can be required to surrender more land than he took and face additional punishment. The text survives on the tablet commonly called Tablet B. Hittite laws also deal with land use and offenses against neighboring property, which illustrates a wider legal concern even where a provision does not concern physically moving a stone [@roth-law, 176].

Babylonian [[kudurru|land-grant monuments]] preserved royal grants or other privileges, often beneath divine symbols. Many invoke punishment against anyone who alters the record or damages the monument. Such stones document ownership on a different scale from ordinary field markers [@roth-law, 176] [@anep, figs. 454, 518–521]. Later Greek boundary stones marked limits or called attention to legal claims, and Roman worship of Terminus gave religious recognition to boundaries.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 19:14 prohibits shifting a neighbor's marker established by earlier generations. The setting is the family's inheritance in the land. Deut 27:17 includes the practice among the offenses publicly placed under a curse. The rule assumes that moving the physical sign could undermine a claim that depended in part on remembered limits.

Prov 22:28 likewise prohibits removing an old marker. Prov 23:10–11 adds the fields of the fatherless and appeals to their powerful defender. The collection's relationship to Amenemope is widely accepted. The parallel in the second saying is particularly close because it combines the boundary rule with concern for a vulnerable family. The biblical and Egyptian texts identify different people as needing protection and develop the grounds for that protection in their own ways.

## Interpretive Significance

Job 24:2 associates moving markers with other acts of taking property by force. Hos 5:10 compares Judah's leaders to people who move a boundary, drawing on the familiar offense to characterize their conduct. The comparison depends on the wrong being recognizable to the audience. Across these texts, the marker matters because it gives public expression to a right.
