## Overview

In first-millennium BCE Mesopotamia, people used plaques and pendants to protect against Lamashtu, a female demon feared for attacks on pregnant women and infants. Some plaques depict Lamashtu herself alongside Pazuzu, a dangerous wind demon whose image was used to ward her off. Small pendants could show Pazuzu's head alone. These objects provide a material comparison for the charms mentioned in Isaiah and a cultural setting for biblical poetry that pictures disease and other threats as active forces.

## Archaeological Evidence

Bronze and stone objects preserve several ways of depicting protection. On a bronze plaque in the Louvre, Pazuzu's head projects above the upper edge, and his body appears on the back. On the front, Lamashtu appears in the lowest register, or horizontal band, above a donkey in a boat. The scene is generally read as her removal from the person in danger, not as a portrayal of her being worshiped. [@ddd, 236–237] [@anep, figs. 657–661]

Mesopotamian ritual texts describe Lamashtu as a threat around pregnancy, birth, and early infancy. They also help explain why a threatening face could appear on [[amulets|amulets, or objects used for protection]]. Pazuzu was himself associated with harmful winds, but in this setting his image was used against Lamashtu. The objects illustrate how one of the [[demons|dangerous beings]] could be enlisted to repel another rather than falling into a fixed category of either good or evil. [@ddd, 236–237, 520–521]

The plaques associated with Arslan Tash in northern Syria offer a close Levantine comparison if authentic. Commonly assigned to the seventh century BCE, they combine images with inscriptions, one of which is usually read as barring harmful beings from a household. That reading places the plaque alongside practices intended to protect [[apotropaic-doorposts-and-thresholds|doors and thresholds]]. Scholars have both defended and questioned the plaques' authenticity; neither comes from a controlled excavation, so they cannot by themselves establish how widespread such practices were. [@kai, no. 27] [@ddd, 520–521, 725–726]

## In the Bible

Ps 91:1–6 contrasts protection under God with threats that come by day or night. The Hebrew terms *deber* ("pestilence") and *qeṭeb* ("destruction") are often read against the background of ancient ideas about demons, in part because the poem depicts the dangers as moving and acting. The wording can also be read as personification, the description of an impersonal danger as if it were a person. The psalm is a poem of trust, not an incantation, or ritual formula, addressed to the threatening beings. Ps 121:6 similarly promises protection by day and night but does not identify the sun or moon with a demon. [@ddd, 231–232, 673–674] [@keel-symbolism, 78–85]

Isa 34:14 places [[lilith|Lilith]] among the inhabitants of a desolate landscape. The Hebrew name is commonly compared with Mesopotamian *lilītu*, a term for a female spirit, not a name for Lamashtu. In later Jewish tradition Lilith could threaten infants, a role comparable to Lamashtu's. That similarity does not show that the two figures were identified in the biblical period. [@ddd, 520–521]

Isa 3:20 includes objects commonly understood as charms among items worn or carried by Jerusalem's women. The list does not name the beings, if any, associated with the charms or describe the way the objects were used. The [[ketef-hinnom-amulets|silver scrolls from Ketef Hinnom]] offer a separate example of inscribed protective objects in Judah, not evidence for the use of images of Pazuzu or Lamashtu there. [@cos2, 2.83]
