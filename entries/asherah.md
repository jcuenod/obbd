## Overview

Asherah is the biblical name usually connected with Athirat, a goddess attested in Late Bronze Age texts from Ugarit on the Syrian coast. In Iron Age Israel and Judah, biblical texts also use *ăšērâ* for a sacred object that could stand near an altar. Hebrew inscriptions bring the two senses into contact by invoking Israel's God, YHWH, and an asherah associated with him. The texts and archaeological finds help explain the worship practices criticized in the Bible, but scholars disagree about when the term denotes a goddess, a sacred object, or the goddess's presence through an object.

## Sources and Iconography

The late second-millennium BCE texts from Ugarit call Athirat the Lady Athirat of the Sea and describe her as the mother or progenitress of the gods. She is associated with [[el|El]], the senior god, and is understood as his consort. The stories refer to her seventy sons and show her taking part in negotiations over divine rule. In the story of Baal's palace, she speaks with El on Baal's behalf [@parker-unp, 83, 125–129, 134] [@ktu3, 1.4 I–IV].

Two Iron Age finds are central to the discussion about YHWH and Asherah. The [[kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions]] give blessings that invoke regional designations of YHWH and the expression usually rendered “his asherah.” A damaged tomb inscription from [[khirbet-el-qom-inscription|Khirbet el-Qom]] associates a blessing or protection with a similar expression, although details of the syntax are disputed [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 290, 293, 409]. The texts themselves establish that the expression could be used in blessings. They leave its referent open to interpretation.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 16:21 forbids planting an asherah by YHWH's altar. In Judg 6:25–30, Gideon cuts down the asherah beside Baal's altar and uses its wood for an offering. Such descriptions suggest a wooden object or tree and indicate possible variation in its form. A first-millennium biblical pairing with [[baal|Baal]] belongs to the presentation of worship in Israel. At Ugarit the goddess's principal divine partner is El.

The authors of Kings present the presence of an asherah or objects associated with it as a charge against kings. An asherah stands in Samaria in 2 Kgs 13:6, and Manasseh sets up a carved image in the Jerusalem temple in 2 Kgs 21:7. In 2 Kgs 23:4–7 Josiah removes equipment from the temple, burns the asherah, and ends activities associated with it. Some scholars regard the reference to Asherah's prophets in 1 Kgs 18:19 as a later addition because they play no clear role in the ensuing contest. The proposed reading of Hos 14:8 (MT 14:9) as referring to Anat and Asherah depends on changing the transmitted Hebrew and remains disputed [@ddd, 100–106].

## Scholarly Issues

The suffix translated “his” is unusual on the personal name of a deity. Some scholars therefore read the blessing formulas as naming a sacred object used in worship of YHWH. Others interpret the word as the goddess or as a representation through which her presence could be understood. The evidence from Samaria and Jerusalem has led scholars such as Saul Olyan and Judith Hadley to consider how Asherah or her symbol could have had a place in worship supported by royal institutions, with differences among periods and places.

The [[judean-pillar-figurines|female clay figurines from Judah]] and the decorated [[taanach-cult-stand|Taanach stand]] have been interpreted as images of Asherah or her attributes. Neither set of objects carries an inscription identifying the goddess. Their figures and tree imagery can therefore inform the discussion without providing secure identifications [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 154–160, 329–336].
