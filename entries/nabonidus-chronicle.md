## Overview

The Nabonidus Chronicle is a Babylonian cuneiform tablet that records events in the reign of the last Neo-Babylonian king. Its entries describe the years when Nabonidus stayed in Tayma, interruptions to Babylon's New Year festival, and the military campaign that brought Cyrus into Babylon in 539 BCE. The tablet supplies a chronological account of the transfer of power and a comparison with biblical writing about the same period. The broken text leaves several events and names open to interpretation.

## Description and Provenance

The chronicle survives on a damaged clay tablet in the British Museum (BM 35382) written in Babylonian cuneiform. Modern editors commonly designate it Chronicle 7. It follows the regnal years of [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]], using the concise style of the [[babylonian-chronicles|Babylonian chronicles]]. The accession and final years contain gaps, as do parts of the intervening entries. Damage and the lack of a controlled excavated context limit what can be established about the tablet's copying and original use.

## Contents

A recurring entry places Nabonidus in Tayma while the crown prince, officials, and army remain in Babylonia. The prince is called *mār šarri*, the king's son, and is unnamed. The text also reports interruptions of the [[akitu-festival|New Year festival]], including that Nabû did not come to Babylon and that Bel did not go out. It distinguishes those missed ceremonies from offerings that continued.

The final campaign begins with fighting at Opis. The chronicle then describes the capture of Sippar without battle. Ugbaru, identified as governor of Gutium, and the Persian army enter Babylon on 16 Tashritu, without fighting in the city. [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] subsequently enters on 3 Arahsamnu, in late October 539 BCE on a commonly used conversion. The tablet reports arrangements for government and worship following the capture.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Dan 5:1 portrays Belshazzar as king. The chronicle's crown prince is commonly identified as [[belshazzar|Belshazzar]] through other sources. His position in Babylonia during Nabonidus's absence provides a historical setting for traditions about him, while the chronicle maintains the distinction between his status and the king's. Dan 5:30–31 (MT 5:30–6:1) associates Belshazzar's death with the accession of Darius the Mede. The chronicle records Persian forces taking the city and Cyrus arriving later.

Isa 45:1–2 portrays God opening gates and clearing a path for Cyrus. The peaceful entry into the city of Babylon can be compared with that imagery, while the fighting at Opis shows that the campaign also involved violence. In Isa 46:1–2, Bel and Nebo are pictured being carried as burdens. The chronicle describes cult statues being moved into Babylon before the conquest and returned afterward. The transport of images offers regional background for Isaiah's portrayal of Babylonian gods unable to save themselves.

## Scholarly Issues

The text uses forms of the names Ugbaru and Gubaru in accounts of the entry and subsequent administration. Scholars debate their identification. Both have figured in attempts to identify [[darius-the-mede|Darius the Mede]], a name and title unrecorded in the chronicle. A damaged place name in an earlier account of Cyrus's campaign has sometimes been restored as Lydia. Reading it as Lydia or as another region depends on damaged signs and historical arguments.
