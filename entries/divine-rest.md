## Overview

Mesopotamian creation stories depict gods settling into sanctuaries after labor or conflict, and they sometimes explain catastrophes as the result of disturbed divine sleep. Biblical texts use related imagery in several ways. Genesis marks the seventh day by God's cessation from creation. The Psalms call Zion God's resting place, and biblical accounts of temple building connect the project with Israel's security from enemies. The background helps distinguish rest as the end of work, rest as dwelling in a sanctuary, and sleep as a figure for apparent inaction.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In Tablet I of [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]], the noise of the younger gods keeps Apsu from resting. He plans to destroy them, and Ea defeats him. After Marduk's victory over Tiamat, Tablet VI describes the gods building his temple in Babylon, Esagil, where they can find lodging. The sanctuary gives their assembly a fixed location and marks the stability of Marduk's rule [@lambert-bcm, 45–60].

In [[atrahasis|Atrahasis]], growing human numbers make such a din that Enlil loses sleep. Enlil calls for measures to reduce the population, culminating in the flood. This story and Enuma Elish turn disturbed rest into a reason for divine intervention. The founding of Esagil in Enuma Elish gives rest another meaning by associating it with an established divine household [@lambert-millard-atrahasis, 66–87].

## In the Hebrew Bible

In Gen 2:2–3, God ceases (*šābat*) from work and blesses and sanctifies the seventh day. The passage uses the verb “cease,” related to the name of the [[sabbath|Sabbath]], for the transition from making the world to blessing the day. The commandment in Exod 20:11 uses a different verb, *nûaḥ* (“rest” or “settle”), for the divine example. Exod 31:17 says that God ceased and was refreshed, using both *šābat* and a verb for recovering one's breath or vigor. The wording gives human hearers a way to understand the rhythm of work and rest.

Temple texts use a noun from the root of *nûaḥ* for a place where YHWH or his sacred objects come to rest. In Ps 132:8, 13–14 the people call on YHWH to come to his resting place with the [[ark-of-the-covenant|ark]], and YHWH names Zion as that place. In 1 Chr 28:2 David calls the temple a house of rest for the ark and refers to God's [[footstool]]. Isa 66:1 uses that royal image on a cosmic scale, calling heaven God's throne and earth his footstool and asking what house or resting place people could provide.

In 2 Sam 7:1–2, 11, David raises the matter of a temple after receiving rest from enemies, and the divine response includes a promise of rest. In 1 Kgs 5:4 (MT 5:18), Solomon's freedom from threats provides the occasion for building. Here rest names the security of the kingdom, a condition that makes the sanctuary possible. The distinction matters when comparing biblical [[temple-building-accounts|temple building accounts]] with the settlement of Marduk's court.

## Interpretive Significance

John Walton reads the seven days in Genesis as the installation of an ordered world in which God takes up rule, drawing on ancient representations of the [[temple-as-cosmos|temple as a cosmos]]. The link between divine rest and temple occupancy is well attested in Mesopotamia. Whether Genesis 1–2 itself presents a temple inauguration remains debated. In Heb 4:1–11, the argument draws on God's seventh-day rest and on the promise of entering God's rest, extending the language of creation to the community's hope.

Sleep imagery has its own range. Ps 44:23 (MT 44:24) asks God to wake up, expressing the experience of abandonment. Ps 121:4 assures worshippers that Israel's keeper stays awake. In 1 Kgs 18:27 Elijah mocks Baal's silence by suggesting that he may be asleep. These uses show why a comparison with troubled divine sleep in Mesopotamian stories cannot settle whether a biblical prayer refers to a specific foreign tradition.
