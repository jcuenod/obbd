## Overview

Jehu was an Israelite commander who seized the throne in the ninth century BCE and ended the dynasty founded by Omri. Accounts in Kings connect his accession to a prophetic commission and killings at Jezreel. An Assyrian monument records his tribute to Shalmaneser III in 841 BCE, and a fragmentary Aramaic monument gives a different account of who killed the kings opposed by Jehu. These records place his coup within a regional contest in which Israel, Judah and Damascus faced pressure from one another and from Assyria.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[black-obelisk|Black Obelisk]] of Shalmaneser III records Jehu’s tribute and depicts a prostrate man before the Assyrian ruler. The scene may represent Jehu himself or an envoy acting for him. The caption identifies the tribute with Jehu and uses the Assyrian expression that calls him a son of Omri. Assyrian scribes continued to identify Israel with [[omri|Omri]] even after his dynasty fell. The caption gives political geography in a conventional form and does not establish kinship. [@rima3] [@anet, 280–281]

The [[tel-dan-stele|Tel Dan Stele]], commonly attributed to [[hazael|Hazael]] of Damascus, claims the deaths of an Israelite king and a king of the House of David. The names are damaged and are commonly reconstructed as Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah. On that reading, the Aramean king claims the same deaths attributed to Jehu in Kings. The damaged inscription and the victory rhetoric of a royal monument are important when assessing the apparent conflict. [@kai] [@cos2]

## Biblical Portrayal

1 Kgs 19:16–17 gives Elijah a commission involving Jehu. In 2 Kgs 9:1–10:36 the prophetic action proceeds through Elisha and his emissary and leads to Jehu’s revolt. Kings identifies Jehu as the son of Jehoshaphat and grandson of Nimshi (2 Kgs 9:2, 14). The coup kills Joram and Ahaziah and brings down the household of Ahab. It also ends royal sponsorship of Baal in the northern kingdom as portrayed by Kings. [@abd]

2 Kgs 10:30 praises what Jehu did to Ahab’s house and promises that his descendants will sit on the throne. Hos 1:4 invokes bloodshed at [[jezreel|Jezreel]] as grounds for judgment on Jehu’s dynasty. The two texts preserve a tension in the evaluation of the events. Kings also relates territorial losses to Hazael in Jehu’s reign (2 Kgs 10:32–33), although it leaves the tribute to Assyria unmentioned. [@abd]

## Historical Assessment

In 841 BCE [[shalmaneser-iii|Shalmaneser III]] campaigned against Hazael, who resisted him, and recorded Jehu’s submission. Jehu may have sought to reduce pressure from Assyria or improve his position against Damascus, but the sources do not state his motives. Proposals about the kings’ deaths include Aramean involvement in the coup, a claim of credit for its outcome by Hazael and conflicting independent traditions about responsibility. Arguments for each proposal depend partly on the reconstruction of the stele. The timing of Jehu’s tribute is better attested than his relationship with Hazael in the coup. [@rima3] [@cos2] [@abd]
