## Overview

Milkom, also written Milcom, was a god associated with the kingdom of [[ammonites|Ammon]]. The books of Kings identify him as an Ammonite deity, and his name appears in Ammonite inscriptions and personal names. Other biblical passages use the same consonants as a word meaning “their king,” making it possible to read them as a reference to a human ruler or to the god. These passages illustrate how ancient spellings, later vocalizations, and translations can affect the evidence available for reconstructing a deity's worship.

## Sources and Iconography

The fragmentary Amman Citadel Inscription names Milkom in a context involving building and protection. One interpretation understands it as an oracle in which the deity addresses the king. The text is damaged, so its full setting and the building involved are uncertain. Its findspot at [[rabbah|Rabbah]], the Ammonite capital, gives the reference particular importance.

A seal impression excavated at Tell el-ʿUmeiri identifies its owner as Milkomʾur, a servant of Baʿalyashaʿ. The first element of the owner's name invokes Milkom. The individual he served is generally identified with Baalis, king of the Ammonites in Jer 40:14. Personal names of this kind are valuable evidence of continuing use of a divine name. They give less information about the god's specific cult.

Ammonite stone sculptures include heads wearing tall crowns, some with forms related to Egyptian crown imagery. Proposals to understand such figures as deities or kings turn on the treatment of headdress and other attributes. Identifying a particular example as Milkom remains uncertain in the absence of a secure label or distinctive attribute.

## In the Hebrew Bible

1 Kgs 11:5, 33 names Milkom among the deities whose worship Solomon adopts or tolerates. 2 Kgs 23:13 attributes the desecration of a high place associated with Milkom and the other foreign gods to Josiah. The notices connect the Ammonite god with the book's treatment of royal responsibility for worship in Judah. In 1 Kgs 11:7 the traditional Hebrew text names [[molek|Molek]] in connection with Ammon. Many textual critics regard this as a confusion in a passage that elsewhere names Milkom.

In Jer 49:1, 3, the figure whose people occupy Gad is then threatened with exile along with his priests and officers. The traditional vowels give “their king,” while the Greek version and many modern translations understand the name Milkom. Zeph 1:5 uses the same Hebrew consonants in a passage about people swearing by the figure in question as well as by Yahweh. Here too the divine name is a possible reading. Amos 1:15 describes the king and his officers going into exile and has sometimes been read in light of the similar oracle in Jeremiah.

## Scholarly Issues

2 Sam 12:30 reports that David took a very heavy crown from *mlkm* after capturing Rabbah. Reading “their king” makes the crown a royal object, while reading Milkom makes it a crown taken from the god or his image. Scholars consider the weight and the context when deciding between these interpretations. Other passages offer different clues, such as priests in Jer 49:3 or the religious setting of Zeph 1:5. Clear references in Kings and Ammonite inscriptions provide the most direct evidence for the god's identity.
