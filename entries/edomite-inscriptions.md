## Overview

Edomite inscriptions from the Iron Age survive mainly on seals, impressions, and broken pieces of pottery. Finds from southern Transjordan, the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, and the eastern Negev name officials, record communications, and attest the god Qos. A seal impression from Umm el-Biyara identifies an Edomite king also named in Assyrian records. These small texts give independent evidence for Edomite government and religious life and help place the biblical accounts of interaction with Edom in a region where political, commercial, and cultural boundaries shifted.

## Description and Provenance

The corpus includes brief inscriptions on seals, stamped jar marks, and [[ostraca|ostraca]], pieces of pottery reused for writing. It is concentrated in the late Iron Age. Some examples use forms that can be distinguished from contemporary Hebrew, with features associated with Aramaic cursive writing. Other examples are short or damaged, and attribution can depend on the letter forms, language, names, and place of recovery taken together [@ahituv-echoes] [@avigad-sass-wsss].

## Contents

At Umm el-Biyara in the region of [[petra-and-sela|Petra]], a clay seal impression preserves part of a name and title restored as Qos-gabar, king of Edom. The restoration is supported by the records of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal, which name a king of Edom as Qaus-gabri. Impressions from Tell el-Kheleifeh near the Gulf of Aqaba name Qosʿanal as a servant of the king. Finds from [[bozrah|Buseirah]] include ostraca and seals, and inscribed objects also came from the shrine at Ḥorvat Qitmit in the eastern Negev [@rinap4] [@avigad-sass-wsss].

A letter from Ḥorvat ʿUza, generally dated around 600 BCE, blesses its recipient by [[qos|Qos]]. This is important because the divine name appears independently in the blessing. It occurs more often as part of personal names, as in Qos-gabar and Qosʿanal. The letter provides evidence of someone using an Edomite writing tradition and invoking Qos at a site that also yielded Hebrew letters [@ahituv-echoes].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In 2 Kgs 16:6 the written Hebrew text has Arameans settling at Elath, while the traditional marginal reading has Edomites. The inscriptions at Tell el-Kheleifeh attest Edomite activity in the region. Their relationship to the textual variant depends in part on the debated identification of Tell el-Kheleifeh with biblical [[ezion-geber|Ezion-geber]] or Elath. They also leave open whether they belong to the specific event described in Kings.

2 Chr 28:17 reports Edomite raids in the reign of Ahaz. Jer 49:7–22 names Teman and Bozrah in an oracle, while Ezek 35:10 and Obad 10–14 portray Edom as laying claim to land or exploiting Judah's trouble. The nearby [[arad-ostraca|Arad ostracon 24]] warns of an Edomite threat. The inscriptions from the Negev give independent evidence of contact across the southern frontier, and the letter and shrine are also compatible with trade, religious activity, and mixed settlement.

## Scholarly Issues

Ezra 2:53 lists the descendants of Barkos, a name usually explained as “son of Qos.” Later Idumean ostraca, mostly in Aramaic, preserve many names containing Qos. A large portion of this later material lacks documented excavation contexts. The names illustrate continuity in the use of Qos, and the later ostraca belong to a different period and language from the Iron Age Edomite corpus.
