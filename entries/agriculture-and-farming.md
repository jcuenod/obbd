## Overview

In Iron Age Israel and Judah, most cultivation depended on rainfall concentrated in the cooler months. Farmers timed ploughing and sowing to the autumn rains and harvested barley before wheat in spring and early summer. Grapes and olives ripened later, providing wine and oil alongside the grain used for bread. The Gezer Calendar, a short inscription from the southern Levant, records a year of tasks familiar to farming households. This rhythm helps explain why biblical stories mark time by the harvest, why laws reserve part of the yield for vulnerable people, and why prophets speak of rain and crops as divine gifts.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The [[gezer-calendar]], commonly dated to the tenth century BCE from the shape of its letters, names blocks of time by the work done in them. In a common interpretation, it lists ingathering, sowing, late sowing, flax work, barley harvest, a further harvest with measuring, pruning, and summer fruit. The readings of individual tasks remain disputed. Proposals for its purpose include a school exercise, a seasonal song, and a practical record of work. Its brief text gives little indication of how it was used. [@ahituv-echoes] [@anet]

In much of the highlands, autumn rain softened the ground for ploughing and helped seed germinate. Spring rains could sustain cereal crops as they matured. Households could spread risk by growing several crops, using plots with different soils or exposures, and storing grain or processing the harvest for later use. On slopes, stone retaining walls helped hold soil and limit runoff, making some ground easier to cultivate. The dates of individual terraces are often difficult to establish because the walls were repaired and reused. [@king-stager-life] [@mazar-archaeology]

Animal-drawn ploughs opened the ground, sickles cut grain, and [[threshing-floors|threshing floors]] provided hard surfaces on which to separate the grain from stalks. Wheat and barley supplied much of the grain. Vineyards and olive trees complemented annual crops, with installations for making wine and oil described under [[winepresses]] and [[olive-oil-production|olive oil production]]. Deut 8:7–9 places cereals, vines, and olives within a wider picture of abundance that also includes figs and pomegranates.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 28:23–29 uses the skill of a farmer as an illustration. The passage moves from preparing the soil and setting out different seeds to treating each crop with the appropriate threshing tool. In Ruth 1:22, Naomi and Ruth arrive at Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest. Ruth continues gleaning, or gathering grain left by the reapers, through the wheat harvest (Ruth 2:23). Lev 19:9–10 sets aside field edges and what remains after harvesting for poor people and resident foreigners.

The recurring sequence of farm work also shaped public worship and law. Exod 23:14–17 connects the three annual feasts with Unleavened Bread, the first produce of the harvest, and ingathering. The [[festivals-agricultural|agricultural festivals]] gave seasonal work a place in the worship calendar. The law of the [[sabbatical-year|sabbatical year]] extends the pattern of rest to the land itself by forbidding sowing and pruning in the seventh year while allowing people and animals to eat what the land produces (Lev 25:1–7).

## Interpretive Significance

Deut 11:10–17 describes the land as dependent on rain from heaven and uses that dependence to explain the consequences of Israel's choices. Jer 5:24 similarly pairs the seasonal rains with the appointed weeks of harvest. In Hos 2:8–9 (MT 2:10–11, using the Hebrew verse numbering), YHWH claims to have supplied the grain, new wine, and oil. The passage mentions offerings to [[baal|Baal]], the storm god, making the source of agricultural plenty part of its dispute over worship.
