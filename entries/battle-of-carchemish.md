## Overview

In 605 BCE the army of Babylonia defeated an Egyptian force at Carchemish, a city on the Euphrates at the present border of Türkiye and Syria. Nebuchadnezzar, then crown prince, led the Babylonian army. The victory broke Egyptian control over much of Syria and the Levant and opened the way for Babylonian rule there. A Babylonian chronicle records the battle and pursuit of the defeated army. The same defeat provides the setting of a poem in Jeremiah and helps explain why Judah came under Babylonian domination soon afterward.

## Sources

The [[nebuchadnezzar-chronicle|Nebuchadnezzar Chronicle]] records the battle under the final year of Nabopolassar, Nebuchadnezzar's father. It says the Babylonian army crossed the river, broke the Egyptian army at Carchemish, and pursued survivors as far as the district of Hamath. The claim of complete victory is the chronicle's account of the battle. Its record of the sequence is particularly valuable because it also reports the death of Nabopolassar and the crown prince's return to Babylon to take the throne [@abc] [@glassner-chronicles].

British excavations led by Leonard Woolley and later Turkish-Italian investigations uncovered Iron Age remains at [[carchemish|Carchemish]]. Egyptian objects attest contact with Egypt, and excavators associate deposits such as the filling of a well in a former Assyrian administrative complex with the Babylonian takeover around 605 BCE. Such evidence helps place the transition in the history of the city. The Babylonian chronicle provides the account of the battle itself.

## Reconstruction

During the collapse of Assyrian power, [[necho-ii|Necho II]] sent Egyptian forces north to support the remaining Assyrian army. This is the context of his march in 609 BCE and his encounter with [[josiah|Josiah]] of Judah at Megiddo. By 605 BCE Egyptian forces were at Carchemish. The city's location on the Euphrates made it an important point of access between Mesopotamia and Syria.

The victory allowed [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar]] to extend Babylonian authority into regions where Egypt had exercised control. The immediate aftermath included his return to Babylon upon his father's death. The western campaigns continued after his accession. Egypt remained an independent power and later resisted Babylonian expansion, although the defeat changed who could exact tribute and impose demands on kingdoms such as Judah.

## Biblical Accounts

Jer 46:2–12 locates the defeat of Necho's army at Carchemish in Jehoiakim's fourth year. The poem presents Egypt as a rising river and then describes its soldiers in flight. The historical setting is the same battle that the chronicle reports. Kings summarizes its larger consequence by saying that Babylon took territory previously controlled by Egypt (2 Kgs 24:7).

The accounts of Josiah's clash with Necho concern the earlier Egyptian campaign of 609 BCE (2 Kgs 23:29; 2 Chr 35:20–24). Chronicles names Carchemish as Necho's objective. The Babylonian chronicle for that earlier year instead reports Egyptian and Assyrian operations at Harran. The route and objective attributed to Necho in the biblical sources have to be distinguished from the Egyptian defeat four years later.

Dan 1:1–2 puts a siege of Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar in Jehoiakim's third year. In Jeremiah, the battle of 605 BCE falls in Jehoiakim's fourth year, identified with Nebuchadnezzar's first (Jer 25:1; 46:2). Some interpreters explain the difference by whether a king's partial first year was counted. Others see a chronological discrepancy in Daniel's account. The surviving Babylonian evidence gives no independent record of a siege of Jerusalem in 605 BCE.
