## Overview

A wall painting in the tomb of Khnumhotep II at Beni Hasan in Egypt shows a party of people arriving from outside Egypt with goods and pack animals. Its inscription dates the scene to the sixth year of Senusret II, early in the second millennium BCE, and gives the group's size as thirty-seven. The painting shows men, women, and children in distinctive clothing, along with objects carried by the group. It provides a visual comparison for Genesis accounts of travel to Egypt. The reasons for the depicted visit and the party's precise origin remain debated.

## Description and Provenance

The scene is part of the painted decoration of a rock-cut tomb at Beni Hasan in Middle Egypt. Khnumhotep II was a regional governor during the Egyptian Middle Kingdom. An Egyptian official shown in the painting presents a written record that dates the event to year six of Senusret II. The scene belongs to a tomb program that presents the governor's office and his household, so the choice and arrangement of figures serve that wider purpose. [@anep]

## Contents

The inscription calls the visitors *Aamu*, an Egyptian term applied to peoples from areas to the northeast of Egypt, and associates the party with a place or group called Shu. It numbers the group at thirty-seven, though the painting depicts a selection from them. A man whose name is commonly rendered Abisha leads the party. His title means “ruler of foreign lands.” The same Egyptian expression lies behind the name [[hyksos|Hyksos]], later used for a dynasty that ruled northern Egypt. The earlier use shows the title was already applied to a foreign leader before that dynasty. [@anep]

The accompanying text associates the visit with *mesdemet*, usually understood as galena, a lead mineral used in dark eye paint. The clothing includes patterned fabrics, and the animals are shown carrying loads. One person carries a portable lyre, and others carry weapons or implements. Some scholars have identified equipment on a donkey as bellows and proposed that the party included traveling metalworkers. The object's identity remains uncertain.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 12:10 describes Abram going to Egypt during famine. Gen 46:5–7 recounts the journey of Jacob's wider family with livestock and possessions. The scene at Beni Hasan illustrates how an Egyptian artist could portray foreign groups traveling with families and goods. Its inscription gives a different reason for the visit, a delivery involving a valued mineral. It also suggests why the presentation of local products in Gen 43:11 would have been intelligible in accounts of contact with Egyptian officials.

The patterned garments provide evidence for ancient [[clothing-and-dress|clothing and dress]]. Their relevance to Joseph's tunic (Gen 37:3) remains debated. The Hebrew term has been understood as describing either a decorated garment or one extending to the wrists or ankles, among other proposals. The painting gives a visual example of patterned cloth while leaving the meaning of the Hebrew expression open.

## Scholarly Issues

Scholars have understood the visit as an instance of trade, a diplomatic presentation, or part of an expedition connected with obtaining the mineral. The painted scene and captions make the contact clear, and the inscription provides the date and details of the party. The specific route, the status of the visitors, and how literally the scene documents the named event remain subjects of study.
