## Overview

The Arad ostraca are inscribed fragments of pottery found at the fortress of Tel Arad, in the northern Negev. The best-known group dates to the early sixth century BCE, the last years of the kingdom of Judah. It includes messages to an official named Eliashib concerning supplies and military orders. Other sherds come from earlier or later occupation. These everyday records show how Judah managed a frontier post and add context to biblical accounts of the kingdom's final years and its relationship with Edom.

## Description and Provenance

Yohanan Aharoni's excavations at [[arad|Arad]] uncovered numerous texts, mostly written in ink on broken pottery. More than a hundred are in Hebrew, while later phases yielded an Aramaic group and a smaller number of texts in other languages. The Hebrew texts span several periods, and the earliest material could go back to the ninth century BCE. The major group associated with Eliashib belongs with the final Judahite fortress. Subsequent studies by Ze'ev Herzog and Lily Singer-Avitz have revised the dating and interpretation of several layers identified in the original excavation. [@neaehl, 1:82–88] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 5–108]

## Contents

Messages to Eliashib include orders to issue wine, flour and oil. Several refer to the Kittim, a name often taken here to designate a group of troops or travelers from the eastern Mediterranean. Their precise origin and status remain debated. Other records contain lists and amounts. Some use hieratic numerals, signs adapted from an Egyptian writing system and employed in Judah's record keeping. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 5–41] [@ahituv-echoes, 92–153]

Arad 24 orders the dispatch of troops to Ramath-negeb in view of a possible Edomite move against it. Another letter, Arad 40, also refers to [[edomites|Edom]]. The documents show direct military concern about Judah's southern neighbor. Their correspondence concerns local orders and threats rather than a narrative of how the kingdom fell. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, Arad 24 and 40] [@ahituv-echoes, 92–153]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Edom is accused of violence against Judah or of taking advantage of its distress in Obad 10–14, Ps 137:7 and Ezek 25:12–14. The Arad orders show that Edom was a concern to Judahite officials and give a setting in which later hostility can be understood. They supply no direct evidence of the events lamented in those passages. In a separate comparison, the name Kittim occurs in Gen 10:4 and Jer 2:10, where its associations are with western lands, while the Arad orders give the name an immediate local setting.

Arad 18 mentions a “house of Yahweh.” The most common identification is the Jerusalem temple, especially on reconstructions in which the shrine at Arad was already out of use. The words alone do not name the place. The mention is important as evidence that a sanctuary could enter routine official correspondence. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, Arad 18] [@ahituv-echoes, 92–153]

## Scholarly Issues

The texts attest [[writing-and-literacy|practical literacy]] in a garrison far from the capital. Differences in handwriting suggest that several people took part in producing the records. The number of identifiable hands and what it implies for literacy across Judah are debated. The stratigraphic assignment of individual sherds is also important. Arad 40 has been assigned to an earlier occupation than Arad 24, but that dating has been questioned on archaeological and textual grounds. [@neaehl, 1:82–88] [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, Arad 24 and 40]
