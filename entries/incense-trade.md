## Overview

Frankincense and myrrh are aromatic resins from trees and shrubs of southern Arabia and northeastern Africa. Overland caravans and maritime routes carried them to markets in the Levant, Mesopotamia, and around the Mediterranean. Assyrian records document Arabian contacts by the eighth century BCE, and excavated residues attest frankincense at a Judahite shrine. The trade explains why the Bible associates Sheba with costly spices and why aromatics could appear as royal gifts, offerings, or merchandise. The sources give stronger evidence for some periods and routes than for others.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

South Arabian kingdoms, including Saba and Qataban, lay within networks carrying local and imported goods. Camels made long overland journeys practical, and roads connected producing regions and ports with northern Arabian oases, the Levant, and Mesopotamia. By the eighth century BCE Assyrian inscriptions record wealth arriving from [[qedarites-and-arabs|Arabian rulers and communities]]. Tiglath-pileser III mentions Samsi, queen of the Arabs, and tribute that includes aromatics in accounts of contacts with Arabian groups. Sargon II's texts also name Itʾamar the Sabaean in connection with tribute [@rinap1] [@cos2].

In Hellenistic and Roman times the [[nabataeans|Nabataeans]] drew wealth from segments of this trade. Classical descriptions record travel from southern Arabia toward Gaza, while sea routes connected production areas and ports around the Red Sea and Indian Ocean. The evidence points to multiple routes and intermediaries, with opportunities for control and profit changing over time.

Residues from the shrine at [[arad|Arad]] provide direct evidence for frankincense burned in Judah. A study published in 2020 identified frankincense on one altar and cannabis on another. The study does not establish the source of either individual shipment. A proposed Sabaean reading of an inscribed jar fragment from Jerusalem would also have implications for the trade, but the script and text are disputed.

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 1 Kgs 10:1–15 the queen of [[sheba|Sheba]] brings Solomon spices, gold, and precious stones. Assyrian records and South Arabian inscriptions anchor the history of such contacts especially from the eighth century BCE onward. The gifts fit the kinds of goods known from the trade, while the episode cannot by itself date a trade network to the tenth century. Ezek 27:22 lists the merchants of Sheba and Raamah among Tyre's suppliers. Isa 60:6 links camels with gold and frankincense, and Ps 72:10, 15 uses gifts from Sheba in its portrait of royal prosperity.

Gen 37:25 describes a caravan carrying aromatics to Egypt, without identifying the goods as frankincense. Job 6:19 names the caravans of Tema and travelers of Sheba. Isa 21:13–14 links Dedanites with caravans and calls on Tema to assist fugitives. Together the passages show the association of particular Arabian peoples and oases with long-distance travel.

## Interpretive Significance

Jer 6:20 explicitly connects frankincense with Sheba. Exod 30:34 includes it among the ingredients of [[incense-and-incense-altars|sacred incense]], while Matt 2:11 places frankincense and myrrh among the magi's valuable gifts. These passages draw on the cost and desirability of aromatics in different ways. The movement of the goods from distant sources explains their value without establishing where a particular biblical gift was produced or how it traveled.
