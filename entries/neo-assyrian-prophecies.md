## Overview

Neo-Assyrian prophetic texts record messages given in the names of gods to Assyrian kings, especially Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal. The surviving documents include individual reports and collections of oracles copied together. Many messages come from prophets associated with Ishtar of Arbela. They promise help, reassure the king, or legitimate his possession of the throne. They provide unusually close comparisons for the reassurance of Isaiah and show how royal courts preserved prophetic speech for use beyond the original occasion.

## Manuscripts and Date

The main corpus survives on first-millennium cuneiform tablets from Nineveh, mostly involving kings of the seventh century BCE. Simo Parpola's edition gathers eleven tablets. Some preserve collections arranged by scribes, while others transmit individual reports. The copies give access to speeches through the court's work of recording, editing, and archiving them.

## Contents

Ishtar of Arbela speaks in many of the oracles. Prophets named in the collection include women such as Issar-la-tašiyaṭ and people whose identity or gender is harder to recover. Akkadian terms for prophetic personnel include *raggimu* and *raggintu*. Several messages reassure [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] that the goddess will support him against his enemies. Their language fits the crisis surrounding his claim to power and the continuing importance of succession. The prophets also speak for other deities and in other situations.

In some messages Ishtar uses maternal imagery to describe her care for the king. The divine speaker may promise to guide, protect, or sustain him as a parent cares for a child. Appeals not to fear fit this same pattern of reassurance. Later oracles support [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]], and the collection as a whole reflects a court that preserved favorable prophecies about kingship.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 41:10, 13–14 and 43:1–5 use language of reassurance in which God promises to be present with Israel and to help or redeem the people. A comparable Assyrian form addresses the king and promises divine help against threats. In these passages from Isaiah the recipient is Jacob or Israel, so language readily used of a chosen monarch applies to the people. Isa 44:2 describes God forming Israel from the womb, and Isa 66:12–13 compares the people's comfort with maternal care. The Assyrian use of nurturing imagery for the king gives a regional comparison.

In 2 Sam 7:8–16 divine speech assures David of a lasting house and rule. The royal addressee makes this a different point of comparison from the reassurance addressed to all Israel. Jer 36:1–4 records Jeremiah dictating a collection of prophecies to Baruch. The Assyrian collections show that oral prophecy could also be gathered on written documents in another scribal setting.

## Scholarly Issues

Some prophecies may have circulated during political crises before their collection, while their final written arrangement belongs to the activity of the royal scribes. The archive favors utterances the court considered worth keeping. The parallels in form and language clarify conventions shared by prophetic texts, while leaving the particular routes of transmission open.
