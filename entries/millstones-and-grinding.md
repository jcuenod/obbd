## Overview

Millstones crush or grind grain into flour or meal. In Iron Age households, a person commonly moved a small stone back and forth over a larger stationary one. Later hopper mills and rotary mills changed how grain was fed and how the stones moved. Some Roman mills were large enough to be turned by a donkey. Biblical writers use grinding as a familiar image of household life, hard labor, or humiliation. Israelite law also treats a millstone as essential equipment for producing a family's daily food.

## Archaeological Evidence

The saddle quern has a broad lower stone and a smaller handstone. Repeated strokes over the working surface can wear the lower stone into a shallow curve. Basalt and other volcanic rocks were commonly used, and their abrasiveness helps break up the grain. Grinding regularly took substantial time and effort. Finds of such stones within [[four-room-house|houses]] document where food was prepared and can help explain how work was distributed across domestic spaces.

The Olynthus or hopper-rubber mill has an upper stone with a cavity that feeds grain between the surfaces. It could be moved back and forth with a lever, making it possible to grind larger quantities. Rotary mills instead move one stone in a circular motion against another. Small rotary querns could be turned by hand. Larger Roman mills could be powered by an animal and used to produce flour in quantity. Finds show that these designs overlapped and that older forms continued in use alongside newer ones.

## In the Bible

Exod 11:5 refers to an enslaved woman working behind a handmill. Num 11:8 places milling and pounding in a mortar side by side as ways of processing manna. A handheld upper stone was heavy enough to wound or kill when dropped, as in the death of Abimelech (Judg 9:53). Job 41:24 (MT 41:16) compares Leviathan's heart to a solid lower millstone. The comparison draws on a household object familiar for its mass and durability.

Deut 24:6 prohibits taking a mill or upper millstone as a pledge because it would take a person's livelihood. Taking just the upper stone could leave an entire mill unusable. A comparable rule requires a poor debtor's garment to be returned by nightfall because it is needed for warmth (Exod 22:26–27, MT 22:25–26). In Judg 16:21, Samson is forced to grind in prison. Isa 47:2 represents Babylon's loss of privilege by making her take up the millstones.

Jer 25:10 imagines a place deprived of the sound of milling and the light of a lamp. Eccl 12:3–4 likewise pictures grinders ceasing and their sound growing faint. Matt 24:41 uses two women grinding together to picture sudden separation during daily work. The millstone in Matt 18:6 is described in Greek as a donkey millstone, evoking a particularly large weight. Rev 18:21–22 uses a great millstone cast into the sea and the disappearance of milling sounds to portray Babylon's fall.
