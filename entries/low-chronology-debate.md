## Overview

The Low Chronology debate concerns the dates of early Iron Age buildings and pottery in the southern Levant. Excavators once assigned some major buildings at Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer to Solomon's tenth-century BCE reign. Later proposals moved some of the same remains into the ninth century. Radiocarbon measurements and improved excavation records have refined the question. The dates matter for reconstructing political organization under David, Solomon, and the Omrides, and for determining how far biblical accounts of construction and administration can be connected with particular sites.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Yigael Yadin compared monumental gates at [[hazor|Hazor]], [[megiddo|Megiddo]], and [[gezer|Gezer]] in light of their appearance together in 1 Kgs 9:15. The conventional chronology associated structures at these sites with Solomon. Israel Finkelstein's Low Chronology moved the transition between Iron I and Iron IIA later and reassigned some of the monuments to the ninth-century Omrides or to a ninth-century historical setting. Amihai Mazar's Modified Conventional Chronology proposed a long Iron IIA extending approximately from 980 to 840 BCE, allowing room for building in both centuries [@mazar-archaeology] [@neaehl, 5:1774–1776].

Radiocarbon programs at Tel Reḥov, Megiddo, Khirbet Qeiyafa, and in the Faynan region have improved the absolute dating of particular contexts. Reliable results still require suitable samples, firm stratigraphic assignments, and careful comparison of probability ranges. Studies disagree over some associations and models. The [[bubastite-portal|campaign record of Shoshenq I]] provides an external late tenth-century synchronism, although linking a particular destruction layer to the campaign generally requires more than a plausible date.

## In the Hebrew Bible

1 Kgs 9:15–19 attributes building work at Jerusalem and the three northern and western sites to Solomon. 1 Kgs 4:7–19 describes districts supporting the court, and 1 Kgs 10:14–29 portrays extensive wealth and trade. These claims can be compared with evidence for administration and monumental building, while archaeologists debate the date and attribution of individual buildings. 1 Kgs 14:25–26 describes Shishak attacking Jerusalem, independently comparable with the wider context of Shoshenq's campaign without identifying every city or layer affected.

2 Sam 5:6–12 and 8:1–14 present [[david|David]] taking Jerusalem, building a royal house with foreign assistance, and extending control. Eilat Mazar proposed connecting the Large Stone Structure in Jerusalem with David's palace. The dating and interpretation of it and the associated Stepped Stone Structure remain discussed. Likewise, [[khirbet-qeiyafa|Khirbet Qeiyafa]] is near the Valley of Elah of 1 Sam 17:1–2. Its excavators have argued that it was a Judahite fortress in the time of David, while other scholars propose different political affiliations.

## Interpretive Significance

The chronology and the scale of political organization are related questions. Establishing a date for a wall or gate does not by itself identify its builder or the reach of the government behind it. Some differences among current chronologies concern decades and involve local sequences. The implications for the kingdom described under [[solomon|Solomon]] also depend on how isolated monuments, patterns of settlement, and biblical accounts are weighed together.
