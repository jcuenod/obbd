## Overview

The Persepolis Fortification tablets are administrative records from the reign of Darius I, dated mainly from 509 to 493 BCE. Excavators recovered them in the fortification area of Persepolis in southern Iran. They record the handling of goods, rations, travel supplies, and offerings in the Achaemenid heartland. A separate group from the Treasury documents some later administrative activity. These archives illuminate the orders, seals, and material resources that made imperial government work, providing a comparison for Persian-period settings in Ezra, Nehemiah, and Esther.

## Manuscripts and Date

The University of Chicago’s Oriental Institute, now the Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures, excavated the Fortification tablets in 1933–34. The finds include many tablets and fragments bearing Elamite cuneiform, some with Aramaic writing, and seal impressions on tablets without readable texts. The related Treasury archive belongs to a distinct findspot and extends from the reign of Darius through the early reign of Artaxerxes I, approximately 492–458 BCE. The two archives answer different questions about supplies, accounting, and payments.

The finds remained Iranian property and material was lent to Chicago for study. A legal dispute concerning an attempt to seize the objects in satisfaction of a judgment against Iran did not result in their seizure. Returns to Iran have taken place in stages. Study through the Persepolis Fortification Archive Project includes digital images, editions, and documentation of seal impressions. These records support research when tablets and fragments are dispersed among study and storage locations.

## Contents

Many tablets concern transfers of grain, wine, livestock, and other goods. They record what officials were to supply to workers, travelers, institutions, and religious personnel. Rations for travelers could be linked to written authorizations. The documents name places, offices, and people, allowing the reconstruction of some routes and relationships among administrative centers. The seals identify or mark the participation of particular officials or offices and complement the written documents.

Allocations for cultic purposes include offerings associated with gods of different regional traditions. Wouter Henkelman’s study of the texts shows that such offerings belonged within ordinary economic administration. They provide evidence of support from managed resources, while the arrangements and motives behind religious patronage could differ from place to place.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Neh 2:7–9 connects official letters with travel and access to wood from a royal estate. Esth 3:13–15 and 8:10, 14 imagine written commands distributed by couriers. The archives show how written authorization, supplies, and travel could work together. They document the imperial heartland, so their details cannot be transferred automatically to Judah or to every literary account. Eccl 2:5 evokes planted estates, and Neh 5:14–18 compares practices of provisioning governors. Both can be read with attention to the material costs of maintaining an official establishment.

Ezra 6:9–10 describes provision for temple offerings, and Ezra 4:7 associates Aramaic with administration. The archive provides context for both allocation and [[aramaic-language|administrative Aramaic]], while its largest textual component is Elamite. Linguistic diversity was part of the operation of the empire.

## Scholarly Issues

Several people in the texts bear the name Marduka or a related form. The name is evidence for the plausibility of names of this kind in the Persian setting. It does not establish a link with Mordecai in Esther. More broadly, the archive is a regional sample recording selected transactions. Its importance lies in the detail of what survives, rather than in a claim that the same records or institutions were uniform throughout the empire.
