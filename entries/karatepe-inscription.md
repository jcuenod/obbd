## Overview

The Karatepe inscription is a bilingual monument from an Iron Age fortress in Cilicia, in what is now southern Türkiye. Its ruler Azatiwada describes protecting his people, securing roads and founding a fortified settlement. The text survives in Phoenician and in Hieroglyphic Luwian, a form of Anatolian writing using picture-like signs. The paired versions were instrumental in deciphering the Luwian script. The inscription offers biblical scholars comparisons for the public language of good rule, safety and prosperity in the eighth or early seventh century BCE.

## Description and Provenance

The inscriptions belong to the fortified site of Karatepe-Aslantaş. The discovery of the site and inscriptions in 1946 was followed by excavations headed by Helmuth Theodor Bossert, with Halet Çambel a central figure in their excavation, recording and conservation. The texts were displayed at the gates and are associated with the sculptural program of the fortress. The parallel versions gave scholars an extended Phoenician text with which to compare recurring Luwian signs and names. [@kai, no. 26] [@cos2, 2.31]

## Contents

Azatiwada credits his position to the favor of Baal and names Awariku, king of the Danunians. He describes improving conditions in the land of Adana, building strongholds, supporting his ruler’s dynasty and securing roads. He represents public safety through the image of a woman traveling alone with spindles. The text closes with curses against anyone who attempts to erase Azatiwada’s name or replace it. [@kai, no. 26] [@cos2, 2.31]

The versions also help identify how different languages and traditions designated comparable gods. The Phoenician Baal corresponds to the Luwian storm god Tarhunza. A Phoenician title of [[resheph|Resheph]] is often understood as associating him with birds. Each version uses vocabulary appropriate to its own readership, and interpreting particular divine epithets still requires attention to their context. [@kai, no. 26] [@ddd]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

1 Kgs 4:25 (MT 5:5) and Mic 4:4 use the image of sitting securely under vine and fig tree. Zech 8:4–5 pictures a restored city through the presence of elderly people and children in public spaces. Judg 5:6 gives the opposite picture of abandoned highways and indirect travel. Azatiwada’s image of a lone traveler makes safety concrete in a comparable way. It is part of his claim that the ruler can make ordinary activity possible. The probable reference to the Cilician region of Kue in 1 Kgs 10:28–29 supplies a geographical comparison in the context of the horse trade. [@cos2, 2.31] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Awariku is generally identified with Urikki, the ruler of Que named in Assyrian records. This anchors Azatiwada in the political world of the late eighth century BCE, though the monument itself may date to the early seventh century. Proposed links between the Danunian name and the biblical tribe of Dan or the Greek Danaans remain speculative. Similar names alone cannot resolve the histories of the peoples who used them. [@rinap1] [@kai, no. 26]
