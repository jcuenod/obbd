## Overview

Assyrian artists carved winged and other supernatural figures on palace walls and made smaller figures that could be buried in buildings. Many of these images, especially those from the Neo-Assyrian period, can be compared with ritual descriptions of *apkallu*, or sages. The figures include people wearing fish cloaks, bird-headed beings and winged figures with human heads. Assyrian texts connect them with protecting and purifying important spaces. Traditions about sages before the flood also provide a possible background for later Jewish stories about heavenly beings and forbidden knowledge.

## Archaeological Evidence

The Northwest Palace of Ashurnasirpal II at [[nimrud|Nimrud]], built in the ninth century BCE, used carved stone panels to depict winged figures alongside the king or a [[assyrian-sacred-tree|stylized tree]]. Some figures have human heads and others have the head of a bird of prey. Several carry a small bucket and a raised cone-shaped implement. The exact nature of that implement is uncertain, and interpreting the action as pollination of a date palm goes beyond what the images establish. [@anep] [@keel-symbolism]

Smaller objects preserve a related repertoire. Some depict a person looking out from beneath a fish's head, with the body of the fish hanging down the back like a cloak. Ritual instructions describe the making and placement of several groups of protective figures in houses. Comparison of figures and inscriptions with these rituals led Frans Wiggermann to identify particular types as sages, while leaving room for other kinds of guardian in Assyrian art. The terminology for the cone-shaped implement also points to ritual cleansing. [@ddd]

## In the Bible

The fish cloak is sometimes associated with [[dagon|Dagon]], the god whose statue falls and breaks in 1 Sam 5:2–5. The identification depends in part on resemblance between the divine name and the Hebrew word *dāg* (“fish”). Ancient evidence identifies Dagan as an important deity of Syria and the middle Euphrates, and scholars have proposed a connection with grain, though his precise functions are debated. The fish-cloaked figures belong to a separate Assyrian tradition of protective sages. [@ddd]

Gen 6:1–4 tells of the union of heavenly beings with human women. The Book of the Watchers in [[first-enoch|First Enoch]] develops the story and associates the heavenly beings with the teaching of forbidden arts. Some scholars see a connection with the Mesopotamian traditions of [[enmeduranki-and-the-apkallu|sages who lived before the flood]], including the ones named in a later list of kings and sages from Uruk and the fish-clad teacher Oannes described by Berossus. Even if there is a historical relationship, the literary traditions are the basis for the comparison. The Assyrian reliefs depict protective figures and supply no direct parallel to the Watchers' rebellion. [@ddd] [@abd]

Ezek 28:12–17 has also been compared with imagery of supernatural guardians because it includes a cherub. The wording about the cherub is difficult, and the proposed connection with the Assyrian sages is disputed. The biblical passage belongs to a lament about the king of Tyre and gives no description of the fish-cloaked or cone-bearing types. [@ddd]
