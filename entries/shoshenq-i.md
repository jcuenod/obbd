## Overview

Shoshenq I founded Egypt’s Twenty-second Dynasty and campaigned in the Levant near the end of his reign, conventionally dated around 925 BCE. A monumental scene at Karnak lists places associated with his victories, and a fragment of a royal stele bearing his name was found at Megiddo. He is widely identified with the Shishak who sheltered Jeroboam and later took valuables from Jerusalem in the biblical account. His campaign provides a chronological anchor for the early divided monarchy, although the Egyptian and biblical sources emphasize different places and events.

## Extrabiblical Sources

The [[bubastite-portal|Bubastite Portal]] at Karnak depicts the king triumphing over foreign enemies and preserves name rings associated with places in the Levant. Some names are recognizably related to locations in the southern Levant, while others are damaged or uncertain. The arrangement is a royal display. It cannot be read directly as a military itinerary in exact order. Jerusalem is not securely identified among the surviving names.

A fragment of a stone monument from the Chicago excavations at [[megiddo|Megiddo]] preserves parts of Shoshenq’s name. It provides direct evidence that a monument associated with his rule was set up or deposited at the site. Its archaeological context does not securely date a particular destruction layer, and the surviving text does not specify exactly how Egyptian forces gained access to the city. The monument and portal together make the northern reach of the campaign more visible than the biblical accounts alone.

## Biblical Portrayal

First Kings 11:40 reports that Jeroboam fled to Shishak in Egypt after Solomon sought to kill him. First Kings 14:25–26 places Shishak’s attack in the fifth year of Rehoboam and says he took valuables from the temple and palace. The account focuses on Jerusalem and its losses. It does not describe the campaign’s wider geographical range or say that Jerusalem itself was destroyed.

Second Chronicles 12:2–9 expands the account with a list of peoples accompanying the king and a religious interpretation of the attack. The Sukkiim of 12:3 have been compared with a term in Egyptian sources for a group associated with Libyan auxiliaries or scouts. The relationship is possible, but neither the form of the name nor its narrative setting fixes the identification. The chronicler’s emphasis on repentance and partial relief gives the account a distinct purpose.

## Historical Assessment

The identification of Shishak with Shoshenq I is accepted nearly universally in historical scholarship. The names, chronological setting, and evidence of a Levantine campaign converge on it. Estimates for the campaign around 925 BCE depend on Egyptian chronology and can vary by several years. The synchronism therefore anchors a general period more precisely than it supplies a universally accepted absolute year for Rehoboam’s accession.

The portal and biblical narratives need not cover the same parts of the campaign. The Egyptian source celebrates victory and lists places. Kings and Chronicles explain what the attack meant for Judah and its royal house. Those emphases allow historical comparison while leaving the precise movements of the army and the terms of any submission uncertain.
