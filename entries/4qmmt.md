## Overview

Some Precepts of the Torah, usually called 4QMMT, is a Hebrew legal composition preserved in six fragmentary manuscripts from Qumran Cave 4. It sets out rulings that distinguish its authors from other Jews, especially on purity and the sanctuary. The conclusion explains why the authors have written and connects correct conduct with righteousness. Its phrase “works of the law” provides an important Jewish comparison for Paul's wording, while the purpose and argument of each text must be considered separately.

## Manuscripts and Date

The Cave 4 copies are numbered 4Q394–399. No one copy preserves the whole text. Editions align overlapping passages to form a composite, and the familiar divisions A, B, and C belong to that reconstructed arrangement. The copies establish circulation in the Second Temple period. They do not settle exactly when or for whom the composition was written [@djd10].

## Contents

The central section gives rulings about offerings, purity, and access to holy things. The authors explain their separation from other people and appeal to an addressee to consider their judgments. In the closing section they refer to some of the “works of the law” and anticipate that doing what is right will be reckoned as righteousness (C26–32). A calendrical section often printed before the rulings has a less certain relationship to the rest of the work.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gal 2:16 and Rom 3:20–28 use the Greek expression “works of the law” in arguments about righteousness. The Hebrew expression in 4QMMT shows that related wording could refer to particular acts of legal observance. It cannot by itself determine the scope of Paul's phrase or identify his opponents [@dunn-4qmmt].

Gal 2:11–14 illustrates how a dispute over conduct could affect shared meals. Mark 7:1–23 likewise shows disagreement about practices described as matters of purity and tradition. The rulings in 4QMMT document diversity among Jewish interpreters of Torah. They concern different circumstances from the narrative disputes in Galatians and Mark.

## Scholarly Issues

The composition resembles a letter because its writers address another party and urge a response. The absence of an intact opening and the composite nature of the text leave its original form and audience uncertain. Some have read it in light of divisions reflected in the [[community-rule]] and [[temple-scroll]]. Those comparisons help place its concerns without identifying the authors or the addressee by name.
