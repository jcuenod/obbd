## Overview

Assyria was a state of northern Mesopotamia centered on the upper Tigris. The Neo-Assyrian Empire, which took shape in the early first millennium BCE, reached from Mesopotamia across much of the Levant and at times into Egypt. Its kings took tribute from Israel and Judah, conquered Samaria, and made Judah a dependent kingdom. Their inscriptions and palace reliefs provide independent evidence for people and events in the books of Kings. The empire also provides a setting for biblical texts about forced resettlement, loyalty to rulers, and the use and limits of imperial power.

## Sources

Assyrian royal inscriptions record campaigns, tribute, and construction in the names of the kings who commissioned them. Their claims to victory and divine favor belong to their purpose as royal monuments. Letters and administrative tablets offer perspectives on provincial government, while treaties show how the kings demanded loyalty. Royal reliefs can identify specific places or events, as in the sculptures of the siege of Lachish, and at the same time present them from the palace's viewpoint [@rima3, A.0.102.2, A.0.102.14] [@rinap3-1, no. 22] [@saa2, xv–xxxiv, 4–78].

The Assyrians identified years with the names of annual officials called eponyms. The [[assyrian-eponym-list|Assyrian eponym records]] include an eclipse identified with a solar eclipse in 763 BCE. This provides an absolute date for the sequence and helps fix the dates of campaigns connected with rulers named in the Bible.

## History

The Old Assyrian period is especially known through records of merchants trading in Anatolia in the early second millennium BCE. Middle Assyrian rulers expanded their power across northern Mesopotamia in the later second millennium. The renewed expansion that followed is usually called Neo-Assyrian. Its capital moved from Ashur to Kalhu, now Nimrud, and later to Dur-Sharrukin, now Khorsabad, and [[nineveh|Nineveh]]. Ashur remained an important religious center.

In the ninth century BCE, Shalmaneser III encountered a coalition that included Ahab of Israel at Qarqar in 853 BCE and later counted Jehu among the rulers who sent tribute. Later Assyrian records name Joash, Menahem, Pekah, and Hoshea of Israel and Ahaz, Hezekiah, and Manasseh of Judah. These records provide points at which independent chronologies meet [@rima3, A.0.102.2 ii 78–102, A.0.102.14 lines 44–47, 92–102] [@rinap1, nos. 20–21, 35, 42] [@rinap3-1, no. 22 ii 37–iii 49] [@rinap4, no. 1 v 54–vi 1].

Under [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]], Assyria increased direct control over territories that had previously paid tribute through local rulers. A province had an appointed governor, while a dependent kingdom could retain its ruler on terms imposed by Assyria. The empire moved populations between conquered territories, taking people out of some districts and bringing settlers into them. This [[deportation|forced resettlement]] supported labor needs and state administration, while its scale and the demographic effects at specific sites remain debated. After the reign of [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]], Babylonian and Median forces captured Ashur in 614 BCE and Nineveh in 612 BCE. The remaining Assyrian forces lost Harran in 610 BCE and failed to recover it in 609 BCE [@abc, 93–96].

## In the Bible

2 Kgs 15:19–20, 29 describes Menahem's payment to Pul, the name used for Tiglath-pileser III in that episode, and later Assyrian seizures and deportations under Pekah. In 2 Kgs 16:7–9 Ahaz appeals to the same Assyrian king for help against Damascus and Israel. 2 Kgs 17:1–24 describes Samaria's fall, the removal of Israelites, and the settlement of people from elsewhere. Assyrian records place the capture in the transition between Shalmaneser V and Sargon II, and scholars differ over how to relate the two kings' roles. In 2 Kgs 18:13–19:37, [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] takes fortified towns in Judah, and Jerusalem survives.

Isa 10:5–19 presents Assyria as the instrument of divine anger and condemns the king for claiming success as his own achievement. Scholars widely read the passage against Assyrian accounts of conquest and imperial kingship, though specific links with individual inscriptions are debated. The [[esarhaddon-succession-treaty|loyalty oath of Esarhaddon]] likewise provides a close comparison for the curses of Deut 28:23–24, where the land and sky are likened to metals and rain fails. Scholars differ over whether such passages adapt particular treaty texts or reflect more widely circulating forms of imperial language [@saa2, 51].
