## Overview

Hadad was a storm god worshiped across Syria and Mesopotamia in the Bronze and Iron Ages. Akkadian sources usually call him Adad, while the god known as Baal at Ugarit shares the name Hadad and the powers of rain and thunder. Aramaic royal inscriptions invoke local forms of the storm god. In the Bible, the name appears in royal names and may stand behind Rimmon, the god worshiped at Damascus. Zechariah's reference to mourning associated with Hadad-rimmon has several possible explanations, including a god's name or a place name.

## Sources and Iconography

Storm gods in Syria and Mesopotamia were associated with the rain that sustained agriculture and with weather that could damage it. Weather also provided a language for divine power in battle and royal protection. At Ugarit, [[baal|Baal]], a title meaning “lord,” is also known as Hadad. Images of the storm god can depict a raised weapon or a representation of lightning. A carved figure with these attributes can often be identified as a storm god even where the precise local name is unknown. [@ddd] [@parker-unp] [@anep]

The [[tell-fekheriye-statue|Tell Fekheriye statue]] bears an Aramaic and Akkadian dedication to Hadad of Sikan. The figure on the statue is the ruler who commissioned it, while its inscription names the god whose favor he requests. A distinct statue from Gerçin near Zincirli depicts Hadad himself and bears an inscription of Panamuwa I of Samʾal. It links Hadad with royal authority and with the commemoration of the king. The two monuments show how the storm god could be invoked in different contexts by Iron Age rulers. [@kai] [@cos2]

## In the Hebrew Bible

In 2 Kgs 5:18, Naaman describes entering the house of Rimmon with the king of [[damascus|Damascus]]. Rimmon is usually connected with the Akkadian weather-god title *Rammānu*, meaning “thunderer.” Its Hebrew form also resembles the word for pomegranate. Some scholars see a play on words, but the spelling alone does not establish that intention. Hadad also appears in royal names, including [[ben-hadad|Ben-hadad]], meaning “son of Hadad,” in 1 Kgs 15:18. The presence of the god's name in a royal name shows his importance, although such names do not reveal specific rituals. [@ddd] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Zech 12:11 compares future mourning in Jerusalem to mourning associated with Hadad-rimmon in the plain of Megiddo. One interpretation takes Hadad-rimmon as a divine name and sees a reference to ritual lament. Another reads it as the name of a place and understands the mourning as remembering an event there. The traditions about Josiah's death near Megiddo have also shaped the interpretation. The verse assumes that its readers understand the comparison but does not explain the event itself. The evidence does not securely identify a rite for a dying Hadad at Megiddo. [@ddd]
