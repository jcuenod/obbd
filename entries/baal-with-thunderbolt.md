## Overview

Baal with Thunderbolt is a carved limestone monument from Ugarit on the northern Syrian coast. The upright stone shows a striding god with a raised club and a long, branching weapon. A much smaller figure stands near him. The relief is commonly understood as an image of Baal, the storm god worshiped at Ugarit in the second millennium BCE. Its combination of weapons and a plant-like design helps explain the ancient association of storms with both destructive power and agricultural prosperity. Biblical poets likewise portray the God of Israel wielding thunder, lightning, and weapons together.

## Description and Provenance

The monument was found in 1932 on the slope west of the Temple of Baal at [[ugarit|Ugarit]], modern Ras Shamra. It is in the Louvre, whose inventory number is AO 15775. The curved top and carved face make it a stele, an upright stone monument. A commonly given range is the fifteenth to the thirteenth century BCE, within the Late Bronze Age. Estimates depend on the context of discovery and comparison of the figure's dress and stance with other images. The stone has no date inscription, and the museum uses a broader dating range that includes the Middle Bronze Age. <!-- VERIFY: Check the latest specialist dating against the range in the Louvre record and the discussion of style and find context in the site publication. -->

## Contents

The god wears a horned headdress and strides toward the right. He holds a club above his shoulder and grips a long shaft with a branching design. The smaller figure stands between his body and the shaft. The difference of scale marks the larger figure as the dominant one in the scene, and the smaller is often identified as a king standing under the god's protection.

The raised arm and forward stride belong to the [[smiting-the-enemy-motif|smiting pose]], in which an Egyptian or Levantine god or ruler is shown ready to strike. Levantine figurines use related poses, and Syrian images of the storm deity [[hadad-rimmon|Hadad]] offer comparisons for the weapon and stance. The wavy bands under the larger figure have been read as mountains, water, or a combination. These interpretations draw on imagery of the god's domain in texts such as the [[baal-cycle|Baal Cycle]].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The imagery helps explain how a flash of lightning could be portrayed as a weapon. In Hab 3:11, the sun and moon stand still before the gleam of God's arrows and spear. Zech 9:14 compares God's arrow to lightning. Ps 18:13–14 (MT 18:14–15) combines thunder, arrows, and lightning, and Ps 77:17–18 (MT 77:18–19) uses similar language in a scene of storm and shaken earth.

Ps 29:3–9 describes God's voice thundering over the waters and breaking cedars. The imagery belongs to a wider regional vocabulary for divine power, which the Ugarit relief also illustrates visually. The branching shaft may associate lightning with plant growth. That reading makes sense of the importance of rain in the confrontation between Elijah and the prophets of Baal, framed by drought and the eventual return of rain (1 Kgs 17:1; 18:17–46).

## Scholarly Issues

The usual identification with [[baal|Baal]] depends on the relief's setting near his temple and on the attributes of a storm god. The design on the shaft may suggest a plant, a bolt of lightning, or their deliberate combination. The small figure's identity and the bands beneath the god's feet are also uncertain. The carving can illuminate the Bible's imagery without requiring a connection between any individual biblical author and the object.
