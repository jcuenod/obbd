## Overview

At Tang-i Var in the Zagros Mountains of western Iran, a relief and cuneiform inscription commemorate the Assyrian king Sargon II. The inscription includes a retrospective account of the Ashdod rebellion. It describes Yamani's flight to Kush and his surrender to Assyria by the Kushite ruler Shebitku. The account supplies an Assyrian witness to the capture of Ashdod named in Isa 20:1 and gives political background for Isaiah's warning about reliance on Egypt and Kush.

## Description and Provenance

The monument is carved into rock in a mountain gorge. It shows a royal figure and an Akkadian inscription recording Sargon's campaigns and achievements. The date of the Ashdod campaign, commonly placed in 711 BCE, should be distinguished from the later engraving of the monument. The carving collects events from different regions and years in one account [@rinap2, Sargon II 116].

## Contents

In lines 19–21 Sargon describes the defeat of Ashdod. Yamani fled to Meluḫḫa, the Assyrian name for Kush in this context. Sargon then credits his own power for Shebitku's decision to send Yamani back in bonds. The sequence preserves names and movements of political actors while presenting them within the rhetoric of an Assyrian victory.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Isa 20:1 dates the prophetic episode to the year in which Sargon sent his commander against [[ashdod]]. The inscription records the same conquest from the perspective of the Assyrian court. Isa 20:3–6 turns that setting into a warning about trusting Egypt and [[cush|Kush]]. Yamani's failed refuge makes the warning intelligible as a political comparison. The inscription has no account of Isaiah's symbolic action.

## Scholarly Issues

The naming of Shebitku makes this source important for debates about the order and dates of Kushite rulers. Its account concerns Yamani's extradition, which must be considered separately from the Ashdod campaign and the carving of the inscription. It does not identify who commanded the Egyptian and Kushite forces opposed by Sennacherib in 701 BCE. The entry on [[sargon-ii-inscriptions|Sargon's inscriptions]] compares this account with the king's other records of the western campaigns.
