## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern texts portray dreams as a way to receive a warning, a command, or information about the future. They distinguish dreams with a clearly spoken message from symbolic dreams that call for interpretation. Letters from Bronze Age Mari, Egyptian and Mesopotamian dream manuals, and royal monuments illustrate the range of practices. Biblical accounts from Genesis to Daniel use both kinds of dream, while prophetic and legal texts consider how to recognize a false claim to revelation. The background helps explain why dreams could carry authority and why their meaning and source could be disputed.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

A. Leo Oppenheim distinguished direct message dreams from symbolic dreams and described the literary forms in which ancient scribes recorded them. The dream can lead to a public action or explain a royal decision. The [[gudea-cylinders|Gudea Cylinders]] connect dreams with building a temple. The Egyptian [[dream-stele|Dream Stele]] presents a god's promise of kingship to Thutmose IV. In the defense of his accession, the Hittite king Ḫattušili III describes dreams through which his goddess supported him and spoke to his wife Puduḫepa [@cos1, 1.77] [@cos2, 2.155].

Dreams about rulers could come through other people. The [[mari-letters|Mari correspondence]] records messages reported to the king by people outside the circle of professional diviners as well as by religious personnel. A seer's dream of Ishtar is cited in an inscription of Ashurbanipal as an assurance of support. Nabonidus likewise presents dreams as grounds for royal decisions, including the restoration of the moon god's sanctuary at Harran [@nissinen-prophets, 19–21, 39–43] [@anet, 451, 606].

The New Kingdom Egyptian dream book preserved in Papyrus Chester Beatty III classifies dream images and actions as favorable or unfavorable and gives interpretations. In Mesopotamia the collection known as *Ziqīqu*, named for a deity associated with dreams, contains a series of dream omens. An omen associates a sign with an outcome. These collections illuminate an institutional setting for [[divination]] alongside the narrative use of royal and private dreams.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Direct messages include God's warning to Abimelech in Gen 20:3–7 and the instructions to the magi and Joseph in Matt 2:12–13. At Bethel both image and speech appear in Jacob's dream (Gen 28:12–15). In 1 Kgs 3:5–15, Solomon converses with YHWH in a dream at Gibeon. The sanctuary setting raises the question of [[dream-incubation|dream incubation]], or deliberately seeking a dream.

Other dreams need someone to interpret symbols. Joseph's family recognizes the implication of his dreams in Gen 37:5–11, and Gideon overhears a Midianite interpreting another man's dream in Judg 7:13–15. In Gen 40:1–41:36, Joseph interprets the dreams of two officials and of Pharaoh. He attributes interpretation to God in Gen 40:8 and 41:16. Pharaoh's specialists are called *ḥarṭummîm*, the plural of a word usually traced to an Egyptian title connected with lector priests, who recited and performed ritual texts. The term is one of several [[egyptian-loanwords|Egyptian features]] used in discussions of the Joseph story's setting and composition. Donald Redford and others have argued for a first-millennium context for some names and expressions, and the dating of the story remains debated.

Dan 2:1–45 sets Daniel's disclosure and interpretation of Nebuchadnezzar's dream against the failure of the king's experts. The tree dream in Dan 4:4–27 (MT 4:1–24) likewise requires an interpreter. Both episodes have an ancient court setting in which kings turn to experts. Joseph and Daniel explain their success by appeal to revelation, and court professionals in the accounts fail to obtain the decisive information.

## Interpretive Significance

Num 12:6–8 recognizes dreams and visions as means of [[prophecy-in-the-ane|prophetic revelation]], while giving Moses a more direct encounter. Deut 13:1–5 (MT 13:2–6) refuses a dreamer's authority if the message promotes other gods, even when a predicted sign occurs. Jer 23:25–32 condemns prophets who claim divine authority for false dreams. The biblical treatment is therefore as much about who may speak for God as about the symbolic interpretation of dreams.
