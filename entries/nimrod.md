## Overview

Nimrod appears in Genesis as a mighty hunter and ruler associated with the cities of Mesopotamia. His account interrupts the genealogical form of the Table of Nations to describe an individual known for exceptional strength and the extent of his kingdom. Assyrian depictions of royal hunts and Mesopotamian traditions about early empire offer useful comparisons. Scholars have proposed several models for the figure, while later Jewish tradition made him the ruler behind the building of Babel and used him to explore the dangers of human power.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Mesopotamian rulers represented the hunt as a display of royal strength. Assyrian palace reliefs picture kings hunting lions in tightly controlled scenes, sometimes emphasizing the danger faced by the ruler. Such images turned prowess into a public claim about the person qualified to rule. The regional association of hunting and royal authority gives a setting for the biblical description of Nimrod.

Some scholars compare Nimrod with Ninurta, a god associated with warfare and mighty deeds, or with Tukulti-Ninurta I, an Assyrian king who took Babylon. Other proposals draw on traditions about Sargon of Akkad and his successors or on [[gilgamesh-epic|Gilgamesh]]. Each can explain some part of the portrayal, such as the combination of might and city rule. The biblical account and Mesopotamian records leave open whether the portrait draws on one figure or several traditions.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 10:8–12 places Nimrod in the [[table-of-nations|Table of Nations]] as a descendant of Cush and recounts a saying about his hunting before Yahweh. His first centers are Babel, Erech, Akkad, and Calneh in Shinar. The next sentence describes the building of Nineveh, Rehoboth-Ir, Calah, and Resen. Its wording permits dispute over whether Nimrod goes to Assyria to build them or whether Asshur is the named builder. The passage associates the rise of major cities with the emergence of exceptional rulers.

1 Chr 1:10 repeats Nimrod's connection with Cush and his standing as a mighty figure. Mic 5:6 (MT 5:5) places the land of Nimrod in parallel with Assyria, making the name a regional designation. The Hebrew term *gibbôr* in Genesis also occurs in the account of the mighty figures of old in Gen 6:4. It links the diction of the two passages without defining a single ancestry for their characters.

## Interpretive Significance

The modern name [[nimrud|Nimrud]] for ancient Kalhu, biblical Calah, reflects a later naming tradition. Scholars use the city's ancient name and inscriptions to reconstruct its history. Later Jewish interpretation supplied a fuller account of Nimrod's conduct. Josephus makes him the ruler who promoted the building of the tower of Babel and encouraged defiance of God (Ant. 1.113–119). Genesis introduces Nimrod in the Table of Nations and leaves the builders of the tower unnamed in the next chapter. Josephus's retelling connects the two accounts and gives the collective undertaking a royal instigator.
