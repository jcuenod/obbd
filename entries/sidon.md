## Overview

Sidon was a major port on the coast of modern Lebanon. Excavations document occupation and burial customs in the Bronze Age, and Assyrian inscriptions record conquest and rebuilding in the Iron Age. The city later prospered under Persian rule, when its kings left important Phoenician inscriptions. The Bible uses Sidon both for the city and as a designation with broader Phoenician associations. Its history helps explain references to timber merchants, royal marriages, prophetic judgments, and travel along the coast in the New Testament.

## Location and Identification

Ancient Sidon lay at modern Saida on the Mediterranean coast, north of [[tyre|Tyre]]. Its name survives in the modern city, and inscriptions and ancient itineraries support the identification. Its position made it a center of coastal travel and trade. “Sidonians” could designate inhabitants of the city and in some literary settings seems to refer more generally to [[phoenicians|Phoenicians]] [@kai] [@abd].

## Archaeological History

Excavations at the College Site under Claude Doumet-Serhal uncovered Bronze Age burials and evidence of settlement and ritual activity. The Bronze Age results help show the depth of occupation beneath the later city. In 701 BCE, Sennacherib claimed that Lulî of Sidon fled before him. Esarhaddon later destroyed Sidon around 677 BCE and founded a new Assyrian administrative center called Kār-Esarhaddon. His report celebrates the victory in Assyrian royal terms [@rinap3-1] [@rinap4].

Persian-period Sidon had a royal dynasty known from Phoenician inscriptions, including those on the sarcophagi of Tabnit and [[eshmunazar-sarcophagus|Eshmunazar II]]. Inscriptions of Bodashtart at the sanctuary of Eshmun at Bustan esh-Sheikh document further building by Sidonian kings. The inscriptions show rulers restoring sanctuaries and claiming close relations with their gods and patrons [@kai] [@cos2].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 10:15, 19 places Sidon among the peoples and geographical boundaries associated with Canaan. Judg 18:7, 28 describes Laish as living in the manner of the Sidonians and points to the distance separating it from them. 1 Kgs 5:6 (MT 5:20) associates the Sidonians with expertise in cutting timber. These notices concern a coastal network and its links inland.

1 Kgs 16:31 makes [[jezebel|Jezebel]] the daughter of Ethbaal, king of the Sidonians. Josephus's much later account associates a king of this name with Tyre, consistent with the wider use of the designation. 1 Kgs 17:9 places Zarephath in Sidon's territory. Isa 23:2–4, 12 links Sidon with merchants and trade, and Ezek 28:20–23 addresses the city in an oracle of judgment.

Matt 15:21 places an episode of Jesus' ministry in the district of Tyre and Sidon. Acts 27:3 describes a stop at Sidon on Paul's voyage, where he is allowed to visit friends. These notices fit the continuing place of the city in the coastal routes of the eastern Mediterranean.
