## Overview

A Greek inscription from Priene in western Asia Minor preserves documents from 9 BCE concerning a new provincial calendar. The reform made Augustus’s birthday the start of the year, and the accompanying praise hailed his birth as the beginning of good news for the world. Other copies show that the decision circulated beyond Priene. The decree gives a public imperial example of terms also found in the New Testament. Those terms had several possible associations, including the language of promise and restoration in Isaiah.

## Description and Provenance

The Priene text survives on fragments of an inscription in Greek. The document combines a proposal by the Roman proconsul Paullus Fabius Maximus with action by the provincial council of Asia. Copies or fragments from other cities, including Apameia and Eumeneia, attest provincial circulation. Comparison among witnesses helps editors reconstruct parts of the document. ## Contents

The reform linked the beginning of the year to the birthday of Augustus, 23 September. The text treats changes in time reckoning as an appropriate way to express gratitude. Its praise credits providence with sending a deliverer who ended war and brought order. It calls Augustus a savior and speaks of the beginning of *euangelia*, a plural noun meaning “good news” or “good tidings.” The Greek term already had ordinary uses for favorable announcements. Here its force comes from being attached to an emperor’s birth in an official civic and provincial document.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Mark 1:1 uses the singular form of the word for good news in introducing Jesus. Luke 2:10–11 uses the corresponding verb in announcing a birth and gives the child the title Savior. Luke 2:14 joins the scene to peace, while Phil 3:20 speaks of awaiting a Savior from heaven. Titus 2:13 also uses the language of salvation and divine glory. Provincial honors for emperors show that these terms could carry public associations with rulership, deliverance, and peace. The New Testament passages apply the words within different narratives and arguments.

Isa 52:7 portrays a messenger announcing good news, peace, and God’s reign. Isa 40:9 and 61:1 supply related prophetic contexts. This scriptural language gives the New Testament writers another readily available vocabulary, with strong connections to their direct use of Israel’s Scriptures. The inscription and Isaiah clarify different associations that a hearer might recognize in the same Greek words.

## Scholarly Issues

Some readings emphasize the counter-imperial implications of assigning to Jesus titles and achievements used to honor Augustus. Others give priority to biblical traditions and doubt that individual passages deliberately echo the calendar decree. The decree makes the overlap in language historically visible, especially in regions where public honors for emperors were familiar. Shared words alone cannot show that any particular writer knew this version of the decree or wrote a coded response to it.
