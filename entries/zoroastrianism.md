## Overview

Zoroastrianism is the Iranian religious tradition associated with Zarathustra and the worship of Ahura Mazda. Its early history is difficult to date. Achaemenid royal inscriptions invoke Ahuramazda, while the sacred texts known as the Avesta were transmitted for centuries and took a collected form much later. Contact under Persian rule provides a setting for considering Iranian influence on Jewish thought. Proposed parallels include opposing spiritual powers, resurrection and final judgment, with stronger evidence for particular words and names than for broad doctrinal borrowing.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The Gathas, the oldest Avestan hymns, are traditionally associated with Zarathustra. Linguistic and historical arguments place him at widely different dates, from the late second or early first millennium BCE to the seventh or sixth century BCE. The Avestan materials were transmitted orally and edited in the Sasanian period. Major surviving expositions in Middle Persian, or Pahlavi, were compiled much later, many in the ninth and tenth centuries CE. Their preservation of older material makes them valuable while leaving the date of particular teachings open.

Darius and [[xerxes-i|Xerxes]] invoke Ahuramazda in their royal inscriptions. The [[behistun-inscription|Behistun Inscription]] credits the god with helping Darius prevail over rebels. Xerxes's XPh text rejects the worship of beings called *daivā*. Whether to call these rulers Zoroastrian depends partly on how the tradition is defined. Their inscriptions do not name Zarathustra, and the evidence for other cults under Achaemenid rule gives a more varied picture of the empire. [@tad, C2.1] [@abd, 6:1168–1174]

## In the Hebrew Bible

Isa 45:7 presents YHWH as the author of light, darkness, well-being and calamity. Some have read this as a response to Iranian dualism in the context of Persian rule. It also fits the passage's claim that YHWH alone directs events. Dan 10:13, 20–21 depicts angelic princes associated with nations, and Dan 12:2–3 anticipates resurrection with different outcomes for the dead. Both ideas have been compared with Iranian traditions. 1 Chr 21:1 gives an adversary the role of inciting David to take a census, where 2 Sam 24:1 attributes the incitement to YHWH's anger. The change has figured in discussions about the development of [[satan-the-adversary|Satan]]. [@abd, 6:1168–1174] [@ddd]

## Interpretive Significance

The name Asmodeus in Tob 3:8, 17 is commonly linked to the Iranian demon Aēšma and the term *daēva*. This is more concrete evidence of contact than general similarities in stories of evil or the afterlife. The *magoi* in Matt 2:1–12 are visitors from the east. Their title has Iranian associations and acquired broader Greek uses, leaving the visitors' specific affiliation uncertain. [@ddd, 106–108] [@abd, 6:1168–1174]

The Persian period allowed prolonged contact, and renewed contact and literary exchange continued in the Hellenistic period. A transmission argument requires evidence that a particular Iranian doctrine was current early enough and that it reached the relevant community. Development within Jewish literature and other regional traditions may account for some similarities. Reconstructions involving cosmic dualism, schemes of world ages and [[afterlife-and-resurrection|resurrection]] therefore vary in probability. [@abd, 6:1168–1174] [@ddd]
