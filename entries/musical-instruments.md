## Overview

Musical instruments in the Hebrew Bible include stringed instruments, pipes, frame drums, horns, trumpets, and cymbals. They accompany worship, prophetic performance, celebration, and the movement of armies or assemblies. Archaeological finds and images from the Levant, Egypt, and Mesopotamia help identify their shapes and uses. Some terms, such as the name of David's stringed instrument, can be identified with reasonable confidence. The names of other instruments have several plausible meanings, and familiar modern translation terms sometimes suggest shapes or playing techniques that remain uncertain.

## Archaeological Evidence

Ancient pictures show both lyres and harps. A lyre has strings stretched to a crossbar held between two arms, while harps have another arrangement of strings and frame. The Hebrew *kinnôr*, related to Ugaritic *knr*, is usually identified as a lyre. The *nēbel* may have been a larger lyre or a form of harp. A carved ivory from Megiddo and a drawing on a jar at [[kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet ʿAjrud]] depict lyre players, providing evidence for the instrument in different artistic settings.

The *tōp* is commonly understood as a frame drum held in the hands. Iron Age terracotta figurines depicting women with round objects are often understood as drummers, though individual examples can be ambiguous. Other images document wind and percussion instruments. Assyrian reliefs also depict musicians in scenes of conquest and deportation. [[sennacherib-annals|Sennacherib's annals]] list male and female singers among the people handed over by Hezekiah, evidence that musical performers could belong to a royal household.

## In the Bible

Gen 4:21 calls Jubal the ancestor of lyre and pipe players, and Miriam and the women play frame drums and dance after the crossing of the sea (Exod 15:20). In 1 Sam 10:5 several instruments accompany a prophetic band. David's lyre playing relieves Saul's distress in 1 Sam 16:23. Instruments also accompany the movement of the ark in 2 Sam 6:5. The range of scenes shows that music belonged in religious and court settings as well as public celebrations.

The silver trumpets of Num 10:1–10 are used to call assemblies, signal movement, sound an alarm, and mark festivals. The horn in Josh 6:4 is a *šôpār*, conventionally associated with an animal horn. Ps 81:2–3 (MT 81:3–4) links the drum, lyre, and another stringed instrument with festival music and calls for sounding the horn at the [[new-moon|new moon]]. Ps 150 invokes strings, pipes, drums, horns, and cymbals as instruments of praise.

The Aramaic list in Dan 3:5 includes words commonly identified as Greek loans, such as *qîtārōs*, *pĕsantērîn*, and *sûmpōnyâ*. Particular identifications of instrument types and the meaning of the last word are debated. Some scholars take the vocabulary as evidence of a Hellenistic setting for the tale's present form. Musical terms and instruments could travel through contact before the Macedonian conquests, so the vocabulary contributes to the discussion alongside other evidence.
