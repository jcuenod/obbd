## Overview

The inscriptions of Tiglath-pileser III record Assyrian campaigns, tribute, and changes in the political organization of the Levant. They survive on palace slabs, tablets, and stelae, many in a fragmentary state. They name Menahem and other kings of Israel, Rezin of Damascus, and a king of Judah whose full name corresponds to Ahaz. The accounts provide independent evidence for the Assyrian pressure described in Kings, although they represent an imperial viewpoint and several important lines survive only in part.

## Manuscripts and Date

The annals were inscribed on slabs at the palace in Kalhu, modern [[nimrud|Nimrud]]. Some slabs were removed and reused in the time of Esarhaddon. Many pieces have been lost or damaged, and in some places nineteenth-century copies by excavators preserve evidence no longer visible on the stones. Hayim Tadmor reconstructed the arrangement of the surviving passages using these copies, fragments, and parallel texts. Separate inscriptions summarize campaigns and accomplishments in a different order.

## Contents

The Iran Stele preserves an account of the king's successes and lists western rulers who brought tribute. Among them are Rezin of Damascus and Menahem of Samaria. Its chronological setting helps place Menahem's tribute around 738 BCE. Other inscriptions summarize Assyrian actions against Samaria and Damascus. The wording about some events is damaged, so editions identify where signs or names have been restored.

Summary Inscription 7 lists Jehoahaz of Judah, usually understood as the full form of [[ahaz|Ahaz]]. Another summary inscription describes Pekah's fall and credits [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser]] with installing Hoshea. These texts present the Assyrian king as the effective source of regional political order. They also record [[deportation|deportations]] and transfers of territory as elements of conquest.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 15:19–20 says Menahem pays tribute to Pul to secure his rule. The Assyrian evidence places this action among submissions by several western kings and connects Pul with Tiglath-pileser. 2 Kgs 15:29 describes Assyrian occupation in Galilee and Gilead and the deportation of inhabitants. 1 Chr 5:6, 26 remembers the removal of people from areas east of the Jordan. Together the inscriptions and biblical accounts attest changes in control and population during the campaigns.

2 Kgs 15:30 emphasizes Hoshea's action against Pekah, while the Assyrian account emphasizes the king of Assyria's part in Hoshea's accession. 2 Kgs 16:5–9 describes Ahaz sending a payment and seeking Assyrian help amid pressure from Damascus and Israel. Isa 7:1–9 presents that crisis from a prophetic perspective. In 2 Kgs 16:10–16 Ahaz meets Tiglath-pileser at Damascus, and the narrative connects the visit with construction of a new altar in Jerusalem.

## Scholarly Issues

A common proposal relates the geographical expressions of Isa 9:1 (MT 8:23) to Assyrian divisions of the annexed territories, including Dor, Megiddo, and Gilead. The association is plausible in the context of occupation, although the verse supplies geographic descriptions and leaves their administrative meaning open. Assyrian annals can illuminate the political setting of such passages. Their choice of details and emphasis on the king's agency are part of their purpose as royal inscriptions.
