## Overview

Kuntillet ʿAjrud is a small Iron Age site in northeastern Sinai occupied around 800 BCE. Its buildings stood by a desert travel route, and finds from the site connect it with the northern kingdom of Israel despite its distance from Samaria. Inscriptions and drawings survived on large storage jars and on fragments of wall plaster. Whether the buildings served chiefly as a stopping place for travelers, an outpost, or a religious center remains debated. The site gives biblical readers evidence for how people outside Israel's main towns spoke of YHWH, the God of Israel.

## Location and Identification

The site lies on a hill near a source of water, within reach of a route between the Gulf of Aqaba and the Mediterranean coast. In a dry region, access to water helps explain why a small settlement might have served people traveling along [[roads-and-highways|long-distance routes]]. Kuntillet ʿAjrud and Horvat Teman are modern names for the ruins. The latter recalls Teman, a name in the inscriptions, but those texts do not establish that Teman was the ancient name of the site.

## Archaeological History

Ze'ev Meshel led excavations in 1975 and 1976, and the final report appeared in 2012. The main buildings and finds are usually treated as evidence of one principal period of occupation. Radiocarbon dating, which estimates the age of organic material, places that occupation around 800 BCE. The date and the northern connections fit the general era of Joash and Jeroboam II of Israel, but they do not show that either king founded the station.

The main building contained rooms with low benches near its entrance. Fragments of large storage jars, called pithoi, were found in this entrance area, including jars that bore writing and drawings. The finds show that the people who used the building had access to written blessings, but do not show what took place in the bench rooms. The setting and layout favor a way station or an official outpost in many reconstructions, with religious activity possible in either case. The religious material is clear, but the remains do not securely identify the building as a temple.

The jar drawings include standing figures often compared to Bes, an Egyptian protective deity, as well as a cow nursing a calf and a seated musician playing a lyre, a stringed instrument. Pictures and words on the same jar need not have been made together or intended to explain one another. In particular, the seated musician cannot be securely identified as the goddess [[asherah|Asherah]]. The separate article on the [[kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions]] treats the wording and the relationships between texts and drawings in more detail [@keel-uehlinger-ggig].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Some of the pottery, features of the writing, and a blessing invoking YHWH of Samaria point to links with the kingdom of Israel. The Samaria reference and another blessing invoking YHWH of Teman show that people could associate the divine name with more than one place. Amos 8:14 likewise connects religious oaths with named places, although the prophet condemns those oaths and does not use the site's formulas. These comparisons do not establish who governed the station or where its visitors came from.

Hab 3:3 pictures God coming from Teman and Mount Paran; Deut 33:2 and Judg 5:4 likewise portray YHWH coming from southern regions. The name Teman in a blessing from a southern site makes the comparison worth considering, but a place associated with YHWH in a blessing need not be the place from which he travels in a poem. The site is relevant to the debate over [[yhwh-origins|the origins of YHWH worship]], but cannot settle it. Deut 16:21 forbids planting an asherah, a tree or wooden object used in worship, beside YHWH's altar. Its connection with the blessings is disputed: the meaning of the term there is debated, and the finds do not establish that such an object stood beside an altar at the site.
