## Overview

Tiglath-pileser III ruled Assyria in the eighth century BCE and greatly enlarged its territory. He brought some conquered lands under provincial administration and kept other kingdoms under tribute. His campaigns changed the political map of Israel, Judah, and neighboring Damascus. The Bible names him both as Tiglath-pileser and as Pul, the name used for his rule in Babylonia. Assyrian inscriptions give an independent account of his interventions, including tribute from Israel and Judah and the installation of Hoshea after Pekah's fall.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Tiglath-pileser came to the Assyrian throne in 745 BCE, with the first full regnal year often counted as 744, and ruled until 727 BCE. The [[tiglath-pileser-iii-inscriptions|royal inscriptions]] describe campaigns across the Near East and the imposition of tribute on many rulers. Babylonia was among his concerns, and late in his reign he took its throne. Babylonian King List A gives the name Pulu for the king who corresponds chronologically to Tiglath-pileser. The Ptolemaic Canon uses the form Poros.

His reign consolidated Assyrian methods of control. Conquered lands could become provinces under governors, while client kings were retained where it suited the empire. The army drew on established Assyrian units and people recruited from conquered territories. [[deportation|Population transfers]] removed people from their home districts and settled others elsewhere. Several of these methods predated the king, who used them on a substantial scale.

## Biblical Portrayal

In 2 Kgs 15:19–20, Pul receives silver from [[menahem|Menahem]] in return for support. The Assyrian inscriptions also name Menahem of Samaria as a tributary. 2 Kgs 15:29 describes the seizure of territory in Galilee and Gilead and the deportation of residents. 1 Chr 5:6, 26 remembers deportations among groups living east of the Jordan. Its wording at 5:26 can give the appearance of distinguishing Pul from Tiglath-pileser.

2 Kgs 16:7–10 portrays [[ahaz|Ahaz]] seeking the Assyrian king's help against Damascus and Israel. Assyria takes Damascus, ends [[rezin|Rezin's]] rule, and becomes Judah's overlord. 2 Chr 28:16–21 also describes Ahaz's appeal but emphasizes that Assyria brought distress rather than effective support. Isa 7:17–20 compares Assyria to a hired razor from beyond the Euphrates, an image that captures the danger of relying on a powerful state.

## Historical Assessment

2 Kgs 15:30 attributes the overthrow of Pekah to [[hoshea|Hoshea]]. Tiglath-pileser's records credit the Assyrian king with placing Hoshea on the throne. A local conspiracy followed by imperial recognition would account for the two emphases. The precise arrangements are less clear than the outcome. Northern Israel lost territories that Assyria governed directly, while a reduced kingdom centered on Samaria lasted into the reign of Tiglath-pileser's successors.

These developments help explain the difference between effects on Israel and Judah. Israel lost large areas and inhabitants in the campaigns, while Judah retained its monarchy under Assyrian authority. Tribute, political intervention, and provincial government could operate side by side.
