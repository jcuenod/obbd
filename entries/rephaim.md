## Overview

The Rephaim are a group of the dead in West Semitic ritual and funerary texts and in several biblical poems. The Hebrew Bible also uses the name for peoples remembered as unusually tall inhabitants of Canaan and Transjordan. Late Bronze Age texts from Ugarit portray figures with a related name as banquet participants and recipients of royal funerary rites. Phoenician tomb inscriptions later mention the Rephaim as a company in which the dead might rest. The shared name and the geography of Bashan raise the question of how these traditions are related.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The fragmentary Rephaim texts from [[ugaritic-texts|Ugarit]] (KTU 1.20–1.22) describe a group traveling in chariots and assembling in a setting of feasting. The fragments leave room to interpret them as supernatural figures, royal ancestors or warriors honored as a group. A funerary ritual (KTU 1.161) calls on ancient Rephaim and deceased kings of Ugarit in connection with the funeral of a king. This is strong evidence for ties to the royal dead, although interpretations of the precise status of each group differ. [@ktu3, 1.20–1.22, 1.161] [@cos1, 1.105]

A separate Ugaritic text (KTU 1.108) invokes Rapiʾu, who is described in royal terms and associated with place names often identified as Ashtaroth and Edrei. The much later Phoenician curses of Tabnit and [[eshmunazar-sarcophagus|Eshmunazar II]] of Sidon use the plural for the company of the dead. The inscriptions deny a tomb violator a resting place with the Rephaim. [@ktu3, 1.108] [@kai, nos. 13–14]

## In the Hebrew Bible

The shades who greet the fallen ruler in Isa 14:9 emerge in [[sheol-and-the-netherworld|Sheol]] and include former kings. Job 26:5 depicts the Rephaim trembling beneath the waters, and Ps 88:10 (MT 88:11) asks whether they can rise to praise God. In Prov 2:18, 9:18 and 21:16 they are the company awaiting those who take a ruinous course. Isa 26:14 and 26:19 put two outcomes in tension. The defeated oppressors' shades do not rise, while the dead addressed in the promise are called to rise and rejoice. [@ddd]

Deut 2:10–11, 20–21 applies Rephaim to earlier peoples called Emim by Moabites and Zamzummim by Ammonites, comparing their stature to the Anakim. Deut 3:11, 13 names Og as a survivor of the Rephaim and identifies Bashan with them. Josh 12:4 puts Og at Ashtaroth and Edrei. The term *hārāpāh* for the ancestry of notable fighters at Gath in 2 Sam 21:15–22 may be connected, though its form and interpretation are debated. These traditions portray formidable former inhabitants and warriors. Their stature belongs to that portrayal. [@ddd]

## Interpretive Significance

The parallel place names in Josh 12:4 and KTU 1.108 make a connection with a local tradition of royal ancestors plausible. The surviving texts leave the process by which a tradition of the royal dead might have become a tradition about ancient giant peoples open. Deriving the name from a root meaning “heal” would require explaining its use for ancestors and shades. Proposals include the ritual power of the honored dead, but the etymology and its implications remain disputed. [@ddd] [@ktu3, 1.108]
