## Overview

Biblical stories portray hospitality as providing travelers and strangers with food, water, shelter, and protection. The host may offer a meal or lodging before discovering the visitor's identity. Ancient Egyptian and Ugaritic narratives also use hospitality to establish relations between strangers or between humans and gods. Later Greco-Roman stories offer another comparison for unseen divine visitors. The biblical evidence ranges from generous welcomes to violence against strangers. It helps readers recognize both the obligations a host assumed and how narratives judged communities by the way they received vulnerable people.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the Ugaritic [[aqhat-epic|Aqhat epic]], Danel and his household receive the craftsman god Kothar-wa-Ḫasis and provide a meal. The episode gives a close Levantine example of hosts serving a divine visitor. In the Egyptian [[story-of-sinuhe|Story of Sinuhe]], a ruler in the Levant receives and provides for the Egyptian fugitive. The texts represent hospitality in different literary settings. A welcome can establish favor and create an obligation that extends beyond a meal [@parker-unp, 58–60] [@ael, 1:222–235].

Greeks described reciprocal guest-friendship with the term *xenia*. Greek and Roman stories also tell of gods visiting people in disguise to test their welcome. In Ovid's account of Philemon and Baucis, a couple receives visitors who turn out to be gods. Such stories are later comparanda for Hebrews. They illustrate the wider literary pattern of divine visitors whose identity becomes clear only after they have been received.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Abraham gives his three visitors water for their feet, rest, bread, meat, and dairy food in Gen 18:1–8. Laban offers lodging and provisions for Abraham's servant, his companions, and his animals in Gen 24:31–33. In Exod 2:20, Reuel asks why his daughters left Moses behind and tells them to invite him to eat. These details show that a welcome typically involved access to household resources and personal attention from the host.

Longer relationships could grow from that first offer. In 2 Kgs 4:8–10 the Shunammite woman makes provision for Elisha's recurring visits, including a room and furnishings. Job 31:32 includes keeping strangers off the street among the practices that establish Job's integrity. Hospitality overlaps here with concern for the [[widow-orphan-sojourner|vulnerable outsider]], although laws on resident foreigners address relationships beyond those of visitor and host.

Gen 19:1–11 and Judg 19:15–25 depict communities threatening visitors and hosts trying to protect them. Both portray the danger the hosts shift toward women, and Judges shows the lethal failure of the protection offered. Ezek 16:49 sees Sodom's guilt in pride and neglect of the poor and needy. Wis 19:13–17 associates the story with hatred of strangers and compares the treatment of outsiders with that of the Israelites in Egypt. These readings show how later writers drew attention to the behavior of the community.

## Interpretive Significance

Heb 13:2 calls for hospitality because some have unknowingly received angels. Genesis supplies a likely biblical background, while Greco-Roman tales show how readily the motif could be understood. Welcoming a stranger exposes a household to uncertainty and gives it an opportunity to provide protection. Modern ethnographic parallels may raise questions about these obligations, but the literary and historical settings of the ancient sources give the primary evidence for their meaning.
