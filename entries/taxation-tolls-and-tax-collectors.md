## Overview

Ancient taxes included direct levies on people or property and indirect charges on goods in transit or exchange. Collecting them could involve imperial officials, local authorities, agents, or contractors, depending on the place and levy. Tax receipts and customs regulations show the practical distinctions behind the broad vocabulary of revenue in the Gospels and Romans. They also help explain why collectors could be associated both with routine administration and with concerns about excessive demands.

## Greco-Roman Evidence

A customs inscription from Ephesus assembles regulations for the Roman province of Asia. It identifies places and circumstances in which charges could be collected and provides evidence for the administration of duties on goods. An Egyptian papyrus from 30 CE records payments through bankers for a poll tax and two other levies. These sources illustrate different revenue streams. Their rules and rates are specific to their provinces [@cottier-customs-asia, 16–86] [@oslo-1457].

Tacitus describes complaints about tax contractors under Nero and a debate over abolishing indirect taxes. The decision he reports regulated their collection rather than eliminating them. The account illustrates both the scope for abuse and the state's dependence on revenue [@tacitus-annals, 13.50–51].

## In the Bible

Luke 3:12–13 presents collectors asking John what they should do. His reply assumes they have assigned amounts to collect and tells them to take no more. In Mark 2:14–17 Levi sits at a collection station, a setting consistent with tolls or customs. In Luke 19:1–10 Zacchaeus is described as a chief tax collector, and his offer of restitution responds to the possibility of having taken money by false accusation. The texts give little detail about the specific revenues or contracts assigned to these men.

The tax question in Mark 12:13–17 concerns payment to Caesar and is posed with an imperial coin. In Rom 13:6–7 Paul distinguishes kinds of dues and calls for payment of what is owed. These passages concern obligations to authorities beyond the conduct of individual collectors.

## Interpretive Significance

Direct taxation, tolls, and temple payments had different grounds and may have involved different administrators. Political control also differed between territories such as Judaea and Galilee. The evidence of particular documents can clarify how collection worked without assigning their local rates to the Gospel settings. See [[census-of-quirinius]], [[coinage]], and [[tyrian-shekel]] for related aspects of population registration and payment.
