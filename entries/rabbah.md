## Overview

Rabbah was the capital of ancient Ammon, on the citadel hill of modern Amman in Jordan. Inscriptions, fortifications and stone sculpture give evidence for the city and its ruling elites in the Iron Age. Later Hellenistic and Roman construction transformed the site under the name Philadelphia. The Hebrew Bible presents Rabbah as a fortified royal center, a goal of military campaigns and the subject of prophetic judgment. The remains help explain the importance of the city even though much of its earlier plan is unknown.

## Location and Identification

The ancient center occupied Jebel al-Qalʿa, a hill that commands the surrounding area of modern Amman. The name Rabbah means “great,” and texts can distinguish this place as Rabbah of the Ammonites. The citadel and levels on its slopes preserve traces of several periods. In the Hellenistic era Ptolemy II Philadelphus is credited with renewing the city as Philadelphia. In the Roman period it was associated with the cities of the Decapolis. [@neaehl, 4:1243–1252] [@abd, 5:599–600]

## Archaeological History

Later Roman, Byzantine and Umayyad buildings obscure much of the [[ammonites|Ammonite]] city, but sections of Iron Age walls and substantial buildings have been identified. A fragmentary inscription from the citadel, commonly assigned to the ninth century BCE, mentions [[milkom|Milkom]] and appears to concern building or protection. Its damaged text permits different reconstructions of the project. Other inscriptions from the wider Amman area give names and titles that help situate the city within the kingdom. [@cos2, 2.24] [@kai, nos. 307–308] [@neaehl, 4:1243–1252]

Stone figures from Amman and nearby sites include examples wearing tall crowns derived from the Egyptian *atef* crown. Such imagery could be adapted to royal, ancestral or divine figures. The sculptures attest regional participation in wider artistic traditions. Interpretations of particular figures depend on the details of their attributes, findspots and inscriptions. [@neaehl, 4:1243–1252] [@anep]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Deut 3:11 places Og’s iron bed or couch at Rabbah as a memorable object linked with his size. Proposals to identify it as a basalt sarcophagus or a large stone tomb remain speculative. In 2 Sam 11:1, Joab besieges Rabbah. 2 Sam 12:26–31 records the capture of the city and refers to a city of waters. Some interpreters distinguish a lower area controlling water from the defended upper city. The text leaves their exact location open. The crown David takes is variously understood as that of the Ammonite king or of Milkom. 2 Sam 17:27 provides a different account of relations with the city, through Shobi’s support for David. [@abd, 5:599–600] [@neaehl, 4:1243–1252]

Jer 49:2–3 and Amos 1:14 predict devastation for Rabbah. Ezek 21:20 (MT 21:25) places it on one of two routes considered by the Babylonian king, and Ezek 25:5 imagines it as a place for camels to graze. These accounts assume that Rabbah was a well-known political center with walls and strategic importance. They do not give a continuous record of any single campaign. [@abd, 5:599–600]
