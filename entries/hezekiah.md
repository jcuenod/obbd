## Overview

Hezekiah, son of Ahaz, ruled Judah in the late eighth and early seventh centuries BCE. A seal impression from Jerusalem names him as king, and Assyrian inscriptions describe Sennacherib’s campaign against him in 701 BCE. Kings, Isaiah, and Chronicles tell of Judah’s losses, Jerusalem’s survival, and work on the city’s defenses and water supply. The Assyrian sources and Jerusalem excavations illuminate the crisis from different perspectives. The chronology of Hezekiah’s accession and the material evidence for his religious reforms remain debated.

## Extrabiblical Sources

Assyrian accounts of the 701 BCE campaign describe [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] taking forty-six fortified Judahite towns and deporting 200,150 people. The figures belong to the royal account, with no surviving independent count. Hezekiah is said to have been confined in Jerusalem like a bird in a cage. Sennacherib claims thirty talents of gold and eight hundred of silver among the tribute sent to him. His inscriptions claim substantial gains and submission. They do not claim to have captured Jerusalem.

The [[lachish-reliefs|Lachish reliefs]] from Sennacherib’s palace at Nineveh depict a major Assyrian victory and identify the site in writing. Excavations at Lachish document a large destruction at the end of Level III. In Jerusalem the [[hezekiah-bulla|Ophel bulla]] attests Hezekiah’s name and title in a controlled archaeological context. Stamped storage jars with the [[lmlk-seal-impressions|lmlk device]] document royal administration in a period that included preparations for the Assyrian conflict. Their distribution and use extend beyond what a single campaign account can explain.

## Biblical Portrayal

Second Kings 18:13–16 reports Assyria’s capture of fortified towns and says that Hezekiah paid thirty talents of gold and three hundred of silver. Kings and Assyrian records agree on the gold amount and differ on silver. The narrative in 2 Kgs 18:17–19:37, closely paralleled by Isa 36–37, concentrates on the Assyrian demands, the response of Hezekiah and Isaiah, and Jerusalem’s deliverance. The two national traditions agree that the city remained in Judahite hands and give different explanations of how the episode ended.

Second Kings 20:20 ascribes a pool and conduit to Hezekiah. Second Chronicles 32:2–5, 30 describes work on springs, walls, and a diversion of the Gihon’s water. Isa 22:9–11 remembers efforts to secure water and strengthen the city, while criticizing reliance on those measures. The broad wall on Jerusalem’s western hill and [[hezekiahs-tunnel|the Siloam Tunnel]] are commonly related to such projects. Their structures give independent evidence of construction. Neither bears an inscription that names Hezekiah as its builder.

Second Kings 18:4 attributes the removal of cultic sites and objects to Hezekiah, including destruction of the bronze serpent. Second Kings 20:12–19 describes the visit of envoys from the Babylonian ruler Merodach-baladan and Isaiah’s response. Prov 25:1 ascribes the copying of a collection of proverbs to Hezekiah’s men, giving him a place in Israel’s memory of scribal activity. These passages make claims about different aspects of his reign and have different kinds of external support.

## Historical Assessment

The 701 BCE campaign supplies the firmest chronological anchor. Taking the fourteenth year in 2 Kgs 18:13 as Hezekiah’s regnal year puts his accession around 715 BCE. The synchronisms with Hoshea in 2 Kgs 18:1, 9–10 suggest a start earlier, around 727 BCE. Proposed solutions include a period of shared rule with Ahaz and problems in the transmission or arrangement of the regnal figures. The chronology here uses the later date for the start of his sole reign as a working convention.

The archaeological [[cult-reforms-archaeology|evidence for cult reforms]] includes changes at the Arad sanctuary, the dismantled altar at Beersheba, and the debated shrine evidence at Lachish. Dating individual changes and identifying their causes remain difficult. A centralized decision under Hezekiah is one explanation, and local political, military, or ritual changes are also possible.
