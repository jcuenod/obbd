## Overview

The temple at ʿAin Dara stood on a mound in the Afrin valley of northwestern Syria. Built and altered between the late second millennium and the Iron Age on the excavator's chronology, it had a columned porch, two rooms on the same axis, and stone reliefs of lions and winged creatures. Oversized footprints carved into its thresholds suggest the entry of a deity. The building provides a close comparison for the description of Solomon's temple, while its own layout, sculptures, and signs of divine presence also show the variety of temple building in the region.

## Location and Identification

Tell ʿAin Dara lies on the east side of the Afrin River, northwest of Aleppo. Its temple stood on the upper mound. A porch with two column bases led to a hall and then to the inner sanctuary. Basalt slabs bearing carved lions and sphinxes, winged creatures with lion bodies, lined parts of the building. No secure dedication identifies its deity. The goddess Ishtar, known in Hurrian tradition as Šaušga, and a storm god have both been proposed, partly from the carved figures and comparisons with other sanctuaries.

## Archaeological History

Ali Abou Assaf's excavations uncovered the building and led him to propose three phases, beginning around 1300 BCE and ending in the eighth century BCE. These dates are approximate. The chronology of the earliest building and of particular sculptures remains debated. A passage around the exterior belonged to a later phase of the temple. The building's history matters for comparison because the features did not necessarily come into use at the same time.

Aerial bombardment in January 2018 severely damaged the temple, including carved stones near the front. A separate colossal basalt lion at the site was subsequently reported stolen. Conservation teams documented losses, collected damaged fragments, and reported further risks to the surviving walls and stonework after the February 2023 earthquake. The conservation record is now important evidence alongside the photographs and plans made before the bombardment.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The columned entrance and sequence of rooms recall the porch, hall, and inner sanctuary of [[solomons-temple|Solomon's temple]] in 1 Kgs 6:2–3, 14–22. Nearby [[tell-tayinat|Tell Tayinat]] offers another example of a temple arranged along one axis. The two temple columns at ʿAin Dara help place [[jachin-and-boaz|Jachin and Boaz]] in an architectural setting (1 Kgs 7:21), though the function of the Jerusalem pillars remains debated. The Syrian exterior passage and the stacked side rooms in 1 Kgs 6:5–10 also differ in construction and use. The carved figures at ʿAin Dara provide a regional setting for the biblical account of carved [[cherubim]] and plants (1 Kgs 6:29, 32). [@hurowitz-exalted-house] [@keel-symbolism]

An oversized pair of carved feet marks the entrance, and two single impressions farther inside suggest successive steps toward the sanctuary. John Monson and others have read the carvings as an image of a deity entering the building. In Ezek 43:7, God calls the sanctuary the place of his throne and the soles of his feet. The carved thresholds give material form to a comparable idea, that a god takes up residence in a temple. The comparison shows how the language could be understood while leaving the practices of the two sanctuaries distinct.
