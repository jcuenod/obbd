## Overview

The cherub throne is a way of understanding biblical descriptions of Yahweh enthroned above or among winged beings called cherubim. Levantine art from the Late Bronze and Iron Ages portrays earthly rulers on seats flanked by winged creatures. Those images help explain why biblical texts associate cherubim with Yahweh's kingship and why the Jerusalem temple account places the ark beneath their outstretched wings. Scholars debate whether the throne was understood as an empty seat for an unseen deity and how the image became associated with Israel's sanctuaries.

## Archaeological Evidence

A carved ivory from [[megiddo|Megiddo]] shows an enthroned figure on a seat with a winged sphinx at its side. A sphinx of this kind combines a lion's body, wings and a human head. The relief on the [[ahiram-sarcophagus|sarcophagus of Ahiram]] from Byblos gives another example of a ruler seated on a sphinx throne. These images show how the guardians and the seat could be combined in a single object. They provide a regional model for picturing the biblical throne. [@anep, figs. 125, 129–130] [@keel-symbolism, 168–171] [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 94–95, 167–169]

The biblical [[cherubim]] are often compared with these winged sphinxes. The comparison is especially close when the figures stand beside or beneath a royal person or throne. Other biblical scenes give cherubim forms and duties suited to their own settings. The images are therefore evidence for a visual convention available to Israelite artists and writers. [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 155–170]

## In the Bible

In 1 Sam 4:4, the title translated as sitting above the cherubim belongs to Yahweh of Armies, whose ark is brought from Shiloh. Second Samuel uses the same language when David sets out to bring the ark from Baale Judah (2 Sam 6:2). Ps 99:1 associates the throne with Yahweh's universal rule, while Ps 80:1 (MT 80:2) calls on the one enthroned over the cherubim to come to Israel's aid. Whether the expression arose in worship at Shiloh or reflects Jerusalem temple language remains debated.

In 1 Kgs 6:23–28, two large cherubim stand in the inner sanctuary with their wings extended across the room. In 1 Kgs 8:6–7 priests bring the [[ark-of-the-covenant|ark]] beneath the wings. The layout can be compared with a throne surrounded or supported by winged figures, though the passage does not explicitly call the furniture a throne. Exod 25:18–22 describes smaller gold cherubim attached to the ark's cover and locates Yahweh's speech above the cover between them. [@keel-uehlinger-ggig, 155–170]

Menahem Haran, Othmar Keel and Tryggve Mettinger have developed the throne interpretation, in which the cherubim form the throne and the ark beneath serves as a [[footstool]]. Mettinger understands the vacant seat as a form of [[aniconism]], worship without a figured image of the deity. The reconstruction is influential, but whether there was once a statue in the sanctuary remains debated. The biblical descriptions clearly associate the winged figures with divine presence, while the installation's physical details must largely be inferred. [@keel-symbolism, 168–171] [@mettinger-no-graven-image, 100–103]
