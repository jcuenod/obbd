## Overview

Jericho is an oasis near the Jordan Valley, east of Jerusalem. The ancient mound at Tell es-Sultan preserves remains from a permanent Neolithic settlement and from later Bronze and Iron Age occupations. Nearby Hasmonean and Herodian palace complexes belong to a different phase of settlement in the oasis. Joshua’s account of a fortified city has prompted debate over the dates of the mound’s walls and destruction levels. Jericho’s palm groves, perennial water and roads also explain its prominence in the narratives of Kings and the Gospels.

## Location and Identification

Tell es-Sultan sits beside the spring of ʿAin es-Sultan in the modern West Bank. The spring made sustained settlement and agriculture possible in an otherwise dry landscape. The later palaces at Tulul Abu el-ʿAlayiq lie near the mouth of Wadi Qelt, southwest of the Bronze Age mound. Jericho can designate this broader oasis as well as a settlement of a particular period. ## Archaeological History

Early excavations by Ernst Sellin and Carl Watzinger were followed by those of John Garstang in the 1930s and Kathleen Kenyon from 1952 to 1958. The early Neolithic remains include a substantial stone wall and tower. Later Neolithic deposits yielded plastered human skulls, which suggest elaborate treatment of the dead. Recent Italian-Palestinian fieldwork has investigated the mound’s architecture and periods of occupation. The site was placed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

Garstang associated a destruction with Joshua and dated it to about 1400 BCE. Kenyon distinguished Early Bronze defenses from those of later periods and dated the major destruction of the fortified Middle Bronze city to around the sixteenth century BCE. Radiocarbon measurements on short-lived charred grains support a date earlier than about 1400 BCE. Later Bronze Age activity is attested, but a fortified city with a destruction horizon at the dates often proposed for Joshua remains unestablished. Bryant Wood’s proposal to return the destruction to about 1400 BCE has found limited acceptance. [@neaehl] [@mazar-archaeology]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 2 portrays Jericho as a walled settlement where Rahab shelters Israelite spies, and Joshua 6 gives an account of its fall after a ritual procession. Comparing the narrative with archaeology requires identifying the occupation and walls appropriate to its proposed date. The evidence for a major earlier destruction and traces of later occupation do not establish which, if any, events informed the account. Deut 34:3 associates Jericho with palms, and 2 Kgs 2:4–22 places the healing of a spring there. 1 Kgs 16:34 connects the rebuilding under Hiel with the curse in Josh 6:26. The plain appears again in the account of Zedekiah’s capture in 2 Kgs 25:5. [@abd] [@neaehl]

The palace complex of the Hasmoneans and [[herod-the-great|Herod the Great]] developed nearby. Josephus places Herod’s death at Jericho. Luke 10:30 uses the descent from Jerusalem to Jericho as the setting of a parable. Mark 10:46–52 depicts the healing of Bartimaeus as Jesus leaves Jericho, while Luke 18:35 depicts a healing as Jesus approaches the town. The existence of separate settlement areas has been used to harmonize these accounts, but neither Gospel specifies which site it means. The visit to Zacchaeus in Luke 19:1–10 presents Jericho as a center of activity on the route to Jerusalem. [@neaehl] [@abd]
