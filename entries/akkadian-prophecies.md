## Overview

The Akkadian literary prophecies are a small group of texts from Babylonia and Assyria that describe reigns, calamities, and restorations as events still to come. The surviving tablets come from the first millennium BCE, although some works probably originated earlier. Several compositions turn remembered events into predictions in order to explain the rise or fall of a ruler or the restoration of a sanctuary. They provide a Mesopotamian comparison for Daniel's accounts of one king succeeding another and for Ezekiel's portrayal of a deity leaving and returning to a city.

## Manuscripts and Date

Five compositions are commonly grouped under this heading. Copies of the Marduk and Šulgi Prophecies survive from Assyria, and the Uruk Prophecy survives on a damaged tablet from a fifth-century BCE scholarly collection at Uruk. The Dynastic Prophecy includes events around the Macedonian conquest, and the work called Prophecy Text A has a less certain historical setting. The dates of the copies, the events described, and the original compositions must be considered separately.

## Contents

In the Marduk Prophecy, the god [[marduk|Marduk]] describes his sojourns in Hatti, Assyria, and Elam and foretells a ruler who will restore him to Babylon. The movement of the god refers to the removal and return of his image. The restoring ruler is commonly understood as Nebuchadnezzar I of the late twelfth century BCE. A later Assyrian reader could apply the pattern of loss and restoration to a new setting. The related Šulgi Prophecy places its speech in the mouth of the ancient king Šulgi and anticipates a ruler who will restore sanctuaries and offerings after a period of disorder. [@cos1]

The Uruk Prophecy describes a succession of kings who harm the city or its worship, followed by a ruler who restores its sacred image and a son who continues his line. Many identify the restoring ruler as Nebuchadnezzar II, but other proposed identifications and a reading centered on recurring themes of Uruk's history remain viable. The fragmentary Dynastic Prophecy covers rulers from the Assyrian period through the clash between Darius III and the Macedonians. Its depiction of success for Darius departs from known events and may preserve an expectation voiced while the conflict was still unfolding. Prophecy Text A assigns lengths of reign to successive monarchs, but attempts to match them to known rulers, including kings of the Second Dynasty of Isin, remain disputed. [@anet]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Dan 11:2–45 uses a succession of mostly unnamed kings to review conflicts that scholars correlate with known events, especially the rivalry between the successors of Alexander and the actions of Antiochus IV. The account becomes harder to map onto historical events near the end. This movement from recognizable history to an uncertain future is comparable to features of the Akkadian texts. Dan 8:23–25 likewise gives a portrait of a recognizable ruler in predictive language. The proposed connection between the Dynastic Prophecy and Daniel's [[four-kingdoms-schema|four-kingdom sequence]] (Dan 2:36–45) remains debated. The evidence establishes a comparison in form without fixing how Daniel's writers came to use it.

Ezek 10:18–19 and 11:22–23 depict YHWH's glory leaving the temple and the city. In Ezek 43:1–5 it returns. The Marduk Prophecy treats the absence and return of a deity through the journey of a sacred statue. Both help illustrate the regional idea of [[divine-abandonment|divine abandonment]], while Ezekiel presents the movement of divine glory in a vision.

## Scholarly Issues

The phrase “literary predictive texts,” associated with A. K. Grayson, emphasizes written composition and the language of prediction. William Hallo's term “Akkadian apocalypses” draws attention to similarities with works that reveal history in advance, though it can suggest a closer relationship with Jewish apocalypse than the evidence establishes. These compositions also differ from spoken oracles recorded in the [[neo-assyrian-prophecies|Neo-Assyrian prophetic texts]]. Predicting the past, a technique also called *vaticinium ex eventu*, is found earlier in Egypt's [[prophecy-of-neferti|Prophecy of Neferti]]. Its use in more than one setting leaves a number of possible routes for the development of the biblical form.
