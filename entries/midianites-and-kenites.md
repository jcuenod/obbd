## Overview

The Midianites and Kenites appear in biblical traditions set south and east of Israel and Judah. Moses takes refuge in Midian and marries Zipporah, while Judges connects his wife's family with the Kenites. Elsewhere Midianite traders travel to Egypt, and Midianite forces raid Israelite settlements. Pottery made in northwestern Arabia and found in the copper-producing region of the Arabah illustrates connections across this landscape. Biblical traditions of Moses and of God's coming from the south have led some scholars to propose a southern setting for early worship of Yahweh.

## Sources

Biblical narratives provide most of the explicit notices concerning the two groups, and these narratives give them different roles in different settings. Their cultural background can be explored through excavations in northwestern Arabia and the southern Arabah. A distribution of pottery or a style of metalworking gives evidence for regional contacts. Identifying the groups named in the Bible with particular archaeological remains requires additional evidence.

## History

Midian is generally placed in northwestern Arabia, especially the northern Hejaz east of the Gulf of Aqaba. Qurayyah Painted Ware, known in some earlier publications as Midianite pottery, takes its modern name from a site in that region. It occurs at [[timna|Timna]] and other sites of the southern Arabah. Finds in association with Egyptian material traditionally led scholars to place many examples in the thirteenth and twelfth centuries BCE. Later finds have broadened the discussion of its chronology and distribution. The pottery shows that people and goods moved between northwestern Arabia and the copper-producing districts.

The name Kenite has sometimes been connected with a word for a smith. The proposal, along with the proximity of copper-working districts, has encouraged a picture of Kenites as metalworkers. The linguistic connection and the occupation of any particular Kenite group remain uncertain. The Midianite-Kenite hypothesis proposes that Israelites took up the worship of Yahweh from groups to their south. Scholars point to traditions of Moses's family, poetic depictions of God's approach from the south, and Egyptian references to southern [[shasu|Shasu]] groups. The evidence leaves the route and circumstances of transmission open. See [[yhwh-origins|Origins of Yahweh Worship]].

## In the Bible

Gen 25:1–4 puts Midian among the sons of Abraham and Keturah. In the story of Joseph's sale, Gen 37:25–36 uses both Midianite and Ishmaelite names for traders. Judg 8:24 likewise uses the name Ishmaelites while describing the spoils after the defeat of Midian. These names may overlap in some contexts, and the changing names in the Joseph story are also a feature of its literary composition. The camels in Gideon's story have prompted debate over what the account reflects about the history of [[camels-and-domestication|camel use]] in the southern Levant.

Exod 2:15–22 describes Moses's refuge in Midian and marriage to Zipporah. In Exod 18 Jethro, the priest of Midian, offers sacrifices and advises Moses about judging disputes. Num 10:29–32 associates Hobab the Midianite with knowledge of the wilderness, and Moses asks him to guide the group. Num 25:6–18 gives the Midianite woman Cozbi a central role in the violent crisis at Peor. Judg 6–8 recounts Gideon's defeat of Midianite raiders.

Judg 1:16 places Kenite descendants of Moses's father-in-law among the people settling south of Arad. In Judg 4:11, 17, Heber the Kenite separates from his relatives and has peaceful relations with Jabin. His wife Jael nevertheless aids Israel against Jabin's commander. Saul later spares the Kenites on account of their earlier kindness to Israel (1 Sam 15:6). These stories portray the Kenites as a group capable of maintaining relationships across political boundaries.
