## Overview

Shasu is an Egyptian designation applied in New Kingdom sources to groups associated with mobile life in the southern Levant and neighboring territories. Royal monuments name Shasu lands, and a frontier report describes Shasu from Edom bringing people and animals into Egypt. One place or group name written with consonants resembling YHWH has been linked to the origins of Israel’s God. The connection is debated. Egyptian usage and the scattered settings in which the term appears give no basis for treating all Shasu as a single people or equating them with early Israel.

## Sources

Egyptian military and geographical texts name Shasu in descriptions of the lands beyond the eastern frontier. The lists at [[soleb-shasu-inscription|Soleb]] and Amarah West include a name often read with the consonants *yhw*. A related listing is sometimes connected with Seir. The lists register places or groups known to Egyptian scribes. The form with *yhw* is not explicitly identified as a god, and the exact location remains uncertain.

[[papyrus-anastasi|Papyrus Anastasi VI]] preserves a frontier report stating that Shasu groups from Edom were allowed through a checkpoint at Tjeku to pasture or water associated with Pithom. It provides evidence for a particular movement and for Egyptian officials’ role in managing it. The document gives a different picture from royal scenes in which defeated people represent the reach of Egyptian power.

## History

The term is usually applied to mobile or partly settled pastoral groups across parts of the southern Levant, Sinai, and Transjordan. The geographical range makes a single tribal identity unlikely. Mobility does not mean that every family remained outside permanent settlements or provincial administration. Frontier documents show that movements could be negotiated when water and grazing were needed.

Some scholars have interpreted figures in reliefs associated with Merneptah’s campaigns as Shasu and proposed that they represent the Israel named on the [[merneptah-stele|Merneptah Stele]]. Others identify relevant figures as inhabitants of Canaanite cities or use other criteria to connect the two sources. The reliefs do not label the disputed figures as Israel. The stele independently attests a group named Israel, not a secure identification of the figures or a correspondence between Israel and all Shasu.

## In the Bible

Deut 33:2, Judg 5:4–5, and Hab 3:3 locate divine manifestations in relation to southern places such as Seir, Sinai, Paran, and Teman. Scholars compare these traditions with the Egyptian *yhw* reference in proposing a southern setting for the early worship of [[yhwh-origins|YHWH]]. Neither the consonantal resemblance nor the poetic geography proves the original location of that worship. They remain separate kinds of evidence that can be assessed together.

In Gen 47:4–6 Jacob’s household asks to reside in Egypt because conditions in Canaan leave it without pasture. The frontier report offers a concrete example of officials admitting people with animals for related practical reasons. The comparison concerns a pattern of movement and provisioning. The report names no people in the Genesis story.
