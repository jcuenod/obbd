## Overview

Dor was a Mediterranean port on the Carmel coast, south of modern Haifa. It was occupied by the Middle Bronze Age and remained important through the Roman period. An Egyptian narrative names its early Iron Age ruler, a Phoenician inscription records its transfer to Sidon under Persian rule, and excavations document trade and changing coastal industries. In the Bible, Dor appears in accounts of tribal territory, Solomon's administration, and a Hellenistic siege. Its history shows why a coastal town could remain closely tied to maritime trade while passing between inland kingdoms and empires.

## Location and Identification

Ancient Dor is securely identified with Tel Dor, also known as Khirbet el-Burj. The tell rises beside natural anchorages on the Carmel coast. The Hebrew forms rendered Naphath-dor or Naphoth-dor probably refer to a district associated with the city. Translations such as “heights of Dor” may be appropriate, although the geographical extent of the name remains uncertain [@neaehl].

## Archaeological History

Ephraim Stern directed major excavations beginning in 1980, and Ilan Sharon and Ayelet Gilboa led subsequent work. The early Iron Age [[report-of-wenamun|Report of Wenamun]], set around the beginning of the eleventh century BCE, describes a town of the Tjeker or Sikil ruled by Beder. Stern interpreted features of the excavated settlement in terms of a distinctive [[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]] presence. Gilboa and Sharon emphasize continuity in local Canaanite material culture alongside maritime contacts and a more varied population. Both the Egyptian designation and the excavated pottery matter to the debate, although they describe different kinds of evidence [@ael] [@neaehl].

In the late eighth century BCE, after the western campaigns of [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]], Dor became an Assyrian provincial center. The Phoenician [[eshmunazar-sarcophagus|inscription of Eshmunazar II]] later records the grant of Dor and Joppa to Sidon by a king usually understood as Persian. In the Hellenistic and Roman periods, Dora continued as a port. Excavators have identified an installation for [[purple-dye|purple dye]] in a Hellenistic level, and waterfront studies have documented structures associated with anchorage and work ashore. The bays have yielded both early anchors and wrecks from later periods, evidence for maritime activity spread across many centuries [@kai] [@neaehl].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Josh 11:2 places the district of Dor among the territories of Jabin's northern coalition, and Josh 12:23 lists a defeated king of Dor. Josh 17:11–12 and Judg 1:27 remember Dor in relation to Manasseh but report that its inhabitants remained. The town and its dependencies appear again in the Manassite list in 1 Chr 7:29. The notices associate Dor with the reach of Israel's inland communities and with a persistent population of its own.

In 1 Kgs 4:11, Ben-abinadab, husband of Solomon's daughter Taphath, administers the district. A tenth-century BCE date for Israelite control follows from reading the list as a contemporary record of Solomon's administration. Other reconstructions place political incorporation later, in the time of the ninth-century Omrides. The finds establish Dor's regional importance, and the date when it came under Israelite rule remains debated.

In 1 Macc 15:11–14, 25, the Seleucid king Antiochus VII pursues his rival Tryphon to Dor and besieges him by land and sea. The description makes sense in light of the town's strong coastal position. The shore that sustained the city's trade also gave it a means of access that a besieger needed to close.
