## Overview

The *Res Gestae Divi Augusti* is Augustus's account of his public career, completed by his death in 14 CE and displayed at his mausoleum in Rome. Surviving copies from Asia Minor preserve a text that lists honors, military successes, public works, distributions and three censuses of Roman citizens. It presents peace as a result of Augustus's victories and benefactions. The work supplies context for New Testament references to the emperor and to peace, while its census figures belong to a different administrative setting from Luke's account of Jesus's birth.

## Manuscripts and Date

Augustus directed that the text be displayed on bronze at his mausoleum in Rome. The bronze display is lost, but an almost complete copy survives at the temple of Roma and Augustus in Ancyra, modern Ankara. It presents both Latin and Greek versions. Fragments of the Greek text from Apollonia and of the Latin text from Antioch in Pisidia supplement the Ancyra copy. The provincial copies show that readers far from Rome could encounter the emperor's account in a civic and cultic setting.

## Contents

Augustus speaks in the first person. He presents his early military intervention as a defense of the republic, catalogs powers and honors granted to him, records donations and foundations, and celebrates the extension of Roman power. Chapter 8 gives totals for censuses of Roman citizens in 28 BCE, 8 BCE and 14 CE. Chapter 13 claims that the Senate ordered the closing of the gates of Janus three times during his leadership, a sign that Rome regarded peace as secured by victory. The designation *divi filius*, “son of the deified one,” in Augustan titulature referred to Julius Caesar's deification and Augustus's adoptive descent from him.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Luke 2:1 sets the birth narrative in the reign of Augustus and presents the emperor as authorizing an enrollment. The censuses in chapter 8 counted Roman citizens. An enrollment of inhabitants for provincial taxation, such as that associated with [[census-of-quirinius|Quirinius]], belonged to a different administrative category. The figures in the *Res Gestae* do not document the registration in Luke. [@abd, 1:883–886]

The proclamation of peace in Luke 2:14 had a public vocabulary familiar to subjects of Rome, who could encounter assurances of peace in imperial texts and honors. The language of peace and safety in 1 Thess 5:3 may also evoke public claims of security. Its short and conventional phrasing leaves direct allusion uncertain. [[imperial-cult|Imperial honors]] and titles add a further context for early Christian language about divine sonship, which also has roots in Jewish scripture and tradition. Acts 13:14 names Antioch in Pisidia, one of the places where a copy stood, without mentioning the inscription. [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

The first-person account is selective about civil conflict and presents accumulation of authority as service to Rome. In that respect it admits comparison with earlier works of [[royal-annals-and-historiography|royal self-presentation]] such as the [[behistun-inscription|Behistun Inscription]] of Darius. The monuments differ in language, political institutions and the claims each ruler makes about legitimate authority. Comparison identifies recurrent strategies in inscribed accounts of rule without requiring a direct literary relationship. [@tad, C2.1]
