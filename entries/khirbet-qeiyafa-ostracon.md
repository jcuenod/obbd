## Overview

The Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon is a pottery sherd bearing five lines of ink writing from a fortified settlement overlooking the Elah Valley. It was excavated in 2008 from an Iron Age context around 1000 BCE. The script belongs to an early stage of the alphabet, but the surviving signs are too uncertain to yield a generally accepted reading. The ostracon documents early writing at the site and is often discussed in debates about the beginnings of Hebrew literacy and administration.

## Description and Provenance

The sherd was recovered during excavations led by Yosef Garfinkel and Saar Ganor at [[khirbet-qeiyafa|Khirbet Qeiyafa]]. Haggai Misgav, Garfinkel and Ganor published the first edition in 2009. The writing was applied in ink to the surface of the pottery. Its strokes are faint, and some visible marks may reflect damage instead of writing. Radiocarbon dates and pottery from the fortified occupation together place the find broadly near the transition from the eleventh to the tenth century BCE. The exact chronology of the occupation remains contested.

## Contents

The signs are written in an early alphabetic script often termed Proto-Canaanite. The editors and later readers differ on individual letters, word division and the direction of writing. Proposed words include forms related to serving or slavery and judging, as well as possible references to a widow and a king. The uncertainty grows when these readings are joined into sentences. Gershon Galil proposes an ethical text with injunctions to protect vulnerable people. Émile Puech reads a text concerning the establishment of kingship. Other proposals include a list of names, practice writing and an administrative message. These reconstructions cannot all describe the same text.

A separate jar inscription found at Qeiyafa names a man generally read as Ishbaal son of Beda. It shares the personal name Eshbaal in 1 Chr 8:33 without naming the same father or otherwise establishing that it refers to Saul’s son. The jar was inscribed by a different technique and has its own archaeological and textual context.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

The site overlooks the valley where 1 Sam 17:1–3 places the opposing armies. The sherd has no secure reference to the Goliath story. The writing has also been compared with the episode in Judg 8:14 about a young man listing town officials and with the reference to a royal scribe in 2 Sam 8:17. These are debated comparisons because they depend on assumptions about how widespread writing was and what institutions it served. If an ethical interpretation is accepted, the protections in Exod 22:21–24 (MT 22:20–23) and Isa 1:17 supply possible parallels. The wording required for that comparison remains disputed. [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Some scholars argue for Hebrew, citing proposed vocabulary and forms of a verb meaning to do or make. Others find too few diagnostic features to distinguish Hebrew from closely related Canaanite dialects. The inscription attests an early practice of writing at or connected with the town. Establishing an official scribal system or widespread [[writing-and-literacy|literacy]] would require further evidence. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]
