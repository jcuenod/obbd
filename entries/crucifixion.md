## Overview

Crucifixion was a public form of execution associated with Roman rule, although earlier societies also used forms of suspension and exposure as punishment. In the Roman world it was commonly imposed on enslaved people, rebels, and others of lower legal status. Literary accounts describe its use in Judea, and rare archaeological finds include human remains from a first-century tomb near Jerusalem. The punishment's public shame and the Jewish tradition about a body hung on wood help explain why Jesus' crucifixion became a central problem and theme for the writers of the New Testament.

## Jewish and Greco-Roman Evidence

Ancient Greek and Latin authors describe forms of suspension and exposure under several powers, including Persian, Carthaginian, Hellenistic, and Roman rulers. The words they use can cover different treatments of a living or dead person. Under Rome, crucifixion became closely associated with enslavement and threats to public order. Seneca describes variation in the practice, which makes it difficult to reconstruct any particular execution from a generic description.

[[josephus|Josephus]] reports that the Hasmonean ruler Alexander Jannaeus ordered the crucifixion of about eight hundred opponents (Antiquities 13.380). He also attributes two thousand crucifixions to the Roman governor Varus during unrest after Herod's death (Antiquities 17.295). In his account of Titus's siege of Jerusalem, Roman soldiers crucify captives outside the city (War 5.449–451). These numbers are the historian's reports. Their main evidential value is to show the punishment's place in the suppression of revolt.

Excavation at Givʿat ha-Mivtar near Jerusalem recovered human remains from an [[ossuaries|ossuary]], a chest for the bones of a deceased person, inscribed with the name Yehohanan. A nail still penetrated one heel bone. Joseph Zias and Eliezer Sekeles reexamined the remains and rejected aspects of the initial reconstruction, including the proposed positioning of both feet. A nailed heel has also been reported at Roman-period Fenstanton in England. A lesion found on a heel bone at Gavello in Italy has been proposed as another case, though that interpretation is less certain.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Deut 21:22–23 requires that a person put to death and hung on a tree be buried the same day. The text calls the exposed person accursed and connects removal of the body with avoiding defilement of the land. The plain sequence of the law is execution followed by exposure. A later application of this language to crucifixion could take the resemblance in public display as significant.

## Interpretive Significance

Mark 15:21–37 describes Simon of Cyrene carrying Jesus' cross, an inscription giving the charge, and Jesus' execution between two other condemned men. The passage does not specify the shape of the object Simon carries. John 19:31–33 describes concern that bodies be removed before the Sabbath, while John 20:25 refers to nail marks in Jesus' hands. These details belong to particular narratives and cannot establish a standard Roman procedure.

Acts 5:30 describes Jesus' death in the language of hanging on a tree. Gal 3:13 explicitly uses the language of Deuteronomy to explain the death of Christ in relation to the law's curse. In 1 Cor 1:23 and Phil 2:8 the form of execution bears particular force because it evokes disgrace and humiliation. The Roman punishment and the scriptural language of exposure both contribute to these associations.
