## Overview

The Lachish Letters are Hebrew messages written in ink on pieces of broken pottery and found at Lachish, a fortified city of Judah southwest of Jerusalem. They date to the early sixth century BCE, around the time of the Babylonian conquest of Judah. The letters record practical concerns such as passing on orders, watching for signals, and answering a superior's criticism. They give a local perspective on the last years of Judah described in Kings and Jeremiah and show how people wrote to one another in that setting.

## Description and Provenance

The inscribed pieces of fired clay are called [[ostraca]], meaning potsherds used as writing surfaces. They bear ink writing in the early Hebrew alphabet, and many survive only in fragments. Excavations at Tell ed-Duweir, ancient [[lachish|Lachish]], recovered the main group in 1935 under James Leslie Starkey, with additional finds in 1938. The main group came from a room by the city gate in the destruction level conventionally associated with the Babylonian conquest. The pieces are divided between collections in London and Jerusalem. <!-- VERIFY: exact present repository and display location of the Jerusalem pieces -->

The excavated pieces are numbered Lachish 1–21; a selection is also published as texts 192–199 in the collection of Canaanite and Aramaic inscriptions [@kai]. Letter 1 is a list of names, not correspondence. The designation “letters” describes the best-known pieces rather than every item in the collection.

## Contents

Several letters address Yaʾush as “my lord,” and he is commonly understood to have been a commander at Lachish, although the letters do not state his title. <!-- VERIFY: evidence for Yaʾush's office and location --> In Letter 3, Hoshayahu calls himself Yaʾush's servant and takes issue with a remark about his reading ability. He swears “as YHWH lives” (lines 8–10, own rendering) and protests that he reads the letters that come to him [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions]. The exchange is evidence for the importance of [[writing-and-literacy|reading in official correspondence]], but it does not show how widely people could read in Judah or establish who physically wrote the reply.

The documents preserve common [[letter-writing-conventions|letter formulas]], including an opening wish that YHWH grant the recipient good news. The form *wĕʿattâ* (“and now”) marks the transition from greeting to business. The divine name also appears in shortened form in personal names such as Hoshayahu. These names are called Yahwistic because they incorporate a form of the name YHWH.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Jer 34:6–7 describes Lachish and Azekah as the last fortified cities of Judah still holding out against Babylon. Letter 4 reports that its writers are watching for signals from Lachish and cannot see Azekah [@ahituv-echoes]. The letter is often read to mean that Azekah had already fallen, but it does not say why Azekah was not visible. <!-- VERIFY: interpretation of the last lines of Letter 4 and how closely they can be placed in the sequence of the Babylonian campaign --> The letters belong to the broader setting of the [[fall-of-jerusalem-587|siege and fall of Jerusalem]] under [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] in 2 Kgs 25:1–4, but they do not recount the siege of Jerusalem itself.

The Hebrew word for the signs watched in Letter 4 is related to *maśʾēt* (“signal”), used for the warning signal in Jer 6:1. Letter 6 uses a Hebrew idiom about weakened hands to complain that officials' words discourage people. The same idiom appears in Jer 38:4, where officials accuse Jeremiah of demoralizing the city's soldiers and residents. The Hebrew of the letter carried by Naaman (2 Kgs 5:6) likewise marks the start of its business with *wĕʿattâ*, though the biblical account gives no greeting.

## Scholarly Issues

Letter 3 reports that the army commander Konyahu son of Elnathan set out for Egypt and separately refers to a message from an unnamed prophet. This is evidence for [[prophecy-in-the-ane|prophetic messages]] circulating in official correspondence, not for the identity of the prophet. Jer 26:20–23 describes a different set of roles: Uriah flees to Egypt and Jehoiakim sends Elnathan son of Achbor after him. The proposal that the two Elnathans are the same person is disputed, and neither the identification of the unnamed prophet as Uriah nor the alternative identification as Jeremiah can be established. <!-- VERIFY: early proposals for identifying the prophet and any case for linking the two Elnathans; the inscription gives no patronymic for Elnathan -->
