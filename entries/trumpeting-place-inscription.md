## Overview

The Trumpeting Place Inscription is a fragmentary Hebrew text cut into a stone from the precinct of the Jerusalem temple. Its surviving words direct attention to a place associated with sounding a trumpet. The stone fell or was brought down into the street near the southwestern corner of the platform. The historian Josephus describes a priest sounding a trumpet to mark the beginning and end of the Sabbath. His account provides the strongest context for interpreting the stone and its former position.

## Description and Provenance

Benjamin Mazar's excavations near the foot of the southwestern corner of the [[temple-mount|Temple Mount]] recovered the stone in debris associated with the destruction of the temple precinct in 70 CE. Its shape is consistent with a parapet or corner block. The surviving inscription is on a part of the stone that could mark a place selected for a particular purpose. The Israel Museum holds the original.

## Contents

The surviving Hebrew reads, in substance, “to the place of trumpeting,” followed by the beginning of another expression. The lost letters have been restored in different ways. Proposed completions refer to announcing or distinguishing a particular time. The surviving words securely identify the activity, while its precise wording and full schedule have to be inferred from other evidence.

[[josephus|Josephus]] describes a priest who stood at a high point and sounded a trumpet to announce the beginning and end of the [[sabbath|Sabbath]] (War 4.582). The location of the find and the reference to trumpeting make his account an especially close comparison. A signal from the high platform could be heard in the area around the temple.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Num 10:10 calls for trumpets to be sounded at appointed celebrations and the beginnings of months. Ps 81:3 (MT 81:4) calls for sounding a horn in connection with the lunar calendar and a feast. These passages show the use of instruments to mark sacred time. The inscription and Josephus give a material and literary example from Jerusalem in a later period.

Matt 4:5 places Jesus at a high point of the temple, conventionally called the pinnacle. The southwestern corner is one of several proposed locations. The inscription gives evidence for a signal station and supplies no identification of the spot in Matthew. Matt 6:2 has sometimes been associated with the stone because it refers to sounding a trumpet. The context there concerns publicizing acts of giving, and the proposed association is speculative.

## Scholarly Issues

The final letters matter for a precise reconstruction of the text, but their loss leaves the main reference to trumpeting intact. The original position of the block is reconstructed from its form, the findspot, and Josephus. The same lines of evidence give the best grounds for linking the station with the beginning and end of the Sabbath.
