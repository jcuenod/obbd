## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern scribes recorded rulers and past events in annals, display inscriptions, chronicles, lists and archives. These genres preserved different kinds of information for different audiences. Assyrian royal inscriptions recount campaigns and construction from the king's perspective. Babylonian chronicles often report successions, wars and cultic events more tersely. The Hebrew Bible incorporates regnal data and refers to written records, and several events in Kings also appear in surviving royal inscriptions. The parallels help readers assess how ancient writers selected and interpreted the past.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Assyrian annals commonly arrange events by campaign and present victories as evidence of royal competence and divine favor. Palace slabs and buried foundation objects give the words different audiences and functions. Summary inscriptions can group achievements by region or theme, and public monuments can link conquests to a building dedication or the exercise of legitimate rule. Repeated editions of the [[sennacherib-annals|annals of Sennacherib]] illustrate how the same campaign could be abridged or incorporated into a longer account. [@rinap3-1] [@rima3]

The [[babylonian-chronicles|Babylonian chronicles]] generally have shorter entries, often arranged by regnal year. They record disasters as well as successes and pay attention to such matters as accession, warfare and interruptions to religious observance. The [[assyrian-eponym-list|Assyrian eponym lists]] name officials after whom years were designated. Related chronicles also record occasional events for individual years. King lists track succession and reign lengths, sometimes grouping rulers into dynasties. Archives held letters, orders and records of transactions that could be retrieved for administrative purposes. ## In the Hebrew Bible

Kings refers to a book of Solomon's acts in 1 Kgs 11:41 and to books concerning the kings of Israel and Judah in 1 Kgs 14:19, 29. The notice introducing Omri in 1 Kgs 16:23–24 includes a synchronism with Asa of Judah, the length and initial seat of Omri's reign, and the acquisition of Samaria. This combination resembles information kept by scribes for chronological or political purposes. The recurring formulas also serve the literary organization of Kings. [@abd] [@glassner-chronicles]

Shishak's campaign in 1 Kgs 14:25–26 is generally connected with the campaign commemorated at Shoshenq I's [[bubastite-portal|Bubastite Portal]]. The Karnak list supplies evidence for operations in the region, and its surviving names do not identify Jerusalem. Tiglath-pileser III's inscriptions name Menahem as paying tribute, as does 2 Kgs 15:19–20 under the name Pul. 2 Kgs 18:13–16 and Sennacherib both report tribute from Hezekiah, including thirty talents of gold, with different silver totals and different emphases. [@anet] [@rinap1] [@rinap3-1]

Esth 2:23 and 6:1 make the recording and later reading of a court incident central to the plot. Esth 10:2 refers readers to the annals of Media and Persia. In Ezra 4:15, opponents of Jerusalem request a search of earlier records. Ezra 6:1–2 describes a search that starts in Babylon and yields a memorandum at Ecbatana. These scenes portray ways in which archives might be used to authorize claims and decisions. [@abd] [@cos3]

## Interpretive Significance

Some scholars infer that the authors of Kings used royal records and chronicle material. Others stress how the source formulas establish the narrative's authority and question whether those titles identify precisely the documents used. The survival of administrative records elsewhere makes archival sources plausible. Neither the original collections cited by Kings nor their exact contributions can be recovered. Royal inscriptions and biblical histories make ideological claims through selection and presentation, which comparison can bring into view even where the accounts agree on an event.
