## Overview

Associations in the Greco-Roman world brought people together around an occupation, a cult, a place of origin, or another shared interest. Their surviving rules and monuments record officers, contributions, gatherings, burials, and honors for supporters. These sources help explain the organized activity of artisans in Acts and offer comparisons for the gatherings described in Paul's letters. The groups differed widely, and common practices such as meeting for meals do not establish a common legal or institutional identity.

## Jewish and Greco-Roman Evidence

Some associations imposed detailed rules. The Athenian Iobacchoi regulated entrance, speech, and conduct at their gatherings. A papyrus from Tebtynis shows salt merchants appointing a supervisor and regulating prices, obligations, and a monthly drinking event. The first text concerns a cult group, while the second combines trade arrangements with social activity [@agrw-7] [@agrw-302].

An Ephesian burial inscription charges the silversmiths' association with caring for a grave and assigns it the proceeds of fines. At Thyatira, dyers honor Claudia Ammion, whose civic roles included priestly service and oversight of contests. These records show associations acting collectively beyond the immediate work of making and selling goods. Jewish diaspora groups could likewise honor benefactors, as the [[berenike-judean-benefactor-decree|Berenike decree]] shows [@agrw-161] [@agrw-129] [@agrw-305].

## In the Bible

In Acts 19:23–41 Demetrius assembles silversmiths and other workers whose livelihoods he connects with the cult of Artemis. The [[ephesus-silversmiths-hermeias-inscription|Ephesian inscription]] gives independent evidence for an organized body of silversmiths in the city. It supplies no membership list for the gathering in Acts.

In 1 Cor 6:1–8 Paul addresses disputes taken outside the congregation. In 11:17–34 and 14:26–40 he calls for order in meals and speech when the group assembles. Association rules show that other groups also had to regulate gathering and conduct. The contents and purpose of Paul's instructions reflect the needs of his own assemblies.

## Interpretive Significance

Rev 2:18–29 includes a dispute about food associated with idols. The [[thyatira-dyers-claudia-ammion|Thyatira inscription]] shows that occupational and civic religious ties could intersect in that city. Revelation does not specify that a trade group required attendance at a dinner. A sanctuary invitation such as the [[sarapis-dinner-invitation|Sarapis papyrus]] also illustrates cult dining without identifying an association. Any reconstruction of guild pressure must remain a hypothesis.
