## Overview

Azazel appears in the ancient Israelite Day of Atonement ritual. The Hebrew text assigns one goat to the God of Israel and a second to Azazel, an expression that many scholars understand as the name of a wilderness being. Other readings take it as a description of the goat, its destination, or the removal it accomplishes. In Jewish writings of the Second Temple period, a similar name belongs to a rebellious angel associated with human wrongdoing. The two traditions help explain both the modern term “scapegoat” and the emergence of Azazel as a figure in later religious literature.

## Sources and Iconography

The oldest textual evidence for the figure is the name in Leviticus. In the Book of Watchers, an early part of [[first-enoch|1 Enoch]], an angel called Asael teaches humans metalworking for weapons, as well as adornment and the use of cosmetics. God orders Raphael to bind him and cast him into a dark place in the desert. The story associates Asael with the spread of wrongdoing and assigns sin to him as the earth is restored (1 En 8:1; 10:4–8).

Aramaic fragments of the Book of Watchers from Qumran give the name as Asael. The Temple Scroll and the work conventionally called Ages of Creation, also found at Qumran, attest a spelling of Azazel with two consonants in a different order from their usual order in Leviticus. <!-- VERIFY: Check the exact spellings and their preservation in the critical editions of 4Q180 and 11Q19. --> The Qumran text about the ages associates its Azazel with the rebellious angels. The later Apocalypse of Abraham depicts Azazel as an opponent of Abraham and develops imagery associated with the ritual of the two goats.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Both goats are brought before YHWH, the God of Israel, before Aaron casts [[lots-and-cleromancy|lots]] over them. The Hebrew text uses matching phrases for the lots, one for YHWH and one for Azazel. The goat assigned to YHWH becomes a purification offering, while the second is presented alive and sent into the wilderness (Lev 16:7–10). Aaron confesses Israel's wrongs over the living goat, which carries them away, and the person who releases the goat washes before returning to the camp (Lev 16:21–22, 26).

Jacob Milgrom and other interpreters distinguish the function of the live goat from that of a sacrifice to a deity. In this reading, the wilderness is the destination of what the people need removed. The rite itself gives no instructions to worship Azazel, and the following chapter prohibits sacrifices to goat-like beings (Lev 17:7). See [[day-of-atonement-elimination|Day of Atonement and elimination rites]] for the ritual's wider ancient context.

## Scholarly Issues

Many critical interpreters understand Azazel as a wilderness being because the name stands in parallel with YHWH when the lots are cast. A second approach reads the expression in connection with the goat that is sent away. The ancient Greek and Latin translations helped establish this interpretation, which entered English through William Tyndale's “scapegoat.” A third approach identifies Azazel with a place, often associated with the cliff from which the goat falls in a later Jewish account in the Mishnah (Yoma 6:6). A fourth takes the word to denote complete removal, describing the function of the rite.

Descriptions of desolate places inhabited by strange creatures, such as the portrayal of devastated Edom in Isa 34:14, provide context for the wilderness interpretation. The Hebrew term in that verse can refer to a goat, but some interpreters take it to mean a goat-like spirit. Details of the Enochic story, especially the desert and the assignment of sins, invite comparison with Leviticus. The resemblance still leaves room for debate over how the separate names and traditions came together. Later Christian and occult traditions further developed Azazel as a fallen or demonic figure.
