## Overview

The Dialogue of a Man with His Ba is an Egyptian poem preserved in a Middle Kingdom papyrus dating to the early second millennium BCE. A man in distress debates his *ba*, an aspect of his person that could act apart from him and is often translated as soul. Their speeches ask whether death offers relief, what funeral preparations can achieve, and whether present life can still be enjoyed. Job and Ecclesiastes address some of the same questions, though each work frames them within its own account of suffering and death.

## Manuscripts and Date

Most of the surviving text is in a manuscript known as Papyrus Berlin 3024. Related fragments in the Amherst collection and in Mallorca preserve parts of the same manuscript, including material from the formerly missing opening. The opening remains incomplete. The copy dates to the Middle Kingdom, but the date of the original composition is less secure [@ael] [@anet].

## Contents

The man worries about what might happen if his *ba* leaves him. The *ba* questions the usefulness of elaborate preparations for death and urges him to make use of the good that life still offers. Its advice resembles some of the [[harpers-songs|Egyptian harpers' songs]], in which reflection on death prompts counsel to enjoy present life. The poem gives both speakers room to change their position rather than reducing the exchange to a single lesson.

Part of the man's speech consists of four poems with repeated openings. In Miriam Lichtheim's translation, two are marked by the questions “To whom shall I speak today?” and the observation “Death is before me today” [@ael]. One develops the man's sense that trust and friendship have failed. Another describes death through images of relief and return. The exchange closes with the *ba* urging the man to remain in life for the time being and promising that they will be together when death comes.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Job 3:20–23 asks why life is given to people who long for death. The wording and the social setting differ from the Egyptian dialogue, but both make despair the subject of extended poetic speech. Eccl 4:2–3 judges the dead, and even those who have never lived, more fortunate than the oppressed living. Eccl 9:7–10 also counsels enjoyment of food, companionship, and work in the present. The Egyptian poem raises both the attractions of release and the possibilities of present life through different speakers.

## Scholarly Issues

Interpreters disagree on whether the dialogue portrays a man considering suicide or a broader dispute about the relative value of life and death. The *ba* is an Egyptian concept with roles in both life and the [[afterlife-and-resurrection|afterlife]]. Its relation to the person does not correspond neatly to modern uses of “soul.” Difficult wording and gaps in the papyrus have also led to differences among translations. The comparisons with biblical wisdom concern shared questions and literary forms, with no demonstrated link between the texts.
