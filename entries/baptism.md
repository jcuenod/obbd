## Overview

Baptism is a water rite associated in the New Testament with John the Baptist and the early communities devoted to Jesus. Biblical purification laws and Jewish practices of immersion give background for the rite. John’s baptism combined washing with repentance and preparation for judgment, while early Christian texts connect baptism with Jesus’ name, the Spirit, and belonging to a community. Archaeological baths and texts from Qumran illuminate neighboring practices. They do not establish a direct line from a single Jewish institution to all forms of Christian baptism.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

Jewish purification practices provide the closest background. Stepped pools interpreted as [[stone-vessels-and-mikvaot|ritual baths]] occur in late Second Temple settings, including [[qumran|Qumran]]. The Community Rule connects washing with obedience and recognizes that water does not purify those who persist in wrongdoing (1QS 3:4–9, 5:13–14). Its descriptions show moral conditions attached to repeated purification. They do not establish that John belonged to the group that used the rule or that he learned his practice there.

The Greek words *baptō* and *baptizō* can denote dipping, immersing, or washing. In the Greek of 2 Kgs 5:14 Naaman dips repeatedly in the Jordan. Jdt 12:7 and Sir 34:25 offer other Jewish uses of related vocabulary. Later rabbinic sources explicitly discuss immersion of converts. The existence and form of that practice before 70 CE remain uncertain. A passage in Epictetus about Jewish identity has sometimes been read in that light, but is insufficient to establish a source for John’s rite.

## In the Bible

Lev 15:5–13 makes washing and waiting part of ending certain forms of bodily impurity. In 2 Kgs 5:10–14 Naaman’s washing is performed at the prophet’s direction in the context of healing. Ezek 36:25–27 joins clean water with renewal and the gift of a new spirit. These passages illustrate recurring relations among water, purity, and changed condition. They do not describe one uniform practice or anticipate each feature of later baptism.

In the New Testament, Mark 7:3–4 and Luke 11:38 use washing vocabulary in Jewish settings. Mark 1:4–11 presents John’s baptism as a baptism of repentance for forgiveness and depicts him baptizing Jesus. Luke 3:7–16 connects the rite with changes in conduct and contrasts water with Spirit and fire. John 3:22–4:2 portrays baptism by John and Jesus’ followers alongside a dispute about purification. The descriptions suggest a decisive act administered to participants, alongside other Jewish washings people could undergo repeatedly. They do not specify whether every participant received John’s rite only once.

## Interpretive Significance

Josephus connects John’s washing with bodily purification once a person had already been purified through righteous conduct (*Ant.* 18.116–119). Mark 1:4 connects baptism with repentance and forgiveness. Both attach importance to a changed life, but they describe the relationship of washing and forgiveness differently. The difference is relevant to the purposes and audiences of the two accounts.

Acts 2:38–41 links baptism in Jesus’ name with forgiveness and the Spirit. Acts 19:1–7 distinguishes John’s baptism from baptism in the name of Jesus. Matt 28:19 names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit in its instruction, while Rom 6:3–4 uses burial and rising with Christ to explain baptism. Comparisons with initiations in Greco-Roman mystery cults illustrate that rites could mark a shared identity. Direct derivation of Paul’s account from one of those cults is difficult to demonstrate, and its focus on Christ’s death and resurrection gives it a distinct argument.

The early Christian *Didache* 7 prefers running water and permits pouring if sufficient suitable water is unavailable, showing variation in practice. Accounts of household baptisms in Acts 16:15, 33 do not state the ages of those baptized. They became relevant to later debates without resolving those debates by themselves.
