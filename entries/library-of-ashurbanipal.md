## Overview

The Library of Ashurbanipal is the modern name for collections of cuneiform tablets assembled at Nineveh, especially in the seventh century BCE. Nineteenth-century excavators recovered thousands of tablets and fragments from the city's palaces, and finds elsewhere on the mound also contribute to the surviving corpus. The collections preserve scholarly, ritual, and literary works, including major witnesses to the Epic of Gilgamesh and the Babylonian creation epic. They are a major source for comparing biblical literature with Mesopotamian traditions and for understanding how ancient courts collected and transmitted knowledge.

## Location and Identification

The finds came from Kuyunjik, the main mound of ancient [[nineveh|Nineveh]], across the Tigris from modern Mosul. Austen Henry Layard recovered tablets in the Southwest Palace around 1850, and Hormuzd Rassam found many more in the North Palace in the 1850s. Later excavations reached other locations, including the temple of Nabu. The tablets from different excavations and types of deposits were subsequently grouped in museum collections, and early records sometimes make their original locations difficult to recover.

## Archaeological History

Some tablets carry a colophon, a closing note that names [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]] or otherwise associates the copy with his collection. Ancient lists also record the arrival of tablets and writing boards after Assyria took Babylon in 648 BCE. Material could be obtained as booty, acquired through scholars, or copied for the king. The evidence points to several routes of acquisition and more than one body of tablets. Government records and pieces without ownership notes need individual assessment before they can be assigned to the king's library.

Much of the collection concerns omens, rituals, incantations, lexical lists, and other specialist subjects. Literary compositions are a smaller portion of the material, though several became particularly prominent in modern study. In 1872 George Smith announced his identification of a flood story on the [[gilgamesh-flood-tablet|flood tablet]] of the [[gilgamesh-epic|Epic of Gilgamesh]]. Its publication helped make Mesopotamian and biblical comparisons a public subject and preceded the more specific Babel–Bible controversy of the early twentieth century [@george-gilgamesh].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gilgamesh and [[atrahasis|Atrahasis]] give Mesopotamian parallels to the flood in Gen 6:5–9:17. Copies from Nineveh also preserve [[enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]], often discussed in relation to the creation account in Gen 1:1–2:3. Each composition has its own history, and copies from other sites help establish the texts. The place where a copy was found does not establish where or how a biblical writer might have encountered a related tradition [@george-gilgamesh] [@lambert-millard-atrahasis] [@lambert-bcm].

Prov 25:1 attributes the copying of a collection of proverbs to Hezekiah's men, a distinct setting for comparison with royal support of [[scribes-and-scribal-education|scribal work]]. Isa 47:13 mocks Babylon's astrologers, whose learned practices are illuminated by copies of celestial omen texts, including [[enuma-anu-enlil|Enuma Anu Enlil]]. Ezra 4:10 names Osnappar, generally identified as Ashurbanipal. Nah 2:1–3:19 (MT 2:2–3:19) addresses Nineveh's destruction, which belongs to the historical background for the tablets' preservation. Excavated findspots need not reflect the library's arrangement before the city fell in 612 BCE.
