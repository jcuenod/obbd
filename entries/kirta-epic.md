## Overview

The Kirta Epic is a Late Bronze Age poem from Ugarit about a king whose family has died. The god El instructs him in a dream, and he gains a bride and heirs. A vow to the goddess Athirat and a subsequent illness place his restored household and kingship in danger. The surviving story ends after a son challenges Kirta’s fitness to rule. The poem offers comparisons for biblical accounts of divine dreams, threatened succession and the obligation of rulers to administer justice.

## Manuscripts and Date

Three clay tablets from Ugarit preserve the main text in alphabetic cuneiform, a system representing letters by wedge-shaped signs. Their language is Ugaritic, and the surviving copies belong to the Late Bronze Age. The date of the story’s earlier circulation is harder to establish. Breaks leave gaps in the plot and in the interpretation of individual speeches. [@ktu3, 1.14–1.16] [@parker-unp] [@cos1, 1.102]

## Contents

Kirta grieves the loss of his family, and [[el|El]] appears in a dream with instructions for obtaining a wife. Kirta leads an expedition to Udum and demands the ruler’s daughter Huraya. The siege has a sequence of six days followed by a decisive seventh day. Kirta vows a gift to Athirat, the Ugaritic counterpart of [[asherah|Asherah]], and subsequently gains a family. His illness is linked in the narrative to the neglected vow. El eventually provides a means of healing him. [@parker-unp] [@cos1, 1.102]

Kirta’s son then challenges his father’s performance of royal justice. Among the accusations is neglect of people without ordinary sources of protection. The argument turns the story of a vulnerable household into a question about what a king owes to other households. Kirta curses his son, and the surviving text leaves the final succession unresolved. [@ktu3, 1.14–1.16] [@parker-unp]

## Significance for Biblical Studies

A king’s dream encounter with a deity in 1 Kgs 3:5–15 is a parallel in literary form. The promise of offspring in Gen 15:1–6 and the loss and restoration of a household in Job 1:13–19 and 42:10–17 provide broader comparisons whose particular connections remain debated. The sequence of days also recalls the timing of the siege in Josh 6:3–4. Repeated patterns of seven days were widely available, and dependence on Kirta is not established. [@parker-unp] [@abd]

Kirta’s mobilization involves people whom the rules about marriage in Deut 20:7 and 24:5 would excuse from military obligations. The danger of leaving a vow unpaid also recalls Deut 23:21–23 (MT 23:22–24). The prince’s argument about justice offers comparisons with Absalom’s intervention in 2 Sam 15:1–6 and the royal duties described in Ps 72:1–4, 12–14. [@parker-unp] [@abd]

## Scholarly Issues

Early efforts to find patriarchal places or migrations behind the poem have not provided a reliable historical reconstruction. The work is stronger evidence for [[royal-ideology|royal ideology]] and its tensions. Kirta’s family relationship with El does not release him from illness or mortality. Ps 82:6–7 offers a disputed comparison for the juxtaposition of divine sonship and death, with different figures and questions at issue. The preserved tablets leave it uncertain whether the narrative ended here or another part has been lost. [@parker-unp] [@ddd]
