## Overview

The Uzziah Epitaph is a small stone plaque inscribed in Aramaic. It records that bones identified as those of Uzziah, king of Judah, were brought to a place that should remain closed. Uzziah ruled centuries before the plaque was written. Its script is usually assigned to the late Second Temple period. The inscription gives evidence for how the king and his burial were remembered. The ancient location of the plaque and the identity of the bones remain uncertain.

## Description and Provenance

E. L. Sukenik identified the plaque in a Russian ecclesiastical collection in Jerusalem and published it in 1931. Archival research has traced its entry into that collection to 1887. It is now in the Israel Museum. The antiquarian records provide clues to the circumstances of its discovery, though they do not supply an excavated ancient burial context.

The text is carved in the square script current in the late Second Temple period. Comparisons with other inscriptions place it in the first century BCE or first century CE. The script therefore dates the marker, while the name and title refer to an eighth-century BCE royal figure.

## Contents

The short inscription identifies the bones as those of Uzziah, king of Judah, records their arrival, and prohibits opening the place where they have been deposited. It thus presents itself as a marker for a transfer of remains. Other inscriptions and objects from the period attest practices of gathering and redepositing bones. Its royal identification makes this text unusual among the surviving examples of burial notices.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

2 Kgs 15:7 records that Azariah, another name for Uzziah, was buried with his ancestors in the [[city-of-david|City of David]]. 2 Chr 26:19–23 describes a skin disease, his isolation, and burial in a field belonging to the royal burial ground. The Chronicle places particular emphasis on the effect of his condition on his burial. That distinction may help account for a later tradition about the location or treatment of his remains.

Neh 3:16 treats David's tombs as a landmark in Jerusalem, and Acts 2:29 refers to a tomb of David known to its audience. These passages reflect the continuing place of royal burials in the city's memory. The epitaph supplies a material example of a royal name attached to an account of reburial.

## Scholarly Issues

The traditional interpretation reads the inscription as a notice of reburial. One recent study has proposed that it could be a deliberately staged identification already in antiquity. The exact grounds on which the bones were identified, and whether they were actually Uzziah's, cannot be established from the plaque. The dating of the script and the history of the object's collection are distinct from those questions.
