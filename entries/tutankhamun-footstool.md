## Overview

Among the furnishings of Tutankhamun's tomb are footstools decorated with bound captives and other symbols of Egypt's defeated enemies. The images turn an ordinary part of royal furniture into a declaration of victory. When the king placed his feet on the decorated surface, his position enacted the subjection represented in the design. Comparable images occur on other objects in the tomb. The footstools give a physical example of the language in Ps 110:1, where the king's enemies become a footstool.

## Description and Provenance

The objects come from tomb KV62, discovered by Howard Carter's team in the Valley of the Kings. Carter's inventory records several footstools, including an elaborately decorated wooden piece given the number 030. Photographs and excavation cards preserve the designs and the association of some stools with seats. The examples date to Egypt's Eighteenth Dynasty. They were among the objects selected for the young king's burial.

## Contents

The surfaces bear figures represented with their arms bound, including figures identified through Egyptian artistic conventions as people from lands south or northeast of Egypt. The traditional label “Nine Bows” refers to a conventional group of Egypt's foes. It could be represented by bows or bound captives. The design expresses a claim about all the lands that the ruler could subdue, regardless of which campaigns he actually fought.

Similar motifs occur on Tutankhamun's sandals, walking sticks, and throne furnishings. Placement on or near the places touched by the king turns the relative positions of ruler and captive into part of the image. This was one of several ways Egyptian artists represented royal domination. The [[narmer-palette|Narmer Palette]] provides a much earlier example of closely related imagery.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Ps 110:1 pictures the king seated at God's right hand while God makes his enemies a [[footstool|footstool]]. The Egyptian object shows how such an image could be given material form. In Josh 10:24 the victors place their feet on the necks of defeated kings. In 1 Kgs 5:3 (MT 5:17) Solomon refers to God placing David's enemies beneath his feet. The passages share a regional image of enemies held in submission.

Isa 66:1 calls the earth God's footstool. In 1 Chr 28:2 David associates the ark and the proposed temple with God's footstool. These applications picture divine sovereignty and the earth or sanctuary in relation to it. The footstools from the tomb supply a specific comparison for the use of furniture as a symbol of authority.

## Scholarly Issues

Scholars have compared the throne language of Ps 110 with Egyptian kingship and with Assyrian and Levantine forms of royal imagery. The tomb furnishings show that the gesture was intelligible in an Egyptian setting. The psalm's place in a wider common vocabulary leaves the precise origin of its expression open.
