## Overview

Clothing in ancient Israel and Judah served practical needs and communicated status, occupation and affiliation. Biblical stories and laws describe garments and their uses, while Egyptian paintings and Assyrian reliefs show how people of the southern Levant were portrayed from the Middle Bronze through the Iron Age. Because intact fabrics rarely survive, the images supply details about cut, pattern and ornament. Their conventions and dates matter when using them to interpret a particular garment, such as the one Jacob gives Joseph.

## Archaeological Evidence

Ancient clothing used materials such as wool and linen, and the processes of making them belong to the history of [[textiles-and-weaving|textile production]]. Fibers and cloth survive only under favorable conditions, so depictions fill important gaps. The [[beni-hasan-asiatics|Beni Hasan painting]] shows a group from western Asia in patterned garments at an Egyptian tomb. Its early second-millennium BCE date makes it an example of dress in the wider region, with no secure link to Joseph's story or coat. [@anep, fig. 3] [@king-stager-life, 259–276]

The [[black-obelisk|Black Obelisk]] of Shalmaneser III portrays people associated with Jehu's tribute in long fringed garments and caps. The [[lachish-reliefs|Lachish reliefs]] of Sennacherib depict some Judahite men in short garments and headbands and women in long outer coverings. The scenes differ in date, setting and subject. They show how Assyrian artists drew distinctions among people and situations as well as providing evidence for the garments themselves. [@anep, fig. 355] [@king-stager-life, 259–276]

## In the Bible

In Gen 37:3, 23, Joseph's special garment expresses his father's favor and becomes part of the conflict with his brothers. The Hebrew *kĕtōnet passîm* names a kind of tunic, but its distinguishing feature is debated. The Greek translation of Genesis supports the tradition of a many-colored garment, while other proposals emphasize long sleeves or a garment reaching the wrists and ankles. The Hebrew expression also appears in 2 Sam 13:18, where it is associated with a royal daughter. [@abd, 2:233–235]

A garment's edge could carry personal associations. At Mari, reports could be accompanied by a prophet's hair and garment hem, presumably for checking the claim or its source. In 1 Sam 24:4, cutting the edge of Saul's robe demonstrates David's access to the king, and Ruth 3:9 uses the spreading of a garment edge in a request for protection and marriage. These passages illustrate the symbolic value of a garment's edge without requiring an identical legal or ritual custom in each place. [@nissinen-prophets, 15–18, 88–91] [@king-stager-life, 259–276]

Garments also served as property that could be taken as security. Exod 22:26–27 (MT 22:25–26) requires the return of a pledged garment by sunset because it may be its owner's only covering. The [[mesad-hashavyahu-ostracon|Meṣad Ḥashavyahu ostracon]] preserves a worker's petition for the return of a confiscated garment, though the circumstances differ from a loan pledge. Other texts give special forms to dress, including the tassels in Num 15:38–40 and the [[ephod]] and other priestly garments in Exod 28. [@dobbs-allsopp-hebrew-inscriptions, 357–370]
