## Overview

A kudurru is an inscribed stone monument from ancient Babylonia that records a legal right, such as a royal grant of land, an exemption from taxes, or a property settlement. These monuments were made under the Kassite dynasty and its successors, from the late second millennium into the first millennium BCE. Although traditionally called boundary stones, they were not ordinarily markers set on the edges of fields. Their inscriptions and carvings show how Babylonians called on the gods to protect property rights, a useful comparison for biblical laws and curses about land.

## Archaeological Evidence

The traditional name comes from the Akkadian word *kudurru*, meaning "boundary." The inscriptions often call the stone a *narû*, or monument; Kathryn Slanski uses the term "entitlement narû" to emphasize the right it records. Temple findspots in Babylonia support the view that many stones were deposited in temples rather than placed on the land. A land-grant inscription can identify the ruler and recipient, describe the parcel and its boundaries, and specify exemptions from taxes or services. Many surviving examples came from Susa, in what is now Iran, where they had been brought from Babylonia. The monument recording a grant by the late eighth-century king [[merodach-baladan|Merodach-Baladan]] shows that the practice continued long after the Kassite dynasty ended.

Carved signs make the gods' role visible. A sun disk represents [[shamash|Shamash]], the sun god; a crescent and star can represent the moon god Sin and the goddess Ishtar, and a spade is an emblem of Marduk, Babylon's patron god. The heavenly signs belong here to a set of divine emblems, not a map of the zodiac. Inscriptions call on gods to punish actions such as altering the record or overturning a grant, with penalties that can include disease, loss of heirs, and the erasure of a person's name.

## In the Bible

Deut 19:14 forbids moving a neighbor's [[boundary-markers|boundary marker]], and Deut 27:17 places the person who does so under a curse that the people affirm. Prov 22:28 protects the old marker, while Prov 23:10–11 warns against encroaching on the fields of children without a father, whose defender will take up their case. In Hos 5:10 the same offense becomes an image of wrongdoing by Judah's leaders. These texts concern or evoke markers that identify an actual field boundary; the comparison with the Babylonian monuments is how both traditions put land rights under divine protection.

In 1 Sam 8:14, Samuel warns that a king will take the people's best fields, vineyards, and olive groves and give them to his servants. Babylonian grants illustrate the royal power to confer land on favored recipients, but the warning in Samuel does not describe how any particular Babylonian grant was acquired. The sequence in Deut 28:15–68 is comparable in form to the lists of [[blessings-and-curses|curses]] in royal grants and treaties, where violation brings divine punishment. Deuteronomy addresses Israel as a people bound to obey YHWH, rather than protecting a particular grant from future challengers.
