## Overview

Ancient Near Eastern rulers used marriages between royal houses to build or reinforce political ties. Correspondence from Late Bronze Age Egypt shows rulers bargaining over the bride's identity, rank, and gifts. Biblical texts also associate royal marriages with diplomacy, including Solomon's marriage to a daughter of Pharaoh and Ahab's marriage to Jezebel of Sidon. The practice could offer access to allies and resources, while biblical writers also associated some of these marriages with changes in worship. Individual accounts differ in how securely their historical setting can be established.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]], rulers of Babylonia and Mittani negotiate marriages with the pharaoh. The Babylonian king Kadashman-Enlil reports that Amenhotep III refused to send an Egyptian princess abroad, appealing to long-standing custom. The letters show that expectations of reciprocity did not always apply to marriage. They also show rulers negotiating for gifts and status in connection with marriages [@moran-amarna] [@cos3].

In the thirteenth century BCE, Ramesses II married a daughter of the Hittite king Hattusili III after the two courts had concluded a [[egyptian-hittite-peace-treaty|peace treaty]]. Egyptian monuments commemorate the marriage, and Hittite correspondence illuminates the negotiations. The marriage reinforced a relationship already established by treaty and gave both courts an interest in its continuation [@beckman-hdt].

## In the Hebrew Bible

1 Kgs 3:1 presents [[solomon|Solomon's]] marriage to Pharaoh's daughter as an alliance. 1 Kgs 9:16 associates it with a wedding gift of [[gezer|Gezer]], and 1 Kgs 7:8 and 9:24 mention the residence built for her. The pharaoh and his daughter are unnamed. Some historians consider an exception to the older Egyptian practice plausible in the political circumstances of the later Egyptian Twenty-first Dynasty. Others read the notice as part of a literary portrait of Solomon, in the absence of Egyptian confirmation.

In 2 Sam 3:3 David's wife Maacah is the daughter of the king of Geshur. In 1 Kgs 16:31 Ahab marries [[jezebel|Jezebel]], daughter of the king of the Sidonians. In 2 Kgs 8:18, 26 Athaliah's standing in the house of Omri connects the ruling families of Israel and Judah. These episodes attest how the texts remembered marriages crossing political borders, including the border between the neighboring Israelite kingdoms.

## Interpretive Significance

In 1 Kgs 11:1–8 the marriages become part of a criticism of Solomon because his wives are said to turn his religious allegiance toward other gods. Deut 17:17 also warns a king against multiplying wives. Marriage could be a familiar diplomatic tool while writers evaluated its consequences for loyalty and worship. The point becomes especially clear in Kings, where reports of diplomacy and critical judgments appear in the same narrative.

Dan 11:6, 17 refers to negotiations in which a royal daughter is given in marriage. The usual historical setting is the competition between the Ptolemaic and Seleucid kingdoms in the Hellenistic period. The alliance in each case fails to achieve its stated or inferred political aim. These verses draw attention to the limits of marriage as a way to control the actions of another dynasty.
