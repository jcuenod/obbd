## Overview

The Tablet of Destinies is an object of divine authority in Mesopotamian myth. In *Anzû*, its theft from Enlil suspends the normal exercise of power until the warrior Ninurta recovers it. In *Enūma eliš*, Tiamat assigns the tablet to her champion Qingu, and Marduk takes it after his victory. Both stories draw on the capacity of writing and sealing to give force to decisions. Biblical texts describe God keeping records of the living, the faithful, and the dead. Those images also draw on scribal practice, while assigning heavenly writing a different function.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

In the Akkadian myth *Anzû*, the bird of that name takes the tablet from Enlil and flies away with it. The seizure endangers the ordered exercise of divine power. Possession allows Anzû to counter Ninurta's weapons by commanding their constituent materials to revert to their former states. Ninurta eventually overcomes him and retrieves the object. The plot makes the tablet an instrument of effective rule as well as a sign of rank [@anet, 60–72; @cos1, 1.111].

In *Enūma eliš* tablet I, Tiamat promotes Qingu and fastens the tablet to his chest so that his command has authority. In tablet IV, after vanquishing Tiamat's forces, Marduk takes the tablet from Qingu, seals it, and fastens it to his own breast. This follows Marduk's investiture by the gods and the demonstration of his commanding word. The victory marks the recovery of an object that Qingu received without rightful title according to the poem's account [@lambert-bcm, 50–57, 89–95].

The divine scribe Nabu, associated with writing and Marduk's cult, is described in related traditions as writing or determining destinies. Divine writing could establish a fate, record a decision, or preserve knowledge for later action. Different texts emphasize different operations and need not be referring to the mythical tablet itself [@ddd, 607–609].

## In the Hebrew Bible

Moses asks to be blotted out of God's book in Exod 32:32–33, and God responds that he will blot out those who have sinned against him. Isa 4:3 pictures the survivors in Jerusalem as enrolled for life. Mal 3:16 describes a memorial record for those who fear God, in response to a complaint about the apparent prosperity of wrongdoers. The registers are ways of expressing divine remembrance and judgment.

Daniel depicts books opened before a heavenly court (Dan 7:10) and names written in a book as a criterion for deliverance (Dan 12:1). Rev 20:12 has books from which the dead are judged according to their deeds, along with a book of life. The distinction between the books within that scene cautions against making every heavenly register perform the same task.

## Interpretive Significance

A written document could make authority visible and preserve its effects beyond the speaking of a command. Mesopotamian stories dramatize who possesses such authority. Biblical lists and court records give prominence to God's ability to remember, distinguish, and judge persons. Different uses of a shared scribal medium explain why the comparison can illuminate both bodies of literature without identifying the biblical heavenly books with the tablet.
