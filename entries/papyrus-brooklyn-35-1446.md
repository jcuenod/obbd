## Overview

Papyrus Brooklyn 35.1446 is a late Middle Kingdom Egyptian administrative document. One section records cases involving flight or absence from labor obligations. Another lists people of Egyptian and foreign origin associated with a household. The two sections give a view of how Egyptian administrators recorded obligations and how elite households kept track of dependent personnel. They offer background for the household and labor settings of Genesis and Exodus, with social categories that need to be understood in their Egyptian context.

## Description and Provenance

The document is written in hieratic, the cursive form of Egyptian writing used in administration. The surviving papyrus is held in the Brooklyn Museum. Its two written faces preserve records with different purposes and should be read separately. The numerical designation is the museum's accession number [@hayes-brooklyn-papyrus].

## Contents

The administrative material refers to a *ḫnrt wr*, an institution traditionally translated “Great Prison.” Its personnel dealt with compulsory labor and with people who had absented themselves. The translation “prison” can obscure that administrative role. The household list on the other face has a distinct purpose. It identifies individuals, including people designated as Asiatic, a broad Egyptian category for people associated with lands to Egypt's northeast [@loprieno-servitude, 3, 6–8].

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Gen 39:1–6 portrays Joseph, brought from abroad and sold in Egypt, rising to a position of responsibility in Potiphar's household. The papyrus supplies independent evidence for households with foreign dependents. It gives no basis for identifying an individual in its lists as Joseph or dating the narrative from the document. The article on [[joseph-story-egyptian-background|the Joseph story and Egypt]] discusses other evidence for that setting.

Exod 1:11–14 describes a different labor setting, with officials imposing building and other work on the Israelites. The papyrus shows that Egyptian authorities could track labor obligations and absences. The household register and the labor cases concern different forms of dependence. For Ramesside brick-production accounts, see the [[louvre-leather-roll]].

## Scholarly Issues

The people listed in the papyrus are described in several ways. The English terms “slave” and “servant” can suggest a uniform legal position where the text records distinct obligations or relationships. The relevant terms, transactions, and administrative setting need to be considered together. Comparisons with [[slavery|slavery and debt servitude]] and [[corvee-labor|compulsory public labor]] therefore depend on which part of the papyrus is in view.
