## Overview

The Amalekites are remembered in the Hebrew Bible as a people who lived in and around the dry country south of Judah, including the approaches to Sinai. They appear in stories about Israel's journey from Egypt and the early monarchy, where they become a recurring enemy. No known ancient Near Eastern inscription independently names them, and no archaeological site or assemblage can be securely assigned to them. Their place in biblical memory is much clearer than their history or political organization.

## Sources

The main evidence is biblical. Gen 36:12, 16 places Amalek among the descendants of Esau, ancestor of the [[edomites|Edomites]], while Gen 14:7 refers to the territory of the Amalekites in a story set in Abraham's time. The genealogy describes a kinship and the place name helps identify a region for the reader. Neither passage supplies an independently dated record of the group's origin. [@abd]

## History

The stories associate Amalek with the southern borderlands, but individual episodes allow for movement beyond that setting. In Judg 6:3, Amalekites join Midian and other eastern groups in attacking Israel after sowing. First Samuel remembers Amalekites both as subjects of a king, Agag, and as raiders who reach Ziklag and take captives (1 Sam 15:7–9 and 30:1–3). The accounts give little basis for determining how far any one Amalekite leader's authority extended. Archaeological evidence for life in the southern drylands gives a regional context, while proposals assigning particular remains to Amalek are speculative. [@abd] [@mazar-archaeology]

## In the Bible

In Exod 17:8–16, Amalek fights Israel at Rephidim. Deut 25:17–19 supplies a reason to remember the attack. It says Amalek struck those who lagged at the rear when Israel was weary, and it commands Israel to blot out Amalek's memory after gaining rest from its enemies. The juxtaposition of remembering and blotting out shapes the passage's later reception.

First Samuel 15 connects the earlier attack with the command to Saul to destroy people and livestock (1 Sam 15:2–3). The command involves [[herem|devoting to destruction]], a practice that ancient accounts could frame as an obligation to a deity. Samuel rejects Saul as king after Saul spares Agag and the best animals, which Saul says were kept for sacrifice (1 Sam 15:8–9, 20–23). The story presents obedience as the immediate issue while depicting a command that includes killing children. The literary convention of [[conquest-accounts-and-war-rhetoric|total destruction]] in ancient war accounts helps explain the language, and the fate of civilians remains central to the ethical questions raised by the passage.

Esth 3:1 calls Haman an Agagite. The title can be read as a literary link with Agag, recasting the danger posed by Haman in terms of Israel's remembered enemy. In Jewish reception, the Amalek passage became part of the readings associated with Purim, the festival celebrating deliverance in Esther. Interpretations have also restricted the command to an identifiable ancient people or read Amalek as a symbol of evil.
