## Overview

The Gallio Inscription preserves parts of a Greek letter from the Roman emperor Claudius to Delphi. It names Lucius Junius Gallio and gives his title as proconsul of Achaia. Claudius's official titles date the letter to around 52 CE. Acts places Paul's appearance before Gallio at Corinth during his term as proconsul, so the two sources together supply a date around which other events in Paul's life can be arranged.

## Description and Provenance

The inscription survives on nine stone fragments recovered at Delphi. Editors piece together the surviving words by positioning fragments and restoring gaps with the aid of Greek grammar and the formulas used in imperial letters. Inscriptions commonly introduced emperors with a list of titles and honors. Here the number of Claudius's acclamations, public acknowledgments of him as victorious commander, provides the narrowest clue to the letter's date.

## Contents

The letter concerns Delphi and its shortage of citizens. Claudius expresses goodwill toward the city and refers to the cult of Apollo. After mentioning a report from Gallio, the emperor authorizes measures to increase the city's citizen body. Crucial portions of the lines naming Gallio and describing the decree survive alongside gaps that editors restore. Gallio's name, the emperor's designation of him as a friend, and his office survive well enough to identify the official and his jurisdiction.

Gallio was a brother of the Roman writer Seneca. Seneca recalls that Gallio sailed away from Achaia when he developed a fever (Ep. 104.1). The account suggests his stay was cut short, although it gives no calendar date for his departure.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

In Acts, opponents of Paul bring him before Gallio at the tribunal in [[corinth|Corinth]], where the proconsul dismisses their charge as a dispute about their own law (Acts 18:12–17). The Delphi letter attests the name and office of the governor in this scene independently of Acts. Gallio's term is usually placed in 51–52 CE, putting the hearing within that interval on the usual reconstruction.

Acts 18:1–11 connects Paul's time in Corinth with Aquila and Priscilla, who have come from Italy after Claudius ordered Jews to leave Rome, and describes Paul teaching in Corinth for eighteen months. Suetonius also reports a Claudian expulsion (Claud. 25.4). The frequently proposed date of 49 CE for this event is debated. The inscription gives historians a comparatively firm point from which to examine the sequence of events reported in Acts.

## Scholarly Issues

Claudius's twenty-sixth acclamation belongs around 52 CE. Dated evidence for his next acclamation places an upper limit on the period in which he would be styled as acclaimed for the twenty-sixth time. Working out when Gallio began and ended his term requires additional assumptions about provincial appointments and how promptly the letter followed his report. Some reconstructions favor a term in 52–53 CE. Both proposed terms place the meeting reported in Acts in the early 50s CE.
