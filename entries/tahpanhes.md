## Overview

Tahpanhes was a town on Egypt's northeastern frontier, generally identified with Tell Defenneh, the site of Greek Daphnae. Excavations have uncovered remains of a substantial enclosure and material from the Saite period, when Egypt controlled routes from Asia into the eastern Nile Delta. Greek sources describe a garrison in this area. In Jeremiah, Judean refugees come to Tahpanhes after Gedaliah's murder, and Jeremiah performs a sign outside a royal building. The archaeological remains establish a setting for travel, trade, and administration at the frontier and have prompted debate about the location of the sign.

## Location and Identification

Tell Defenneh is in the eastern Delta lowlands west of the modern Suez Canal. The identification rests on the continuity of names and the fit of the site with literary descriptions of Daphnae near Egypt's route to the Levant. The biblical spelling varies in ancient witnesses and transliteration. Jer 46:14 groups Tahpanhes with Migdol and Memphis, and Ezek 30:18 includes it in a survey of Egyptian centers. The references suit a settlement of regional importance, even when they do not describe specific topographic features [@abd, 6:308–310].

## Archaeological History

Flinders Petrie excavated the site in 1886. He reported large mudbrick constructions, a substantial platform, and Egyptian and Greek finds. The Greek pottery provides evidence for contact across the eastern Mediterranean. Its presence supports a picture of a mixed frontier community without identifying the roles of all those who used the vessels. Herodotus describes garrisons at Pelusian Daphnae and other frontier places under Psammetichus I, and he associates Ionian and Carian soldiers with installations in the Delta. His account was written after the Saite period. The archaeology places activity of the appropriate period at the site and leaves questions about how the enclosure and its buildings were used [@abd, 6:308–310].

Petrie proposed a connection between a large brick platform at the site and Jeremiah's account. His report supplies the location and material evidence for the platform. Identifying its original purpose and tying it to a particular biblical event require further evidence.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Jer 43:7–9 presents Tahpanhes as a destination for people escaping the aftermath of Gedaliah's murder. Jeremiah is told to conceal large stones in the brickwork or pavement near the entrance to Pharaoh's house. The narrative identifies a royal or administrative setting through the activity there. It does not give dimensions or enough detail to identify a specific excavated structure. The threat announced in Jer 43:10–13 places Nebuchadnezzar's throne above the stones and extends the judgment to Egyptian temples.

Jer 44:1 names Tahpanhes among places where Judeans lived in Egypt, placing it alongside other locations in the Delta and Upper Egypt. Earlier and later prophetic references use the name to represent Egyptian power or its vulnerability (Jer 2:16; 46:14; Ezek 30:18). The historically attested frontier position helps explain why the town could serve as a point of entry for refugees and a symbol of the reach of foreign armies.
