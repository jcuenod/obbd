## Overview

The Maccabean Revolt began in Judea in 167 BCE in response to measures imposed by the Seleucid king Antiochus IV that suppressed Jewish religious practices and disrupted worship at the Jerusalem temple. The priest Mattathias led the early resistance, and his son Judas Maccabeus led the forces that rededicated the temple in 164 BCE. Fighting continued, and Judas died in battle in 160 BCE, before his family established independent Hasmonean rule. The crisis provides the historical setting for visions in Daniel and explains the origin of Hanukkah, the festival mentioned in John's Gospel.

## Sources

First Maccabees presents the revolt as the beginning of the Hasmonean dynasty, the ruling family descended from Mattathias. It makes the family's leadership central to the defense of Jewish law and the restoration of worship. Second Maccabees, by contrast, identifies its main narrative as an abridgment of a five-book work by Jason of Cyrene (2 Macc 2:19–32). It gives more attention to the temple, divine intervention, and those who died rather than break the law. The [[heliodorus-stele|Heliodorus Stele]] provides independent evidence for Seleucid oversight of sanctuaries before the revolt, not for the outbreak itself.

Daniel presents the persecution in visions rather than as a campaign history. In *Jewish Antiquities* 12–13, [[josephus|Josephus]] retells the revolt and follows the family's rise beyond Judas's lifetime. His account of the revolt draws heavily on 1 Maccabees, so agreement between the two does not necessarily give independent confirmation. [@abd, 4:433–439]

## Reconstruction

First Maccabees describes royal orders that interfered with temple sacrifice, circumcision, and observance of the [[sabbath|Sabbath]] (1 Macc 1:41–64). It places the beginning of Mattathias's resistance at Modein, where he refused to offer the sacrifice demanded by royal officials (1 Macc 2:1–70). After refugees were killed without fighting back on the Sabbath, Mattathias and his companions resolved to defend themselves if attacked on that day (1 Macc 2:29–41). The decision concerns defense against attack, not a general suspension of Sabbath observance.

The Hasideans, a group whose name means pious ones, joined Mattathias's forces in 1 Macc 2:42. Later, 1 Macc 7:12–18 presents them among those who sought peace with Alcimus, the high priest. These episodes do not establish whether the Hasideans had a lasting organization or how they might relate to later [[jewish-sects|Jewish groups]]. Judas was ultimately killed fighting the Seleucid commander Bacchides (1 Macc 9:1–22); the temple's recovery had not ended the war.

Both books place the rededication on the twenty-fifth day of Kislev, but they narrate the death of [[antiochus-iv|Antiochus IV]] and the dedication in opposite orders (1 Macc 4:36–59; 6:1–17; 2 Macc 9:1–29; 10:1–8). Converting dates expressed in the Seleucid era requires attention to calendars with different starting months and is distinct from deciding whether the authors arranged their accounts in strict chronological order. The Jerusalem stronghold known as the Akra poses a different problem: 1 Macc 1:33–36 associates it with the City of David, but its precise site remains debated. In 2015 excavators proposed identifying fortifications at the Givati parking lot with the Akra; critics have questioned whether that location fits the ancient descriptions.

## Biblical Accounts

Daniel describes the interruption of regular sacrifice and anticipates the sanctuary's restoration after a period counted in 2,300 evenings and mornings (Dan 8:13–14). It also depicts faithful resistance and wise teachers who suffer and receive limited aid (Dan 11:31–35). The latter passage has been read as an allusion to Maccabean resistance, but it does not name Judas or his followers.

First Maccabees describes the replacement of the altar, the resumption of sacrifices, and the institution of an annual eight-day dedication festival (1 Macc 4:36–59). Second Maccabees likens the celebration to Sukkot, the Festival of Booths, which the participants had been unable to keep in the usual way (2 Macc 10:1–8). John 10:22–23 places Jesus at the Jerusalem temple during this winter Festival of Dedication, now called Hanukkah.

In 2 Macc 7:1–42, seven brothers face death rather than submit to the king's demand to eat pork, and their mother encourages them and dies after them. The brothers' hope that God will restore their lives gives a concrete example of Jewish belief in [[afterlife-and-resurrection|bodily resurrection]]. Heb 11:35–38 recalls unnamed sufferers, including people who refuse release in hope of resurrection. The parallel makes the Maccabean martyrs a useful comparison without establishing that Hebrews used 2 Maccabees directly.
