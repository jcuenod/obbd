## Overview

Ancient flood stories describe a catastrophe that leaves a few survivors to begin life again. The closest comparisons to Genesis come from Mesopotamia, where texts in Sumerian and Akkadian tell of a divinely warned survivor who builds a vessel. The story appears in several forms and serves different purposes in epic and historical tradition. Genesis shares distinctive details with these accounts, including a mountain landing, the release of birds, and a sacrifice. It also gives its own explanation for the disaster and concludes with a divine promise. The comparison illuminates both the movement of a story and the variety of meanings attached to it.

## Ancient Near Eastern Evidence

The fragmentary Sumerian flood story often called the [[eridu-genesis|Eridu Genesis]] connects the creation of living beings, the founding of cities, and a flood survived by Ziusudra. Afterward, the gods grant him a form of divine life. Other Sumerian sources use the flood to divide an age of early kings from a later one. The fragments preserve only part of the story, and gaps limit reconstruction [@cos1, 1.130, 1.158].

The Old Babylonian [[atrahasis|Atrahasis]] epic ties the flood to an account of how humans were made. Humans take over work once done by lesser gods. As people multiply, their noise disturbs the god Enlil. After earlier attempts to reduce the disturbance, the gods send a flood. The god Ea warns Atrahasis, who survives in a boat. The destruction leaves the gods without their usual offerings, and the story ends by instituting limits that preserve human life under new conditions [@lambert-millard-atrahasis, 42–67, 88–103].

In the Standard Babylonian [[gilgamesh-epic|Epic of Gilgamesh]], the survivor Utnapishtim recounts the flood to Gilgamesh. He receives instructions for a boat, brings living things aboard, and later grounds on a mountain. He sends out birds in succession to discover whether the waters have receded. After his offering, the gods gather at its smell. The account is part of a larger poem about mortality, and Utnapishtim's exceptional fate is important to the point of his story [@george-gilgamesh, 1:508–28, 700–725].

Berossus, a Babylonian priest writing in Greek in the early Hellenistic period, knew a related survivor called Xisuthros. Greek stories about Deucalion and Pyrrha also describe survivors of a flood and the renewal of humanity. Individual details differ, and apparent similarities do not establish how the Greek traditions arose. The surviving Mesopotamian materials offer closer points of comparison with the details of Genesis than the Deucalion stories do.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Gen 6:5–9:17 tells of a flood sent after human wickedness and violence fill the earth. God warns Noah, who builds a vessel according to specified dimensions. The opening of the fountains of the deep and the windows of heaven brings waters into the inhabited world. In Gen 8:4, the vessel comes to rest on the mountains of Ararat, a regional name. The release of a raven and doves later in the chapter has a close parallel in Gilgamesh, although the sequence and choice of birds differ (Gen 8:6–12).

The sacrifice in Gen 8:20–21 is another close parallel. Both narratives describe the deity or gods smelling an offering. Genesis follows the offering with a divine commitment to preserve the rhythms of life, and the rainbow serves as the sign of a pledge to the earth and its inhabitants (Gen 9:8–17). Some readers compare the sign with the goddess's necklace in Gilgamesh, which recalls the flood. The literary relationship is debated because the objects have different functions and neither exact wording nor an equivalent covenant links them.

Many critical scholars understand the Genesis narrative as combining a Priestly account with another account that uses different vocabulary, chronology, and detail. Proposed divisions vary. The lines of comparison also cross these divisions. The larger sequence of origins and flood in Atrahasis and the particular details in Gilgamesh both help describe the relationships, without requiring a single surviving tablet to supply the entire biblical story.

## Interpretive Significance

Akkadian flood material found at Ugarit and a fragment of Gilgamesh from Megiddo show that people were reading Mesopotamian literature in the Late Bronze Age Levant. The Megiddo fragment attests the epic rather than the flood episode specifically. The finds make the movement of such stories credible long before the collection of the biblical texts. They leave open the dates and routes by which particular traditions reached the writers of Genesis.

Leonard Woolley found a substantial layer of flood-deposited material at Ur. Other Mesopotamian sites show floods in different periods. Such layers attest repeated local inundation in river environments. They cannot be combined into evidence of a single worldwide event or confidently tied to the development of one surviving story.

Greek and Mesopotamian versions highlight different reasons for survival and for renewed life. Genesis makes violence and corruption central to the judgment and gives the post-flood promise a continuing place in the world. Later biblical texts emphasize other parts of the story. Isa 54:9 invokes the waters of Noah in the language of an oath, and Matt 24:37–39 uses the flood to depict judgment arriving unexpectedly.
